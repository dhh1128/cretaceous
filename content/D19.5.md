The Barrier came in on the vent at her side, musk and rot, so thick after two hours of hot metal that Teva's eyes ran. She had smelled it every night of her life, faint, on whatever air came over the tangle. Every animal on this side of the wall was meant to smell it and turn away, and she was on this side.

She sat with her head against the seat and breathed it in, because breathing was what she had left. The heat had got into her and had nowhere to go. Her face burned and her lips had split, and each time she looked the tangle filled more of the windscreen.

"Ben," she said. "Is there enough?"

Benal leaned between the seats with his eyes on the panel. "The reserve's full. Ten seconds of the lift units, roughly. By my sum that's the wall, and nothing after it."

"And if it's short?"

"Then we come down outside." He tipped his head at the brown water sliding under the nose. "In that."

In the chase the voice had told Keo that a craft left on the flood would sit on mud in days. Outside the wall they would be on foot, knee-deep in floodwater, with fifteen meters of tangle between them and everyone they had walked out for.

"I'm putting it down on the river," Keo said.

It was the first time he had said it.

"Teva?"

He did not look at her.

She knew both waters. After heavy rain the low groves of the ring stood in the river, and a croc that had come through the grating small and grown up inside went where the water went. She had told four-year-olds at the pool that after rain the groves were the river's, and none of them had needed telling why, because every child knew how Yara died. The channel was the river itself. There was no safe water on the other side of that wall.

The voice had been wrong today about the charge. About the water it had answered Keo with how deep it was and how fast the flood was falling, which were things it could measure.

At the river on the walk out, she had taken the decision out of his hands, and she had been right to.

"The river," she said.

Her hands found the edge of the seat and held it.

Keo pulled the nose up. The slap of the flood under the hull stopped, and the propulsor's note dropped as the speed went out of them and into height.

"Lift units engaging," the voice said.

The scream came down through the roof and up through the floor at once, into her spine and her back teeth. It took the propulsor and the voice and every other sound. Close behind her ear, under it, Benal was shouting.

"One! Two! Three!"

The floor pushed up under her. The tangle rose to meet them, trunks woven into each other, the ceramic nozzles set in the wood with amber running down from each.

"Seven! Eight!"

They were level with the top of it, and that was all the lift units would give.

"Nine!"

The growth went under them and dragged along the belly, a long scrape under her feet that she felt through the seat before she heard it. The hull dipped. It held.

"Ten," Benal said, and the scream stopped.

The propulsor came back, thin. On the panel the charge figure stepped down while she watched, and again, as the reserve filled itself out of what was left. She could read the number, but not the sums Benal was saying behind her, too fast and too low.

At the foot of the tangle on the inside a watcher stood in the trampled ground with his face turned up and his spike half raised against the thing coming over his wall. His whole face had come open. It was too soon for fear on it, or for gladness. Nothing in his life had ever come over the tangle, and this had.

Off to one side the downstream grating stood up out of the brown water, its poles in a row between the pylons.

Then home was under them, all of it at once. The reach ran brown and quick between its gratings, the grazing and the low groves of the ring stood in water and threw the sun back, and beyond them the canopy of the core rose dark. She had lived her whole life inside that shape. Nobody alive had seen it from here.

The wig came down off its height in a long glide. Nobody spoke. Keo swung the nose toward the channel.

She watched him aim at the river and did not stop him. Her hand went to the pocket on the chest of her suit. Through the zip and the dead film she could feel the flower inside, pressed flat, where it had been since the ridge. Keo's eyes were on the water.

He brought the wig onto the line of the channel, heading upstream into the current, and lifted the nose. The hull hit. The slam came up through the seat into her spine, brown water went over the windscreen and ran off clear, and spray blew in at the vent across her face and neck. It was the first cool thing in two hours, and her whole body turned toward it.

"Water contact," the voice said. "Logged."

Then the water was moving under the floor, under her feet, shoving at the hull. Her body knew where it was before she let herself think it, and every part of her wanted to be up and back from the edge.

The hull slowed against the current until it hung almost still, the bank hardly moving beside them, and the propulsor's note went thin and stayed thin. Keo pushed the stick forward. Nothing more came. The bank began to slide the other way.

The current took them back down the reach stern first. Keo fought the stick, and the hull swung broadside and went on down like a trunk in flood and fetched up hard against the grating. The poles boomed on the hull. Upstream the brown water piled against it, and the floor tipped under her, toward the water.

"Lean!" she said. "The poles — lean!"

All three of them threw themselves toward the grating. Benal's weight came against the back of her seat, and Keo's shoulder came hard against hers. On the low side the river stood up brown against the hull, nearly to the edge of the windscreen, and she could not see a hand's depth into it. Nothing came up out of it.

The hull hung there. Then it came back, slowly, the floor nearly level, and the side settled against the poles and stayed.

"It's holding," Keo said.

He reached for the panel and shut the propulsor off. In the quiet the river was loud through the grating, and the Enclave's own noise came in at the vent, insects past counting and frogs in the flooded groves.

"Two," Benal said, reading the figure.

"Ben first," she said.

Keo shoved the door open on the grating side. The poles stood up past the top of the hull, wet and dark, and above them ran the top of the barrier. Benal went out onto the hull on his hands and knees and onto the poles, and climbed hand over hand and did not look down.

Teva reached behind her seat for the plates. She went out after him with them against her chest. The hull was hot under her knees and it moved, grinding on the grating, and a meter below her the river poured past.

"Give them here." Benal lay flat along the top of the barrier with his arm down.

She handed the plates up to him, a few at a time, then the tablet with the fix on it and the gray thing he had dug out of the crown.

Keo came out slowly, one arm held in against his rib. He left blood on the first pole he took.

"Keo. Your foot," she said. "On my shoulder."

"You're done in."

"Climb."

He put his foot on her shoulder. She took his weight and pushed up, and he got his torn hands round the next pole and the one above it, and Benal had him by the wrist and drew him over.

Then it was her. The poles were wet under her palms, and the river roared through them below. Under her heels the hull groaned against the grating, still heeled toward the brown water piling against its side. She went up one pole and the next, and her hands reached the top of the barrier.
