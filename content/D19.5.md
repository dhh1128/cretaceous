The Barrier's musk came in at the flap by her head, musk and rot, so thick after the cabin's hot metal that Teva's eyes ran. She had smelled it every night of her life, faint, on whatever air came over the tangle. It was meant to turn back every animal on this side of the wall, and she was on this side.

She sat with her head against the seat and breathed it in. Breathing was about all she had left. The heat had got into her and had nowhere to go. Her face burned, her lips had split, and each time she looked the tangle filled more of the windscreen.

"Ben," she said. "Is there enough?"

"The reserve's full," Benal said from behind her. "Ten seconds of the lift units, roughly. By my sum that's the wall, and nothing after it."

"And if it's short?"

"Then we come down outside." The flood slid by under the nose. "In that."

Outside the wall they would be on foot, knee-deep in floodwater, with fifteen meters of tangle between them and everyone they had walked out for.

"Once we're over, I'm putting it down on the river," Keo said. "What do you think, Teva?" He did not look at her.

She knew both waters. After heavy rain the low groves of the ring stood in the river, and a croc that had come through the grating small and grown up inside went where the water went. She had taught four-year-olds at the pool that after rain the groves were the river's. Every child knew how Yara died. The channel was the river itself. There was no safe water on the other side of that wall.

The voice had been wrong about the charge, but it could measure the water: a hull left on the flood would sit on mud in days.

At the river on the walk out, she had taken the decision out of his hands, and she had been right to.

"The river," she said.

Her hands found the edge of the seat and held it.

Keo pulled the nose up. The slap of the flood under the hull stopped, and the propulsor's note dropped as the speed went out of them and into height.

"Lift units engaging," the voice said.

The scream came down through the roof and up through the floor at once, into her spine and her back teeth. It swallowed the propulsor and the voice. Behind her ear, under it, Benal was shouting.

"One! Two! Three!"

The floor shoved up under her. The tangle rose to meet them, woven trunks, ceramic nozzles set in the wood with amber running down from each.

"Seven! Eight!"

They were level with the top of it, and that was all the lift units would give.

"Nine!"

The growth along the top dragged at the belly, a long scrape she felt through the seat before she heard it. The hull dipped. It held.

"Ten," Benal said, and the scream stopped.

The propulsor came back, thin. On the panel the number stepped down, and again, as the reserve refilled itself out of what was left. Behind her Benal was saying sums too fast and too low to follow.

At the tangle's foot on the inside a watcher stood in the trampled ground with his face turned up and his spike half raised against the thing coming over his wall. His whole face had come open. It was too soon for fear on it, or for gladness. Nothing made by hands had ever come over the tangle, and this had.

Off to one side the downstream grating stood up out of the flood, its poles in a row between the pylons.

Then home was under them, all of it at once. The reach ran brown and quick between its gratings, the grazing and the low groves of the ring stood in water and threw back the sun, and beyond them the canopy of the core rose dark. She had lived her whole life inside that shape. Nobody alive had seen it from here.

The wig came down off its height in a long glide, and Keo swung the nose toward the channel.

She watched him aim at the river and did not stop him. Her hand went to the pocket on the chest of her suit. Through the zip and the starved film she could feel the flower inside, pressed flat, where it had been since the ridge. Keo's eyes were on the water.

He brought the wig onto the line of the channel, heading upstream into the current, and lifted the nose. The hull hit. The slam came up through the seat, brown water sheeted over the windscreen, and spray blew in at the flap across her face and neck. It was the first cool thing in two hours, and her whole body turned toward it.

"Water contact," the voice said. "Logged."

Then the water was moving under the floor, shoving at the hull. Her body knew where it was before she let herself think it, and every part of her wanted to be up and back from the edge.

The hull slowed against the current until it hung almost still, the bank hardly moving, and the propulsor's note went thin and stayed thin. Keo pushed the stick forward. Nothing more came. The bank began to slide the other way.

The current took them back down the reach stern first. Keo fought the stick. The hull swung broadside and went on down like a trunk in flood, and fetched up hard against the downstream grating. The poles boomed on the hull. Upstream the river piled against it, and the floor tipped under her, toward the water.

"Lean!" she said. "The poles — lean!"

All three of them threw themselves toward the grating, Benal's weight against the back of her seat, Keo's shoulder hard against hers. On the low side the river stood up brown against the hull, nearly to the edge of the windscreen, and she could not see a hand's depth into it. Nothing came up out of it.

The hull hung there. Then it eased back until the floor was nearly level, and settled against the poles.

"It's holding," Keo said.

He shut the propulsor off. In its place the river rushed through the grating, and the Enclave came in at the flap: insects past counting, frogs in the flooded groves.

"Two," Benal said, reading the figure.

"Ben first," she said.

Keo shoved the door open on the grating side. The poles stood up past the top of the hull, and above them ran the top of the barrier. Benal crawled out onto the hull and onto the poles, and climbed hand over hand without looking down.

Teva reached behind her seat for the plates, the tablet with the impact on it and the gray thing Benal had dug out of the crown, and went out after him with all of it in the crook of one arm. The hull was hot under her knees and ground on the grating, and a meter below her the river poured past.

"Give them here." Benal lay flat along the top of the barrier with his arm down.

She handed the plates up a few at a time, then the tablet and the gray thing.

Keo came out slowly, one arm held in against his rib. He left blood on the first pole he took.

"Keo. Your foot," she said. "On my shoulder."

"You're done in."

"Climb."

He put his foot on her shoulder. She took his weight and pushed up. He got his torn hands round two poles and pulled, and Benal caught him by the wrist and hauled him over.

Then her. The poles were wet under her palms. Under her heels the hull groaned against the grating, still heeled toward the water piling against its side. She took two poles and went up, knees and feet against the wood, until her hands reached the top of the barrier.
