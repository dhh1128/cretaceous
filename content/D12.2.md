By nightfall the binding at the head of his spike had gone soft. Keo picked the end loose with his thumbnail and unwound three turns, drew them hard around the base of the tooth, and wound them back. When he pushed the tooth with his thumb it moved anyway.

Most of the light in the camp came off Benal's forearm, where the skin of the suit still glowed. It showed only where Benal sat, at the gap in the stakes with the razor-club across his knees. Teva's suit gave less, in patches. Keo could not see his own hands.

Under the rain he burned. The heat had no place in him he could find, not the leg, not his face, and then it went and the shaking came instead. The cut on his thigh itched and pulled beneath Teva's strips. It was closing. Every time the edges drew in he was weaker for it, as though the mending came out of his arms. He had been hungry all morning at the cutting. Somewhere on the afternoon march it had stopped, and he knew that was worse.

"Teva," he said. "At the spring. If we'd left it the—"

The rib took the rest. It caught at the top of the breath, and he had to wait with his mouth open for it to let go.

"First light," Teva said. "If I'm still well, you both eat, full portions. Then we go down."

He let the breath out a little at a time. When it came back without catching, he did not use it.

Teva got up. He heard the bag slop as she lifted it from beside the packs, and the patches of her went to the gap. Benal drew his knees in to let her by, and then she was outside the stakes. The pot had been empty since the river.

He watched what there was of her go down the rock toward the runnel. It stopped at the bottom and stooped, and stayed stooped a long time. Then it came back up. She came in through the gap and wiped her hands on her legs.

Benal turned his head toward Keo, and then back to the dark.

"Wake me for the middle," Teva said.

"No," Benal said. "You're the one who has to wake up well."

"Lie down, Ben," Keo said. "Go on. You sat up all last night."

"Half of it."

"You can't hear anything in this anyway. I'll sit up."

"The suit can feel it if anything comes." Benal shifted the club on his knees. "And if something eats me tonight, I'd like to be awake for it. I want to know what it was."

"Ben."

"Go to sleep, Keo. I'll wake you for the last."

Before long he could hear in Teva's breathing that she was asleep.

He went back to the binding. He wound it again, tighter, and it was no better.

His fingers had nearly stopped telling him anything when Benal's arm came up off his knee.

"Something warm," Benal said, very low. "Down at the water."

On Teva's forearm the suit lit once, faint, and faded. She did not stir.

Keo reached past the stakes for it and found nothing there. He found nothing of Benal either, a few steps away, or of Teva asleep behind him, where she should have lain in his head like banked coals. It had been gone all night, and he had not noticed.

"What is it?" he said.

"It doesn't say." Benal was on his feet. "It's where the bag is."

He held his arm out toward the runnel and woke the display hard.

For two seconds there was light. Every drop of rain hung lit in the air. At the edge of the runnel, over the bag, crouched an animal as long as Keo's forearm, low and thick through the shoulders, its fur slicked to its back and its jaws in the skin. It looked up into the light and did not stop chewing. Nearer and brighter than any of it was Benal, holding his arm out stiff, his face screwed up against the glare.

Then the dark came back, worse than before. Only the shape of Benal's arm still hung in Keo's eyes.

"*Didelphodon*," Benal said. "Well. Now I know."

"Wrong way," Keo said. "You're meant to bring it to Ben."

Nobody laughed.

Keo got the spike under him and stood. He went two steps toward the gap before he remembered he could not see. On the second his foot went down through the earth where the stakes were set, into a burrow, and his leg went after it to the knee. The ankle turned in the hole with all his weight above it.

"It's gone," Benal said. "Out of range. It took a third, possibly less. She tied the bag to the rock, so the rest is still in the water."

Keo got the leg out with both hands, a little at a time. He sat in the mud with it straight in front of him and said nothing about it. The spike lay across his lap. He had come down on it, and the binding had held.

"Keo?"

"Here."

He went back to his place on his hands and one knee.

Teva had not moved. The rain came down on the stakes and the fronds and drained away downhill to the runnel, where what was left of the mash hung in the water, where he had said to put it.

He lay awake a long time. It was the fever, he told himself.
