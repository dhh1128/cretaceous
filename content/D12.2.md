By nightfall the binding at the head of his spike had gone soft. Keo picked the end loose with his thumbnail, drew three turns hard around the base of the tooth, and wound them back. The tooth still moved.

Most of the light in the camp came off Benal's forearm, where the suit's skin still glowed. It showed Benal at the gap in the stakes with the razor-club across his knees, and nothing else. Teva glowed in patches. Keo could not see his own hands.

Under the rain he burned, and then the heat went and the shaking came instead. The cut on his thigh itched and pulled beneath Teva's strips. It was closing, and every time the edges drew in he was weaker, as though the mending came out of his arms. Somewhere on the afternoon march he had stopped being hungry, and he knew that was worse.

"Teva," he said. "At the spring. If we'd left it the—"

The rib took the rest. It caught at the top of the breath, and he had to wait with his mouth open for it to let go.

"First light," Teva said. "If I'm still well, you both eat, full portions. Then we go down."

He let the breath out a little at a time. When it came back without catching, he did not use it.

Teva got up. The bag slopped as she lifted it, and the patches of her went out through the gap. The pot had been empty since the river.

What there was of her went down the rock to the runnel and stooped there a long time. Then she came back in and wiped her hands on her legs.

Benal turned his head toward Keo, and then back to the dark.

"Wake me for the middle," Teva said.

"No," Benal said. "You're the one who has to wake up well."

"Lie down, Ben," Keo said. "You sat up all last night."

"Half of it."

"You can't hear anything in this anyway. I'll sit up."

Benal shifted the club on his knees. "The suit can feel it if anything comes. And if something eats me tonight, I'd like to be awake for it. I want to know what it was."

"Ben."

"Go to sleep, Keo. I'll wake you for the last."

Soon Teva's breathing told him she was asleep.

He wound the binding again, tighter. No better.

His fingers had nearly stopped telling him anything when Benal's arm came up off his knee.

"Something warm," Benal whispered. "Down at the water."

On Teva's forearm the suit lit once, faint, and faded. She did not stir.

Keo reached past the stakes for it and found nothing there. He found nothing of Benal either, a few steps away, or of Teva asleep behind him, where she should have lain in his head like banked coals. It had been gone all night, and he had not noticed.

"What is it?" he said.

"It doesn't say." Benal was on his feet. "It's where the bag is."

He held his arm out toward the runnel and woke the display hard.

For two seconds there was light. Every drop of rain hung lit in the air. At the edge of the runnel crouched an animal as long as Keo's forearm, thick through the shoulders, its fur slicked to its back and its jaws in the bag. It looked up into the light and went on chewing. Nearer and brighter than any of it was Benal, arm out stiff, face screwed up against the glare.

Then the dark came back, worse than before, with only the shape of Benal's arm still hanging in Keo's eyes.

"*Didelphodon*," Benal said. "Well. Now I know."

"Wrong way," Keo said. "You're meant to bring it to Ben."

Nobody laughed.

Keo got the spike under him and stood. He went two steps toward the gap before he remembered he could not see. On the second his foot broke through into a burrow, and his leg went after it to the knee. The ankle turned in the hole with all his weight above it.

"It's gone," Benal said. "Out of range. I'd say it took a third. Possibly less. She tied the bag to the rock, so the rest is still in the water."

Keo worked the leg out with both hands and sat in the mud with it straight in front of him. The spike lay across his lap. He had come down on it, and the binding had held.

"Keo?"

"Here."

He went back to his place on his hands and one knee.

Teva slept on. The rain drained downhill to the runnel, where what was left of the mash hung in the water, where he had said to put it.

He lay awake a long time. It was the fever, he told himself.
