The rain had not let up all the while they lay under the shelf. The gulch forked around a spur of rock, and brown water came down both arms at once.

Teva stopped at the split.

"It splits again," Benal said.

"Left goes north," Teva said.

"So did the last one. For a while."

The last one had run north a long way and then stopped at a wall of rock, and they had walked all of it back.

On the right of the split a bank of clay went up steep, twice his height, to a lip of bare limestone.

"I'll go up," Keo said. "From the top I can see which one runs through."

It was true, and it was not why. He could not stand at the bottom of one more gulch and wait.

"In this?" Teva said. "You won't see the other side of the gulch."

"I'll see more than I can from here."

"The clay's wet. We walk it and find out."

Benal was squinting up the bank with the rain in his face. "Wet clay at that angle," he said, "the top of it slides on what's underneath. I'd give it one try in two of holding you. Possibly worse. It's had hours to soak."

Keo shrugged the fieldpack off and set it on the stones at the foot of the bank, and laid his spike across it.

"Wait here," he said.

Teva caught his arm. Her hand closed on yesterday's burn, and he pulled free of it and went up.

The clay took his hands to the wrist. He kicked his toes in, and it gave and held, and gave again. Every handhold was oozing out between his fingers by the time he had the next one. He was light in the head, and the burn on his forearms had gone numb in the wet.

Halfway up, with his cheek against the clay and water running down the back of his neck, he thought of the suit.

It had hung on its pole at the back of the Repository, whole and faintly lit, his size. He had told himself he did not need it. He had his ears and his nose and his healing, and he could hold an animal with his head.

That had never been the reason. Yara had died in the grove. He was going to go out past the tangle with nothing on him, and live through what she had not.

And he had held Noli with his head, and the sky had taken her anyway. He had been beside both of them. There had been nothing in him that could have stopped either one.

He pulled himself up another arm's length.

The lip was close now, a dark edge of rock with water sheeting off it. He turned his head to look out where the gulches should be, and there was nothing but rain.

Then the clay let go under him in one sheet, and he went down the bank on it feet first, his hands dragging furrows through the mud. His boot hit the scree and turned under him. He pitched sideways onto the stones. A block of limestone took him in the side, and something in there cracked, and as he rolled off it an edge opened his leg above the knee.

The scree went out from under him and carried him down the slope, and stopped.

He lay on his side with his face on wet rock. Caught in the dead leaves between two stones was a spray of berries, red, the only color in the gulch. He had not eaten since the grounder. He looked at them a long time. He did not know them, and his parents had taught him what to do with a thing he did not know. His hand stayed where it was.

His leg was warm, and then the rain took the warmth off it. The blood was coming out through the torn legging and running pink over the stones.

He was cold. The rain was no cooler than it had been all afternoon, and he was cold all through, and then he was shaking. His teeth knocked. The sound of the water in the gulch was going further away, though he had not moved.

Was it the blood? The fall? The empty belly? Or the healing, starting already, the thing in him that had mended every hurt he had ever had and never asked for anything?

His hand went to the neck of his tunic. The feather was still there.

Stones clattered above him. Teva was coming down the scree on her hands as much as her feet, and behind her came Benal, slower, with one hand flat against his chest.

She was on her knees beside him with both hands on his leg, over the cut, and all her weight leaning into them, before she said a word. Keo heard himself shout.

"I said walk it," she said. "I told you the clay was wet. Ben gave you the odds, and you heard him, and you went up anyway, with nothing on you and nothing in you, in the rain, to look. What did you see? Tell me what you saw."

"Nothing," Keo said.

"Ben. Cloth."

Benal got down on the stones, breathing in short pulls. He took the legging where it was torn and ripped it open along the cut, then tore a strip off and wadded it, and Teva lifted her hands for the moment it took him to push it under them.

"It's deep," Benal said. "It isn't spurting, so I don't think it's the big vessel. Press on the top edge. That's where it's coming from."

Teva moved her hands up and leaned again.

"I can get up," Keo said.

"No."

He got an elbow under him. The thing in his side caught and took his breath away, and he lay back down.

He had walked in front of them since the tangle. He could not sit up.

"Hold this, Ben," Teva said. "Both hands."

Benal put his hands where hers had been. Teva went back up to the foot of the bank and picked up the fieldpack. She swung it up onto her back over her own pack, over the gray roll of her suit, and drew the straps snug. Then she picked up his spike.

"Leave that," Keo said. "I'll carry it."

He watched her come back down the scree toward him with his pack on her back.
