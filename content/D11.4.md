The gulch forked again in the rain.

They had already walked one branch to its end that afternoon, half an hour in and half an hour out, to a wall of wet rock. Now there were two more ways, and from down here they looked the same: gray limestone, scree, rain.

The near bank was red clay, rising a few meters to a lip of rock.

"I'll go up," Keo said. "I'll see from the top which one runs through."

It was true. It was not why. Standing at the fork with the two of them looking at him was worse than anything the clay could do.

"See what?" Teva said. "In this? You won't see the other side of the gulch from up there. We walk the left one. If it ends, we walk back."

"That's an hour."

"Then it's an hour."

Benal was looking at the bank. "That's clay over rock," he said. "On a slope like that, wet, I'd give you less than an even chance of getting to the top in one piece. Possibly a good deal less."

"Wait here," Keo said.

He set the fieldpack against a boulder and laid his spike across it. Teva caught his arm. He pulled free and went at the clay.

It gave under his fingers. He dug in and pulled, kicked a step, pulled again. The bank came away in wet handfuls. He went up anyway. It was something to do with his body, and his body wanted it.

Halfway up, with his face against the clay, he was in the Repository again.

The third suit had hung on the pole, his size, faintly lit. He had told himself he did not need it, and that was true. He had his ears and his nose and his healing and his sense. But under the true thing was the other one, which he had never said even inside his own head. Yara had been six. She had gone into the flooded grove with nothing between her and the water. He had been beside her, and nothing in him had reached the thing waiting there. He had left that suit on the pole to walk out into everything that had killed her, with nothing between him and it, and come home.

Noli. Yara. There had been nothing in him that could stop either one.

Near the lip the clay went all at once.

He went down on his back with the whole face of the bank moving under him. At the bottom his left ankle turned on the scree. A block of limestone took him in the ribs, and something in his side cracked. He rolled onto the broken rock below, and an edge opened his thigh above the knee.

He lay on the scree with the rain on his face and could not get his breath.

When it came back it came in short pulls that stopped at a point in his side. Blood ran pink in the rain across the stones. He turned his head away from it.

By his face, between two stones, there were berries, a small bright cluster against all that gray. He was hungry enough to want them. Nobody knew whether they were safe. He did not reach.

He was cold.

He shook, his teeth going, though the rain was warm. The rock under him tilted and drifted. The fall, the blood, nothing to eat since the grounder. Or the healing, which had always come for free, starting now on a body with nothing in it to spend. The world went a long way off, and he watched it go.

Then Teva was there.

She came over the scree fast, slipping, Benal behind her. She went down on her knees, found the cut with both hands, and leaned her whole weight on it. The pain came back and brought the world with it.

Then she said plenty.

"What were you doing? What did you think you'd see?" Her voice was flat and hard. "Don't move. Don't— Ben. Cloth. Anything."

"It's deep," Benal said. "Teva, it's— I can see—"

"Then don't look at it. Tear it."

Benal tore the split legging up past the knee, tore a strip off and folded it with shaking hands. She pressed it into the cut and leaned on it again.

"I'm all right," Keo said.

"You're not." She did not look up. "Breathe. Slow. Breathe."

He breathed. It caught at the same place every time.

"The rib," Benal said. "He's holding his side. And look at the ankle." His hand hovered near Keo's boot. "I don't know if that's broken. I don't think I'd be able to tell."

"Can you stand?" Teva said.

He got an elbow under him. The ankle took a little of his weight and then would not, and Benal caught him under the arm. He had carried what they all needed all the way from the tangle. He had walked in front of them with his spike and his sense out ahead, because that was what he was for. He could not get up off the rocks.

Teva held the cloth a moment longer. Then she tied it, hard, with a strip from the legging, and stood up.

She went down the scree to the boulder, her own pack on her back with the gray roll of her suit strapped on top. She picked up his fieldpack and looked once at the weight of it. Then she shrugged it up on top of her own and came back with his spike in her hand.

He watched her do it.

He did not understand yet what it meant.
