The gulch forked again in the rain, and they stood at the fork on rock above the water running through it.

They had already walked one branch to its end that afternoon, half an hour in and half an hour out, to a wall of wet rock with a thread of water coming down it. Now there were two more ways, one bending left and one going on, and from down here they looked the same: gray limestone, scree, the rain coming down between.

Up the near side the bank was clay, red-brown and streaked, rising a few meters to a lip of rock.

"I'll go up," Keo said. "I'll see from the top which one runs through."

It was true. It was not why. His legs would not keep still, and standing at the fork with the two of them looking at him was worse than anything the clay could do.

"See what?" Teva said. "In this?" She had to raise her voice over the rain. "You won't see the other side of the gulch from up there. We walk the left one. If it ends, we walk back."

"That's an hour."

"Then it's an hour."

Benal was looking at the bank. "That's clay over rock," he said. "On a slope like that, wet, I'd give you less than an even chance of getting to the top in one piece. Possibly a good deal less."

"Wait here," Keo said.

He set the fieldpack down against a boulder at the foot of the bank and laid his spike across it. Teva caught his arm. He pulled free and went at the clay with his hands.

It gave under his fingers. He dug them in and pulled, kicked a step for his boot, pulled again. The bank came away in wet handfuls that slid past his face and down. He went up anyway. It was something to do with his body, and his body wanted it so badly that he could feel the wanting in his arms.

Halfway up, with his face against the clay, he was in the Repository again.

The third suit had hung on the pole, his size, whole and faintly lit. He had not reached for it. He had told himself he did not need it, and that was true. He had his ears and his nose and his healing and his sense. But under the true thing was the other one, which he had never said even inside his own head. Yara had been six. She had gone into the flooded grove with nothing between her and the water. He had been beside her, and nothing in him had reached the thing that was waiting there. He had left that suit on the pole to walk out into everything that had killed her, with nothing between him and it, and come home.

Noli. Yara. There had been nothing in him that could stop either one.

He pulled himself up another handhold and did not know what he was climbing toward.

Near the lip the clay went all at once.

It slid out from under his boots in a single sheet, and his hands came away full of it, and he was going down the bank feet first on his back with the whole face of it moving under him. He hit the scree at the bottom and his left ankle turned under him with a sound he felt more than heard. He pitched sideways. A block of limestone took him in the ribs, and something in his side gave with a crack, and he rolled off it onto the broken rock below, and a sharp edge there opened the outside of his thigh above the knee.

He lay on the scree with the rain on his face and could not get his breath.

When it came back it came back wrong, in short pulls that stopped at a point in his side. There was blood on the stones. It ran pink in the rain and spread, and it was his. He turned his head away from it.

By his face, in the wet litter between two stones, there were berries. Small and round, a cluster of them, bright red against all that gray. He looked at them a long time. He was hungry enough to want them. He did not know whether they were safe. Nobody did. He did not reach.

He was cold.

It came up through him from somewhere low and spread, until he was shaking, his teeth going, though the rain was as warm as it had been all day. The rock under him seemed to tilt and drift. He did not know what it was. The fall, the blood, nothing to eat since the grounder. Or the healing, which had always come for free, starting now on a body with nothing in it to spend. The world was going a long way off, and he watched it go.

Then Teva was there.

She came over the scree fast, slipping and not caring, and Benal behind her. She did not say anything. She went down on her knees beside him, found the cut with both hands, and leaned her whole weight on it. The pain came back and brought the world with it.

Then she said plenty.

"What were you doing? What did you think you'd see?" Her voice was flat and fast and very hard. "Don't move. Don't— Ben. Cloth. Anything."

"It's deep," Benal said. He was on his knees on the other side, looking. "Teva, it's— I can see—"

"Then don't look at it. Tear it."

Benal took hold of the legging where it was split and pulled. The wet fiber tore up past the knee. He tore a strip off it and folded it with quick, shaking hands, and she took it from him, pressed it into the cut and leaned on it again.

"I'm all right," Keo said.

"You're not." She did not look up. "Breathe. Slow. Breathe."

He breathed. It caught at the same place every time.

"The rib," Benal said. "He's holding his side. And the ankle, look at the ankle." He had his hand near Keo's boot and was not touching it. "I don't know if that's broken. I can't tell. I don't think I'd be able to tell."

"Can you stand?" Teva said.

He tried. He got an elbow under him. The ankle took a little of his weight and then would not, the scree went sideways, and Benal caught him under the arm before he went down again. He had carried what they all needed all the way from the tangle. He had walked in front of them with his spike and his sense out ahead, every step of it, because that was what he was for. He could not get up off the rocks.

Teva held the cloth a moment longer. Then she tied it, hard, with a strip from the legging, and stood up.

She went back down the scree to the boulder where his fieldpack was leaning, with her own pack on her back and the gray roll of her suit strapped on top of it. She picked his up. She looked at it once, at the weight of it. Then she got her arms through the straps, shrugged it up on top of her own, settled it, and came back with his spike in her hand.

He watched her do it.

He did not understand yet what it meant.
