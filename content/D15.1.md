Seawater came up out of Benal before he was awake. He lay on his side and coughed it out of his nose and mouth, and every cough caught at the place under his ribs the constrictor had left.

He did not know where he was.

The light came from everywhere and laid no shadow. The wind was on his back, steady and hard, with not a leaf anywhere for it to shake. He breathed it in and waited for it to tell him something. At home every wind carried leaf rot and cook smoke and the musk of whatever had walked upwind of the barrier. This one smelled of salt, and weed rotting somewhere near his face, and a breath of sulfur like the spring's. He had never breathed air that told him nothing.

He pushed himself up, and the ground opened his palms. It was shell, not sand, broken and cemented into rock, with edges like a tooth.

Behind him surf broke white along the reef. The night's weed lay thrown along the shore in a line, with driftwood in it and pieces of the boat, and a float lay split open a stone's throw off.

Things moved in the weed, no bigger than his palm, shining green and purple. Every one went away from him sideways, legs ticking on the shell, into the cracks. They were like the river's crayfish and not, too flat and too wide, walking at the wrong angle to their own faces. He had got to six legs on the nearest when it went into a crack, and when he bent to look the crack was empty.

Farther along, dozens of birds quarreled over the weed. The forest's birds called and waited to be answered; these all shouted at once, harsh and gargling. He began to sort it into voices and lost count somewhere past twenty.

One left the rest and went out over the water inside the reef, a hand's breadth off the surface, wings held out stiff, above its own reflection. Benal counted. He was at thirty-one when it tipped, flapped twice, and came round. He did not know what had held it up.

Beyond the shore the ground lay low and flooded, a sheet of water ruffling in the wind, and past it the crown stood up. Along the shore, a short walk off, there were walls.

He was on Genesis.

He reached for the fieldpack. His hands went over the shell on both sides of him. Then he remembered the strap tearing at his ear in the black water, and his hands coming open.

The board had been in it.

He went through the equation in his head, the known part and the term with the question on it, and it was all there. He could write it again on anything flat, with anything that made a mark. But the board was what he had shown people. At home nobody had ever taken his word for what was in his head, his father least of all.

A head was what he had left.

The wind dried the salt water on his face and neck, and he shivered.

He got up and walked along the shore, calling. The surf took his voice before it had gone ten steps, and he called anyway.

"Keo! Teva!"

Keo was past the next heap of weed, on his side above the weed line. One arm was still through a piece of the deck, the struts locked round his elbow, and a furrow ran up the shell behind him from the water. He had dragged himself and the deck out of the sea before he passed out. His tunic was in rags. Everywhere a suit would have covered, the shell had opened him: hundreds of small cuts on his forearms, his neck, and the bare shin below the knee where Teva had cut the legging off.

His back rose and fell.

Benal knelt. "Keo." It came out cracked, half a word.

Keo's eyes opened. "Ben."

"Can you sit up?"

He got his good arm under Keo's shoulders. Keo came up grunting at the rib and sat with the deck across his lap.

"Where's Teva?" Keo's voice broke on her name.

"I don't know. I woke up back there alone."

Keo looked along the shore, one way and then the other. His fingers were still locked round a strut.

"You can let go now," Benal said.

The hand stayed shut.

"Keo. We're on the ground."

Keo looked down at his hand. Then he opened it, a finger at a time, worked his arm out of the struts, and laid the deck down on the shell.

"Then this way," Keo said.

Keo got up on his good foot and Benal's shoulder, and they went along the shore, Keo hopping, Benal's weak arm round his back. Ahead a spur of rock ran out across the shore into the water.

"Wait," Benal said.

A voice came from beyond the spur, hoarse, in pieces between the waves.

"Keo! Ben!"

"Teva!" Keo shouted.

She was on the far side of the spur, in the weed to her shins with her back to them, calling toward the sea. She turned and saw them, and stopped calling. A cut ran across her forehead below the helmet's rim, and blood had dried down one side of her face to the jaw.

She looked at them, and her face went all at once. The tears ran down through the dried blood, and she did not wipe them.

"You're here," she said, with hardly any voice.

"You're bleeding," Keo said.

"It's stopped."

"Anywhere else?"

"Everywhere. Same as you."

She came out of the weed and got under Keo's other arm, and for a moment the three of them only held on to each other.

Keo straightened between them and looked along the shore toward the walls.

"We made—" he said, and did not finish.

The next wave broke on the reef. Benal heard it rise and come down and drag back hissing over the coral, and then he heard it again. The birds had gone quiet. His skin prickled, and the air smelled of lightning.

It was the same wave, every part of it where it had been the first time. From the boat he had listened to that reef, and no two waves on it had been alike.

Against his shoulder Keo had gone still, staring at nothing past the break. Beyond him Teva had a hand pressed over her eyes.

Then there was only the surf again, each wave its own. Teva took her hand down. None of them said a word about it.

"Look," Keo said. "Water."

It was everywhere, the night's rain standing in the limestone, in cups and long hollows, some as long as Benal was. He had walked past it the whole way along the shore.

"Is it fresh?" Teva said.

Benal knelt at the nearest and put his mouth to it. After a day and a night of salt it tasted of stone, and nothing else.

"It's rain."

They went down on their knees at it, all three, Keo with his bad leg out straight behind him, and drank until they could not. Benal lifted his head to breathe.

"Drink, Ben," Keo said.

He drank again, then sat back on his heels with the water running off his chin.

"It won't last, not on this," he said, to nobody. "The shell's all holes, and the rock under it's not much better. By tomorrow there'll only be the deep ones left, I think. Possibly not those."

He looked along the shore at the station. Its walls stood where the shore met the flood, salt-white. No light showed in them. He listened, and under the surf and the wind and the birds they gave him nothing at all.

They had walked eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> to it.

It did not know they were here.
