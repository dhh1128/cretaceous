Seawater came up out of Benal before he was awake. He lay on his side and coughed it out of his nose and mouth, and every cough caught at the place under his ribs that had been wrong since the constrictor.

He did not know where he was.

The light came from everywhere at once and laid no shadow under anything. The wind was on his back, steady and hard, and there was not a leaf anywhere for it to shake. He breathed it in and waited for it to tell him something. At home every wind carried leaf rot and cook smoke and the musk of whatever had walked upwind of the barrier, and on the walk it had carried more than he could sort. This one smelled of salt, and of weed rotting somewhere near his face, with a breath of sulfur in it like the spring's. He had never in his life breathed air that told him nothing.

He pushed himself up, and the ground opened his palms. It was shell, not sand, broken and cemented into rock, with edges like the lip of a broken pot.

He sat and looked at his hands, and then at the rest of it.

Behind him the surf stood up white along the reef and came down, with a roar that did not let up. The bank ran off in both directions, a long heap of shell and rock. The night's weed lay thrown along it in a line, with driftwood in it and pieces of the boat. A float lay split open on its side a stone's throw off.

Things moved in the weed. They were no bigger than his palm, and shone green and purple as they went. Every one of them went away from him sideways, its legs ticking on the shell, into the cracks. They were like the river's crayfish and not like them, too flat and too wide, walking at the wrong angle to their own faces. He tried to count the legs on the nearest. He had got to six when it went into a crack, and when he bent to look the crack was empty.

Farther along, birds were quarreling over the weed. There were dozens of them, dropping into it and lifting out of it, and the racket they made was nothing like the forest's birds, which called and waited to be answered. These all shouted at once, harsh and gargling. He began to sort it into voices and lost count somewhere past twenty.

One of them left the rest and went out over the water inside the reef. It flew a hand's breadth off the surface, with its wings held out stiff. It did not move them. Benal counted. It went on above its own reflection. He was at thirty-one when it tipped, flapped twice, and came round. He did not know what had held it up.

Beyond the bank the ground lay low and flooded, a sheet of water ruffling in the wind, and past it the crown stood up pale. Along the bank, a short walk off, there were walls.

He was on Genesis.

He reached for the fieldpack.

It was not there. His hands went over the shell on both sides of him. Then he remembered the strap going up over his head in the black water, tearing at his ear, and his hands coming open.

The board had been in it.

He went through the equation in his head, the known part and the term with the question on it, and it was all there. He could write it again on anything flat, with anything that made a mark. But the board was what he had shown people. At home nobody had ever taken his word for what was in his head, his father least of all.

A head was what he had left.

He shivered. All night the sea had been nearly as warm as his own body, and now the wind was drying the salt water off his face and neck.

He got up and walked along the bank, calling. The surf took his voice before it had gone ten steps from him, and he called anyway.

"Keo! Teva!"

Keo was past the next heap of weed, on his side above the line where the weed stopped. One arm was still through a piece of the deck with the struts locked round his elbow, and a furrow ran up the shell behind him from the edge of the water. He had dragged himself and the deck out of the sea before he passed out. His tunic was in rags. Everywhere a suit would have covered, the shell had opened him: hundreds of small cuts on his forearms, his neck, and the bare shin below the knee where Teva had cut the legging off.

His back rose and fell.

Benal knelt. "Keo."

Keo's eyes opened. "Ben."

"Can you sit up?"

He got his good arm under Keo's shoulders. Keo came up with him, grunting at the rib, and sat with the deck across his lap.

"Where's Teva?"

"I don't know. I woke up back there on my own."

Keo looked along the bank, one way and then the other. His fingers were still locked round a strut.

"You can let go of it now," Benal said.

The hand stayed shut.

"Keo. You can let go. We're on the ground."

Keo looked down at his hand. Then he opened it, a finger at a time, worked his arm out of the struts, and laid the piece of deck down on the shell.

"Which way did you come?"

"From back there. There was nobody."

"Then this way."

Keo got up on his good foot and Benal's shoulder, and they went along the bank, Keo hopping and leaning, Benal with his weak arm round Keo's back. Ahead a spur of rock ran out across the bank into the water.

"Wait," Benal said.

A voice came from beyond the spur, hoarse, in pieces between the waves.

"Keo! Ben!"

"Teva!" Keo shouted.

She was on the far side of the spur, standing in the weed to her shins with her back to them, calling toward the sea. She turned and saw them, and stopped calling. There was a cut across her forehead below the helmet's rim, and blood had dried down one side of her face to the jaw.

For a while she only stood there in the weed and looked at them.

"You're here," she said.

"You're bleeding," Keo said.

"It's stopped."

"Anywhere else?"

"Everywhere. Same as you."

She came out of the weed and got under Keo's other arm.

Keo straightened between them. He looked along the bank toward the walls.

"We made—" he said, and did not finish.

The next wave broke on the reef. Benal heard it rise and come down and drag back hissing over the coral, and then he heard it again.

It was the same wave, every part of it where it had been the first time. He had listened to that reef from the boat, and no two waves on it had been alike.

Against his shoulder Keo had gone still, staring at nothing out past the break. Beyond him Teva had a hand pressed over her eyes.

Then there was only the surf again, one wave and the next, each its own. Teva took her hand down. None of them said a word about it.

"Look," Keo said. "Water."

It was everywhere. Benal had walked past it the whole way along the bank, the night's rain standing in the limestone, in cups and long hollows and pans as long as he was.

"Is it fresh?" Teva said.

Benal knelt at the nearest and put his mouth to it. After a day and a night of salt it tasted of stone, and of nothing else.

"It's rain."

They went down on their knees at it, all three, Keo with his bad leg out straight behind him, and drank until they could not. Benal lifted his head to breathe.

"Drink, Ben," Keo said.

He drank again. Then he sat back on his heels with the water running off his chin.

"It won't stand long, not on this," he said, to nobody. "The shell's all holes, and the rock under it isn't much better. What falls on it drains away. By tomorrow, I think, there'll only be the deep ones left. Possibly not those."

He looked along the bank at the station. Its walls stood on the landward edge of the bank, salt-white, square, with the flood behind them. No light showed in them. He listened, and under the surf and the wind and the birds they gave him nothing at all.

They had walked eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> to it.

It did not know they were here.
