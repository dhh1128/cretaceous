The rock still held the day's heat. Benal sat with his palms on it, a few paces back from where the brow dropped to the sea. The sun had gone into the water on his left. Behind him the half moon stood high over the mainland. It threw his shadow out across the bare rock toward the edge.

All afternoon the concrete had been full of the voice, the cells' fizz and the argument. His ears had had enough.

Far below, the swell broke along the reef. Each one came in with a long roar and rattled back over the coral. He was counting them and could not stop.

A few paces along the brow, a crack split the rock, an arm's length across. The first time he sat here he had noticed the hole at its lip. It was round and clean-sided, too round for rain or sea to have made. A stain of rust ran down the rock under it. Nothing else up here bled rust. Something of the founders' had stood in that hole once and rusted away. Beside the crack the rock dipped into a hollow packed with stones that looked like any stones.

He had come to do the numbers, so he did them. Full some time tomorrow afternoon, and the water would be gone before then. At first light it would have about ninety-five kilometers on its figure, <!-- @WF-wig.range.firstlight: 95km --> and home was about eighty-eight. Then there was the weight it would let them carry. He did them again from the beginning, slower. They came out the same.

The air smelled of lightning. The hairs lifted along his arms. The surf was gone. It was day.

A man was on his knees by the crack, lifting stones out of the hollow and setting them to one side. His clothes were patched, and faded past any color. He took something up off the rock in both hands and lowered it into the hollow. He pressed rubble round it and over it. Then he set the stones back on top, one at a time. When he got up, he came up stiff, a little at a time.

Where the hole had been, the lip of the crack was whole rock.

The man put a hand to the small of his back and turned his head to look along the crown. Benal saw his face. It was thin. The hair above it was gray. Through the white of the left eyebrow ran a bare notch that broke it in two.

Benal had seen that eyebrow at first light yesterday, on a young man's face in the metal of the register. The leaf had said temporal lab. He had read that leaf longer than most, and found it again for Teva. Elias Sandoval. At the back of the register was the roll of the two hundred <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> who walked. There was no Elias Sandoval on it. He was one of the seventy-five or so who stayed.

Benal looked past old Elias along the crown. There were no arches and no mounds. The crown was bare rock to its far end. There was no Genesis.

Then the surf was back, and the moon, and the hole at the crack's lip. Along the crown the arches stood against the sky.

"He's old," Benal said.

The swell came in and went back.

"S-so it's after. Years after they c-came." He shook his head. "No. There wasn't anything. No arches. N-no mounds. Rock." He looked along at the arches. "The founders put those up. They're still standing. So it's before."

He pressed his palms into the rock.

"Before they came, and he's old. He's young on the leaf. The leaf's from before the jump. So he got old after it. Here." He breathed out. "He went back. Further back than here, as the leaves said. And he lived, and he got old on this rock."

His hands were not steady. He looked at the hollow, and the crack beside it.

The moon was enough to see the hole, but not enough to dig by. The hollow was a few paces from the drop. The stones were packed hard. He had no knife of his own, and nothing to lever them up with. And he did not trust what he had seen. It had been one look, alone, at night. Anyone could tell him it was fever. He wanted to be sure before anyone could. He would come back in the morning.

It was not because his arm hurt and he was alone and afraid. He was just being reasonable.

He got up and went back across the top. Past the arches the air turned. It came against his face off the land, over the water, rank with the mainland after days of nothing but salt: sodden leaves, mud and rot. It was the forest, and the flood standing in it. He stopped and breathed it until it thinned.

In the room by the inner door the other two were awake, sitting against the wall. The hum came up through the floor.

"Where were you?" Teva said.

"On the brow, on the sea side. It's quiet."

"For your ears."

"For my ears."

Keo had his legs out in front of him. "Did you do the numbers?"

"Full tomorrow afternoon. The water's gone before that. At first light, a few kilometers over, on the voice's figure."

"I'll count the water again at first light," Teva said.

He sat down against the wall beside Keo and put his palms on the floor. He said nothing about the man at the crack. Yesterday, in this room, he had said a true thing. Keo had asked what difference it made, and Teva had agreed with him.
