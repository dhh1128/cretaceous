The rock still held the day's heat. Benal sat with his palms on it, a few paces back from where the brow dropped to the sea, and let it come up into his hands. The sun had gone into the water on his left. Behind him the half moon stood high over the mainland, and it threw his shadow out ahead of him across the bare rock toward the edge.

All afternoon the concrete had been full. The voice talked out of the wig's cabin, the cells fizzed under its floor, and then Teva and Keo went at each other about first light and waiting. His ears had taken as much as they could. He had told them he was going somewhere quiet and come the other way, across the top and past the arches, to the seaward brow.

Far below, the swell broke along the reef at the foot of the face. He heard each one come in with a long roar and rattle back over the coral, and he found he was counting them and could not stop.

A few paces along the brow, a crack split the rock, an arm's length across. The first time he sat here he had noticed the hole at its lip. It was round and clean-sided, too round for anything the rain or the sea had made, and a stain of rust ran down the rock under it. Nothing else up here bled rust. Something of the founders' had stood in that hole once and rotted away. Beside the crack the rock dipped into a hollow packed with stones, the same as any stones. In the moonlight he could just make out the hole.

He had come to do the numbers, so he did them. Full about the middle of tomorrow, the voice said, and the middle of tomorrow was when the water ran out. At first light, about ninety-five kilometers on its figure, <!-- @WF-wig.range.firstlight: 95km --> and home was eighty and the sea, about eighty-eight. Then he took the weight it would let them carry and worked out how many plates went into it. He did them again from the beginning, slower. They came out the same.

The air smelled of lightning. The hairs lifted along his arms, the surf was gone, and it was day.

A man was on his knees by the crack, lifting stones out of the hollow and setting them to one side. His clothes were patched with other cloth and faded nearly white. He took something up off the rock in both hands and lowered it into the hollow. He pressed rubble round it and over it, and then he set the stones back on top, one at a time. He was slow at it. When he straightened, his back would not come straight all at once.

Where the hole had been, the lip of the crack was whole rock.

The man put a hand to the small of his back and turned his head to look along the crown, and Benal saw his face. It was thin, and the hair above it was gray. Through the white of the left eyebrow ran a bare notch that broke it in two.

Benal had seen that eyebrow at first light yesterday, on a young man's face in the metal of the register. Temporal lab. He had read that leaf longer than any other, and found it again for Teva. Elias Sandoval. At the back of the register was the roll of the two hundred <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> who walked, and there was no Elias Sandoval on it. He was one of the seventy-five or so who stayed.

Benal looked past him along the crown. There were no arches and no mounds. The crown was bare rock to its far end. There was no Genesis.

Then the surf was back, and the moon, and the hole at the crack's lip. Along the crown the arches stood black against the sky.

"He's old," Benal said.

The swell came in and went back.

"S-so it's after. Years after they c-came." He shook his head. "No. There wasn't anything. No arches. N-no mounds. Rock." He looked along at the frames. "The founders put those up. They're still standing. So it's before."

He pressed his palms into the rock.

"Before they came, and he's old. He's young on the leaf. The leaf's from before the jump. So he got old after it. Here." He breathed out. "He went back. Further back than here, as the leaves said. And he lived, and he got old on this rock, and he buried something beside that crack."

His hands were not steady. He looked along at the hollow, and the crack beside it.

The moon was enough to see the hole by and not enough to dig by, and the hollow was a few paces from the drop. The stones were packed hard. He had no knife of his own, and nothing to lever them up with. And he did not trust what he had seen. It had been one look, alone in the dark at the end of a day of the voice in his ears, and anyone could tell him it was fever. He wanted to be sure before anyone could.

He was glad of every one of the reasons, and he knew he was glad.

He got up and went back across the top. Past the arches the air turned. It had lain still all evening. Now it came against his face off the land, over the water, and brought the mainland with it after days of nothing but salt: wet leaves, mud and rot. It was the forest, and the flood standing in it. He stopped and breathed it until it thinned.

In the room by the inner door the other two were awake, sitting against the wall, and the hum came up through the floor.

"Where were you?" Teva said.

"On the brow. The seaward side, past the arches. It's quiet."

"For your ears."

"For my ears."

Keo had his bad foot out in front of him. "Did you do the numbers?"

"Full about the middle of tomorrow. At first light, about ninety-five, on the voice's figure. Home's about eighty-eight."

"A few over," Keo said.

"A few over. They come out the same every time I do them."

Teva was looking at him. "Did you drink?"

"Not yet."

"Then you're first at the hollows in the morning. I'll count the water again at first light."

He sat down against the wall beside Keo and put his palms on the floor. He said nothing about the man at the crack. Yesterday, in this room, he had said a true thing. Keo had asked what difference it made, and Teva had agreed with him. This one he wanted in his hands before he said it.
