The cloth from the scree came away from the cut in one wet fold, pink all through. Under it the bleeding had slowed to an ooze. Teva laid the cloth aside and cut what was left of his legging off at the knee.

Over her head the fronds sagged and dripped. Benal was on his knees behind her, holding the low edge of them up on his forearm, and the worst of the water ran off behind her instead of into the cut.

"How bad?" Keo said.

"Lie still."

"Teva. How bad is it?"

"It's stopped bleeding." She looked at it a moment longer. "Mostly."

The cut ran across the outside of his thigh, a hand long, gaping where the stone had gone in deepest. The light was going gray, and she bent close to see the edges.

She had done this before, for her mother, in their own nest, and her hands remembered how before she did. It had been a bite, and on the first day it had looked like nothing much. Then the fever came, and then the days, and then the morning when the breathing stopped. She could still hear that breathing if she let herself.

The edges of Keo's cut were clean. Her mother's had been clean too, the first day.

"What does it look like?" Benal said.

"Dirty."

"There'll be grit from the scree in it." He leaned to see past her shoulder, and the fronds sagged with him. "Limestone, mostly. It's the clay I'd worry about, if any of it got in, because clay holds—"

"Ben. The salves."

He let the fronds down and went stooping to the packs at the back.

"Save the salve," Keo said.

Benal brought a pot back. Teva pulled the stopper and set it by her knee.

"Now the spikes," she said. "Up at the back, where the rock's driest."

The two spikes lay where she had dropped them, and the bindings at their heads had gone dark and soft.

"Nothing in here is dry," Benal said. "If it rains all night, they won't be dry before tomorrow evening." He picked them up anyway. "Possibly not then."

"Then put them where they'll get no wetter."

He laid them along a ledge at the back, where the rock was dark but not running, and came back and reached for the fronds again. He was breathing high and shallow, stopping short each time at something under his ribs.

"Now sit down," she said. "Back there, out of the drip."

"I can hold the fronds up. I'm not doing anything else."

"You've been breathing like that since the scree."

"That's the chest, from the constrictor." He considered it. "It's not much worse than it was."

"Sit down, Ben."

He looked at the fronds, and at her, and went. At the back he folded himself down with his knees nearly to his chin. "Then I'll take the first watch."

"Wake me for the last."

She looked down at the leg. At the foot of the bank she had had him by the arm, and she had let him pull free. Nobody could hold Keo once he had decided something. That was true, and it was no use to her now. She had let go.

She pulled the stopper from a full skin. "Hold still."

The rainwater went into the cut and came out pink across the rock, and then paler. His leg jumped under her hand. She pinned it with her forearm and poured again, slowly, holding the wound open with two fingers. Gray specks of grit washed out. Some stayed, and those she went in after with her fingertips.

Keo groaned through his teeth. "Careful."

"Be quiet or be bait. Your choice."

At the back of the overhang Benal made a sound that was nearly a laugh, and it caught short against his chest.

"What's in there?" Keo said.

"Rock."

"Get it out."

"What do you think I'm doing?"

She found the last sliver by feel, flicked it away and poured once more. Then she scooped salve onto two fingers. It smelled bitter, and she worked it into the cut along both edges while Keo breathed in short pulls through his nose. She folded a pad from the cut-off legging, laid it over the salve and held it there.

Under her hand his leg had begun to shake, a hard, steady shiver that would not stop, though the rain was warm. He lay with one hand flat on his chest inside the neck of his tunic. She thought he was holding the rib.

Then her own hands shook, once, and she pressed them against the pad until they stopped.

She was cold. It was not the rain. She had been wet through since the front came over. There was nothing left in her to make heat with: nothing eaten since the grounder, no sleep worth the name for two days. The cold sat in the middle of her and worked outward. Benal sat in his suit, not shaking at all.

"Your teeth are going," Benal said. "I can hear them from here."

"Then stop listening to them."

"Put your suit on," Keo said. His own teeth knocked on the words.

"I am."

She rinsed her hands in the drip at the edge of the fronds and went to her pack. The suit was the gray roll strapped on top. Yesterday it had cooked her.

"It'll have dumped the heat by now," Benal said. "A whole day of rain. It won't warm you, it isn't built for that, but it'll keep the—"

"I know what it's built for."

She stepped into it wet, over wet skin, and dragged it up. Her bad shoulder caught, and she worked it in anyway. The tab hissed shut at her throat.

When she knelt again a drip was falling square on the pad. She leaned over it until the drip fell on her back instead.

The cord was in Keo's fieldpack. She cut a length and bound his ankle over the boot, around and under and around, pulling each turn hard.

"This goes tight," she said. "Tell me if your toes go numb."

"They're numb already."

She let out one turn and tied it off.

"Eat something," Keo said. "There's pan in the fieldpack."

"The pan ran out a week ago."

He frowned, working it out, and she watched him not manage it.

Halfway through a breath he stopped, and his face pulled tight.

"It's the rib," he said.

"I know. Breathe slow. There's nothing I can do for that one."

He breathed slowly. She could hear it catch at the same place every time. Under it she could hear the other breathing, from the nest. She made herself listen only to his.

It was dark now, and the water in the gulch was louder. She cut the rest of the legging into strips by feel and began to wind them around his thigh over the pad, lifting the leg at the knee to pass each one under. The rain went on over the fronds, and through them, and down the rock into the gulch.

"We'll be—" Keo said, and stopped. He was shaking again.

"Don't."

She passed the next strip under and drew it snug.

She took the last turn around, tucked the end and tied it, pulling the knot down flat. Then she laid her palm on the dressing to see that it held. It held. The cloth was wet already. Under it she could feel the warmth of his leg and his pulse going in it, fast.

Her hand stayed there.

Then she took it away and wiped it on her knee.

"Sleep," she said.

She could just see that his face was turned toward her in the dark, and nothing else.
