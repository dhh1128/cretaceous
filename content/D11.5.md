Dusk was coming on by the time they had Keo under another overhang. The cloth from the scree peeled off the cut under Teva's fingers in one wet fold, pink all through. The bleeding had slowed to an ooze. She cut what was left of his legging off at the knee.

Over her head the fronds sagged and dripped. Benal knelt behind her, propping their low edge on his forearm, and the worst of the water ran off behind her instead of into the cut.

"How bad?" Keo said.

"Lie still."

"Teva. How bad is it?"

"It's stopped bleeding." She looked again. "Mostly."

The cut ran across the outside of his thigh, a hand long, gaping where the stone had gone in deepest.

She had done this before, for her mother, in their own nest, and her hands remembered before she did. A bite, and on the first day it had looked like nothing much. Then the fever, and the days, and the morning the breathing stopped. She could still hear that breathing if she let herself.

The edges of Keo's cut were clean. Her mother's had been clean too, the first day.

"What's it look like?" Benal said.

"Dirty."

"There'll be grit from the scree in it." He leaned to see past her shoulder, and the fronds sagged with him. "Limestone, mostly. It's the clay I'd worry about, because clay holds—"

"Ben. The salves."

He let the fronds down and went stooping to the packs.

"Save the salve," Keo said.

Benal brought a pot back. Teva unstoppered it and set it by her knee.

"Now the spikes," she said. "Up at the back, where the rock's driest."

"Nothing in here's dry," Benal said. But he took them, their bindings soaked and soft, and laid them on a ledge at the back where the rock was dark but not running. Then he came back and reached for the fronds again, his breath catching high under his ribs.

"Now sit down," she said. "Back there, out of the drip."

"I can hold the fronds up."

"You've been breathing like that since the scree."

"That's the constrictor." He considered it. "It's not much worse."

"Sit down, Ben."

He looked at the fronds, and at her, and went. At the back he folded himself down with his knees nearly to his chin. "Then I'll take first watch."

"Wake me for the last."

She looked down at the leg. At the foot of the bank she had had him by the arm, and she had let him pull free. Nobody could hold Keo once he had decided something. That was true, and no use to her now. She had let go.

She unstoppered a full skin. "Hold still."

The rainwater went into the cut and came out pink across the rock, then paler. His leg jumped. She pinned it with her forearm and poured again, holding the wound open with two fingers. Grit washed out. What stayed, she went in after with her fingertips.

Keo groaned through his teeth. "Careful."

"Be quiet or be bait. Your choice."

At the back Benal huffed, nearly a laugh, and it caught on his chest.

"What's in there?" Keo said.

"Rock."

"Get it out."

"What do you think I'm doing?"

She found the last sliver by feel, flicked it away and poured once more. The salve smelled bitter. She worked it along both edges while Keo breathed in short pulls through his nose, then folded a pad from the cut-off legging and held it over the salve.

Under her hand his leg had begun to shake, a hard shiver that would not stop, though the rain was warm. He lay with one hand flat on his chest inside the neck of his tunic. She thought he was holding the rib.

Then her own hands shook, once, and she pressed them against the pad until they stopped.

She was cold, and it was not the rain. There was nothing left in her to make heat with: nothing eaten since the grounder, no sleep worth the name for two days. The cold sat in the middle of her and worked outward. Benal sat in his suit, not shaking at all.

"Your teeth are going," Benal said. "I can hear them from here."

"Then stop listening."

"Put your suit on," Keo said. His own teeth knocked on the words.

"I am."

She went to her pack. The suit was the roll strapped on top. Yesterday it had cooked her.

"It'll have dumped the heat by now," Benal said. "A whole day of rain. It won't warm you, it isn't built for that, but it'll keep the—"

"I know what it's built for."

She stepped into it wet, over wet skin, and dragged it up. Her bad shoulder caught; she worked it in anyway. The tab hissed shut at her throat.

When she knelt again a drip was falling square on the pad. She leaned over it until it fell on her back instead.

She cut a length of cord from Keo's fieldpack and bound the ankle he had turned in the fall, over the boot, around and under and around, hauling each turn hard.

"Tell me if your toes go numb."

"They're numb already."

She let out one turn and tied it off.

"Eat something," Keo said. "There's pan in the fieldpack."

"The pan ran out a week ago."

He frowned, working it out, and she watched him not manage it.

Halfway through a breath he stopped, and his face pulled tight.

"It's the rib," he said.

"I know. Breathe slow. There's nothing I can do for that one."

He breathed slow. It caught at the same place every time. Under it she could hear the other breathing, from the nest. She made herself listen only to his.

It was dark now, and the water in the gulch was louder. She cut the rest of the legging into strips by feel and wound them around his thigh over the pad, lifting the leg at the knee to pass each one under. The rain went on over the fronds, and through them, and down the rock into the gulch.

"We'll be—" Keo said, and stopped. He was shaking again.

"Don't."

She took the last turn around, tucked the end, and pulled the knot down flat. Then she laid her palm on the dressing. It held. The cloth was wet already. Under it she could feel the warmth of his leg and his pulse in it, fast.

Her hand stayed there.

Then she took it away and wiped it on her knee.

"Sleep," she said.

In the dark she could see only that his face was turned toward her.
