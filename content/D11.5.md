Her hands were in it before anyone had said a word.

They had got him under a second overhang a short way along the gulch, a step above the running channel, and laid him on his back with the leg out straight. Benal had stood stakes across the open front and dragged thorn against them. Where the stone roof gave out they had laid fern fronds, weighted with stones, and the fronds leaked.

Teva peeled back the cloth. It came away dark and heavy, but under it the bleeding had slowed to an ooze. She cut the torn legging off at the knee with her tooth-knife and kept it, because they were going to need it.

Benal held a frond over her work, tilting the drip past her elbow.

"Lower," she said. "You're dripping down my back."

"I'm dripping on everything," Benal said. "It's a structural problem. The frond is mostly holes." He lowered it.

The cut was a hand's length, deepest in the middle, its lips ragged and packed with grit.

She knew how to do this. She had done it in their own nest, for her mother. A bite first, hardly worth a look. Then the fever. Then the red creeping out from the bite a little farther every morning, for days. Teva had cleaned that wound every one of those mornings, and she still had the sound of the breathing at the end in her ears whenever she let things go quiet.

She looked at the edges of Keo's wound for the color that had come into her mother's, and it was not there. Not yet, which was all that *yet* meant.

"Ben. The salves."

"In my pack. The side pocket, I think, unless they've migrated."

"I know where they are. Get them."

He wedged the frond against the rock and went. When he came back with the little pots she had already started on the grit.

"Now the spikes," she said. "Up on the ledge, off the ground."

Wet bindings went soft and slipped, and if they stayed wet long enough the archaea in them woke and the wood began to go.

"It isn't dry anywhere," Benal said. "There are only degrees of wet. I can find you the least wet place, roughly, if you tell me what you'd accept as a—"

"The least wet place."

He went.

"Now sit down."

"I'm all right."

"You've been breathing like that since the scree." Careful and shallow, with a catch at the top of each breath, in the chest the snake had crushed. "Sit by the stakes, out of the drip, with your club. You've got first watch, and I need you awake for it. I'll take the last."

"I can take the first and the middle," Benal said. "I don't sleep well on stone anyway. I've been doing the arithmetic, and if you count the hours I actually sleep against the hours I lie here thinking about sleeping—"

"Three hours. Then you wake me."

"Three hours," he said. "Roughly."

He sat down by the stakes with the club across his knees.

"Save the salve," Keo said, from a long way off. "We'll need it. Later. For worse."

She opened the first pot: bitter and green, the smell of every cut she had ever had at home.

"Ben's knife," Keo said. "Use his, yours is—"

"Ben hasn't got a knife."

"Oh." He thought about it, a long way off. "That's right."

She unstoppered a skin of the rainwater they had caught at midday and poured it slowly into the cut, and the grit ran out pink onto the stone. What would not wash out she picked out one piece at a time with her fingernails, and at every piece he went rigid under her hands.

"Careful," he groaned.

"Be quiet or be bait. Your choice."

Behind her Benal made a sound that started as a laugh, turned into a cough halfway, and ended with him holding his chest.

"Sorry," he said. "That was funny. I wasn't ready for it to be funny."

Keo shut his eyes. Teva went on picking. Her voice had come out flat and hard, and that was right. Nobody was looking at her hands.

Halfway through she was cold.

She had been wet since the front, with nothing in her to make heat back with: nothing to eat since Noli's grounder, and no sleep worth the name for two nights. The rain on her neck was warm as breath, and her hands began to shake.

Benal, by the stakes in his suit, was not shaking. Keo was, but Keo had a hole in his leg.

Her suit lay beside her in a gray wet heap. Yesterday on the fern it had filled with her own heat, and she had climbed out of it to breathe. She stepped into it as it was, wet over wet skin, and pressed the tab until it hissed shut.

It did not make her warm. She braced her hands against the rock and went on. She did not look at her arm to see what the suit thought of itself. She would not have believed it.

"Better?" Benal said from the stakes.

"Hold the frond."

He got up, careful of his chest, and held it.

She spread the salve into the cut. Keo's breath went in through his teeth and stayed there. She cut the legging into strips, laid a pad over the salve, and bound it round the thigh, firm and flat. A drop came through the fronds onto the dressing. She moved her shoulder to take the next.

A suit would have taken that edge. She had thought it on the scree, and she thought it again now. He had come out past the tangle in a tunic and leggings, the only one of them with nothing between him and anything, and she had looked at that on the first morning and let it go, and let it go every morning after. She pulled the strip tighter than it needed and made herself ease it off again.

"Tighter," Keo said. "No. Looser. You want it so the blood can still—"

"I know what I want."

"I'm only telling you—"

"I know."

His ankle had swollen inside the boot until the hide stood tight. She left the boot on, because it would never go back on, and bound the ankle over it with cord, tight enough that he hissed.

"The rib," Keo said.

"There's nothing to do for the rib. Breathe slow."

"I am breathing slow."

"Slower."

It was nearly dark, and she did the rest by feel. He was shaking hard now, and she could feel that too, coming up through the leg into her hands.

"Teva," he said. "It's all right. We'll be—"

"Don't."

He stopped. She went on with the cloth.

She tied off the last strip. Then her hand stayed where it was, flat on the dressing. The leg was warm under her palm and shaking, and no blood was coming through. She left her hand there a moment longer than the knot needed.

Then she took it away.

His eyes were open, catching the last of the gray, and they were on her face.

Fever, she thought, and reached for the skin to make him drink.
