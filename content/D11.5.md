Her hands were in it before anyone had said a word.

They had got him under a second overhang, a short way along the gulch, its floor a step above the running channel, Benal on one side of him and the rock on the other, and laid him on his back with the leg out straight. The overhang ran into the gulch wall and made the back and the sides and most of a roof. Benal had stood stakes across the open front and dragged thorn against them. Where the stone gave out they had laid fern fronds over the gap, wide and waxy, weighted down with stones, and the fronds leaked. The rain came down on them in a steady hiss that found its way through in a dozen places and did not let up.

Teva peeled back the cloth she had pressed on him on the scree. It had soaked through long ago and came away dark and heavy, but under it the bleeding had slowed to an ooze. That was something. The torn leg of his legging was in her way, so she cut it off at the knee with her tooth-knife and laid it on the rock beside her, because they were going to need it.

Benal was holding a frond over her work with both hands, tilting it so the worst of the drip ran off past her elbow.

"Lower," she said. "You're dripping down my back."

"I'm dripping on everything," Benal said. "It's a structural problem. The frond is mostly holes." He lowered it.

The cut ran across the outside of his thigh above the knee, a hand's length, deepest in the middle where the edge of the block had gone in. Its lips were ragged and packed with grit, gray from the scree and darker where the blood had dried into it. She looked at the edges of it for a long time.

She knew how to do this. She had done it in their own nest, for her mother, with the Keepers coming and going. A bite first, a small one, hardly worth a look. Then the fever. Then the heat coming into the skin around the bite and the red creeping out from it a little farther every morning, for days. Teva had cleaned that wound every one of those mornings, and she still had the sound of the breathing at the end in her ears whenever she let things go quiet.

She did not let them go quiet now. She looked at the edges of Keo's wound for the color that had come into her mother's, and it was not there. It was not there yet, which was all that *yet* meant.

"Ben. The salves."

"In my pack. The side pocket, I think, unless they've migrated."

"I know where they are. Get them."

He wedged the frond against the rock as best he could and went. When he came back with the little pots in his big hands she had already started on the grit.

"Now the spikes," she said. "Lay them up on the ledge, on the rock and off the ground."

The bindings had been soaking since the front came through. Wet bindings went soft and slipped, and if they stayed wet long enough the archaea in them woke and the wood began to go.

"It isn't dry anywhere," Benal said. "There are only degrees of wet. I can find you the least wet place, roughly, if you tell me what you'd accept as a—"

"The least wet place."

He went. She heard the spikes go down on the stone, one and then the other, and then his feet coming back over the grit, and she did not look up.

"Now sit down."

"I'm all right."

"You've been breathing like that since the scree." She had heard it under the rain all the way along the gulch, careful and shallow, with a catch at the top of each breath, in the chest the snake had crushed. "Sit by the stakes, out of the drip, with your club. You've got first watch, and I need you awake for it. I'll take the last."

"I can take the first and the middle," Benal said. "I don't sleep well on stone anyway. I've been doing the arithmetic, and if you count the hours I actually sleep against the hours I lie here thinking about sleeping—"

"Three hours. Then you wake me."

"Three hours," he said. "Roughly."

He sat down by the stakes with the club across his knees, where the stone stayed dry. His breathing grew quieter, though the catch was still in it.

Keo's head moved on the rock. "Save the salve," he said. His voice came from a long way off. "We'll need it. Later. For worse."

She opened the first pot. The smell came up, bitter and green, the smell of every cut she had ever had at home.

"Ben's knife," Keo said. "Use his, yours is—"

"Ben hasn't got a knife."

"Oh." He thought about it, a long way off. "That's right."

She unstoppered a full skin of the rainwater they had caught off the lip of the first overhang at midday. She poured it into the cut slowly, and the grit ran out pink onto the stone. Not all of it came. She rinsed her fingers in the last of it. What would not wash out she picked out one piece at a time with her fingernails, bending close to the light that was left, and at every piece he went rigid under her hands.

"Careful," he groaned.

"Be quiet or be bait. Your choice."

Behind her Benal made a sound that started as a laugh, turned into a cough halfway, and ended with him holding his chest.

"Sorry," he said. "That was funny. I wasn't ready for it to be funny."

Keo shut his eyes. Teva went on picking. Her voice had come out flat and hard, and that was right. Her hands were the part of her that knew anything, and nobody was looking at her hands.

Halfway through she was cold.

The cold came all at once. She had been wet for hours, wet since the front, with nothing in her to make heat back with: nothing to eat since Noli's grounder last night, and no sleep worth the name for two nights. The rain on the back of her neck was warm as breath. That made no difference. Her hands began to shake.

Benal, by the stakes in his suit, was not shaking. Keo was, but Keo had a hole in his leg.

She held her hands flat over the wound and made them stop. They stopped, and then they started again.

Her suit lay beside her on the rock where she had let it fall, a gray wet heap. She had carried it all day, rolled on her pack, and the rain had soaked the roll. Yesterday on the fern it had filled with her own heat and had nowhere to put it, and she had climbed out of it to breathe. A day of rain had taken that heat back out of it. She picked it up. It was slick and limp and heavier than it should have been. She stepped into it as it was, wet over wet skin, drew it up over her shoulders and pressed the tab until it hissed shut.

The foam closed round her. It did not make her warm. Her hands still shook, so she braced them against the rock and went on. She did not look at her arm to see what the suit thought of itself. She would not have believed it.

"Better?" Benal said from the stakes.

"Hold the frond."

He got up, careful of his chest, and held it.

She spread the salve with two fingers into the cut and along both lips of it. Keo's breath went in through his teeth and stayed there. She cut the leg of his legging into strips with her knife, rinsed the grit of the scree out of them with the skin, laid a pad of it over the salve, and bound it with the next strip round and round the thigh, firm and flat. A drop came through the fronds onto the back of her hand. Another fell on the dressing. She moved her shoulder to take the next.

A suit would have taken that edge. She had thought it on the scree with her whole weight on the cut, and she thought it again now with the strip going round under her hands. The weave might have kept the edge from opening his thigh. She could not know what the fall would still have done to his ribs and his ankle. He had come out past the tangle in a tunic and leggings, the only one of them with nothing between him and anything, and she had looked at that on the first morning and let it go, and let it go every morning after. She pulled the strip tighter than it needed and made herself ease it off again.

"Tighter," Keo said. "No. Looser. You want it so the blood can still—"

"I know what I want."

"I'm only telling you—"

"I know."

She turned to his ankle. It had swollen inside the boot until the hide stood tight. She left the boot on, because it would never have gone back on again. She bound the ankle over it with cord, crossing it over the instep and back round the heel and over again, tight enough that he hissed.

"The rib," Keo said.

"There's nothing to do for the rib. Breathe shallow."

"I am breathing shallow."

"Shallower."

It was nearly dark. The gray had gone out of the rain beyond the stakes, and she could no longer see the pale strips against his skin. She did the rest by feel. He was shaking hard now, and she could feel that too, coming up through the leg into her hands.

"Teva," he said. "It's all right. We'll be—"

"Don't."

He stopped. She went on with the cloth.

She made the last turn. She found the end of the strip with her fingers and tucked it under. She tied it off and pulled the knot down hard against itself. Then her hand stayed where it was, flat on the dressing. The leg was warm under her palm and shaking, and no blood was coming through. She left her hand there a moment longer than the knot needed.

Then she took it away.

His eyes were open. She could just see them, catching the last of the gray at the mouth of the overhang, and they were on her face.

Fever, she thought, and reached for the skin to make him drink.
