Teva woke because the sun had found her face around the trunk while she slept. She blinked into it.

She felt the fork of the conifer, bark warm under her cheek. She smelled resin.

Beside her, Benal put his hand up against the light.

Keo yawned and squinted. She watched him take in the sun in the wrong place, the shadows gone long. He had said he would take the first turn.

So, so dangerous for all of them to sleep.

She looked down. Noli slept in the sun among tree roots, her snout tucked under one wing, feathers riffling in a breeze.

She forced herself to breathe. They were alive.

Keo opened his mouth, and she could see the apology coming.

"Well," Benal said first. He was looking up at the sky through the needles with his hand still over his eyes. "Apparently the Cretaceous has decided to let us live. Possibly out of pity."

Below them Noli stirred. Overhead, a bird lit on a branch directly above Benal's head and looked at him.

"Don't you dare," he told it.

Teva snorted. Keo pulled at the bees' wax in his hair.

They should go; most of the day's distance was still ahead.

"Since we're late," Benal said. "And since none of us seems to be going anywhere." He looked from one of them to the other. "Should we each say one true thing? A cheap one."

Keo groaned, but he half-shrugged and half-smiled, too.

"One each," Teva said. She was working resin off her fingers.

"I'll go first," Benal said. "Shall I?" When Teva and Keo waited, he took a breath. "This is the best day I've ever had."

It came out like a confession.

Keo reached over and knocked his knuckles on Benal's knee. "Ben," he said. "A snake nearly ate you yesterday."

"Nearly," Benal said. "That's the part I like."

Keo's lips twitched.

Teva looked at Benal. Fed, she thought, and alive, in a tree with the two of them. She believed him. Why did that make her sad and fiercely happy at the same time?

"So what are your not-best days like?" she said.

He thought about it. "Gardens in the morning," he said. "Weeding, mostly, or the groves. Then teaching math to the little kids. Some of them like me, I think." He turned his hand over. "Then home, and the board, in my corner, with my knees up. Mom asks if I've eaten, and I say I will. And then it's dark." He shrugged. "Mostly good. But today is better."

Teva realized she was adding her own contribution as the words came out.

"The Watch," she said. "Omya's star check." They both looked at her. "It's broken in me. I keep forgetting the seventh field. I go through from the beginning and I come to it, and it isn't there, every time, at the same place."

Benal had gone still. She thought he was going to ask her something, but he only looked at her a moment longer and then looked away. She felt a flash of gratitude.

Keo said, "We'll see it tonight, if it's clear."

"It's not that I can't see it," she said. "It's that I don't know what's supposed to be in it."

He did not have an answer to that.

They sat in silence.

After a few moments, Benal straightened and pointed. In the trunk just above Keo's head, where the tree had been hurt a long time ago and had wept resin into the wound, a hard golden bead of amber stuck. A fly was trapped inside, its wings out.

"Anywhere else," Benal said, "if that got buried right, it might still be here in the distant future when the founders are born. Possibly. One of them could dig it up and hold it to the light and look at that fly." He considered it. "Not here, though. Here it goes with the rest of us."

Keo looked at the fly. Teva looked at Keo.

"Your turn," she said.

"I don't have one true thing."

"C'mon. Something cheap."

Keo spread his hands, bee-bitten and split across the palms from the climb. He looked up and grinned. "Okay, here's one. We'll be fine."

Benal and Teva parroted it back at him in unison. Benal rolled his eyes.

"Teach us what you did to Ben's arm," Teva said. "Yesterday, on the leaves. When the suit was counting out the self destruct." She did not look at Benal. "If it's me next time, or him, and you're not there."

Keo looked at her for a long time. Then he moved along the branch to her and took her wrist, and turned it over to show the display.

"Watch."

He showed her where to touch, in what order, pausing to look over at Ben, who was copying.

"That's it?" Benal said.

"That's it."

"How do we know if we've got it right?"

"You don't. It doesn't count unless the suit thinks you're dead. You can't practice it for real. You find out if you got it right the one time it matters."

Benal bent to practice. Keo nodded at her, and she did it herself, first with mistakes, then repeating until she could do it with her eyes shut. But when she finished he didn't release her wrist. She looked at Keo's fingers, and then at him. At first Keo didn't react. Then he started, let go, and went back along the branch.

Teva sat with her arm on her knee and did not know what that had been.

After a while Benal said, looking at his arm, "If anyone needs to stop the suit, it'll be me. It's always going to be me." He held his arm out and turned it in the sun. "The webs this morning went for my face. Yesterday the snake. The bees will be next. I think it's possible that I'm delicious."

Keo looked him up and down. "You're definitely delicious," he said. "Look at the size of you. You'd feed a pack for a week."

"I'd feed a pack for a week," Benal agreed, gravely. "Possibly two, if they were careful."

"Delicious," Keo said. "Show me again, Delicious." So Benal ran through the motions on it one more time.

"You'll be dessert," Teva said, face carefully neutral.

Benal turned his head and glared at her. Keo laughed out loud. Teva went back to the resin on her fingers.
