She woke because the sun had come round the trunk while she slept and found her face. She opened her eyes into it and did not know for a moment where she was.

Then the rest came back: the fork of the conifer, the bark warm under her cheek, the smell of resin. Benal beside her, waking too, putting his hand up against the light. And Keo against the trunk with the spear across his knees, his eyes opening at the same moment as hers.

She watched it go across his face as he understood it: the sun in the wrong place, the shadows gone long and the other way, the afternoon half over. He had said he would take the first turn. No one had woken anyone, and nobody had watched.

Her body knew before she did. It went cold and tight under the suit the way it had been taught to, the way she had taught the yazhi to make theirs go, because one of them was always awake, always, and the one time nobody was awake was the time something came. She looked down through the branches. Noli lay in the sun at the foot of the tree, asleep on her side. The breeze moved in the needles. Nothing had come.

They were alive. The cold went out of her slowly, and what came after it she did not have a name for.

Keo opened his mouth, and she could see what was coming, the shape of it already on him, the account of what he should have done and how he would do it next time, an apology made out of instructions.

"Well," Benal said first. He was looking up at the sky through the needles with his hand still over his eyes. "Apparently the Cretaceous has decided to let us live. Possibly out of pity."

Keo shut his mouth.

Below them Noli turned over in the sun and stretched all four legs out stiff and went back to sleep. A bird came and sat on a branch above Benal's head and looked at him, and he looked back at it.

"Don't you dare," he told it.

That was all anyone said about it. Teva lay where she was and felt the sun on her face. She did not mind, and she could see that neither of them minded either, which was the strangest thing that had happened since the tangle.

They should go; they were late already, an hour at least, with most of the day's distance still in front of them. None of them moved.

"Since we're late," Benal said. "And since none of us seems to be going anywhere." He looked from one of them to the other. "Should we each say one true thing? A cheap one. Nothing that costs anything."

Keo groaned and put his head back against the trunk.

Teva did not say no. She was busy working resin off her fingers, where it had gotten into everything when they climbed up, and it would not come and she kept at it. Keo had bees' wax in his hair still and was picking it out in little gray crumbs. Benal had his left arm across his knees and was looking at his elbow, where the dark rim had crept a little further in.

"I'll go first," Benal said. "Since it was my idea. Possibly it was a bad idea." He took a breath, in halves, and let it out. "This is the best day I've ever had."

He said it flat, as if he were confessing to something, and then looked appalled at himself. Keo laughed out loud. Teva did not, but she looked at Benal, and he looked back at her and shrugged, carefully, so as not to hurt his chest. Fed, she thought, and alive, in a tree with the two of them, a day after the coils. She could see that it was true, and she could see that he could not have said it on any other day.

No one had asked her, and she found she was saying it anyway.

"The Watch," she said. "Omya's. The one she keeps." They both looked at her. "It's broken in me. There's a field I don't have. The seventh. I have the name of it and not the count. I go through from the beginning and I come to it, and it isn't there, every time, at the same place."

It was the longest thing she had said all day. She heard it go out of her and stop.

It was true, the truest thing she could say, nearly. It sat right next to the other thing, the real one, so close that she could feel the edge of it. She said nothing more and let it stand where it was.

Benal had gone still, the way he did before numbers. She thought he was going to ask her something, but he only looked at her a moment longer and then looked away and let it go. She was grateful to him in a way she would not have known how to say.

Keo said, "We'll see the seventh one tonight. If it's clear." As if it were a thing you could fix by looking.

"It's not that I can't see it," she said. "It's that I don't know what's supposed to be in it."

He did not have an answer to that, and did not try to make one.

It was Benal who saw the amber. It was in the trunk just above Keo's head, where the tree had been hurt a long time ago and had wept resin into the wound: a bead of it gone hard and clear and dark gold, the size of the end of her thumb. There was a fly inside, small, with its wings out, as if it had been caught in the middle of something.

They all knew what it was. It was one of the things the founders had known and handed down.

"If that gets buried right," Benal said, "it could still be there when the founders were born. Possibly. One of them could dig it up and hold it to the light and look at that fly." He considered it. "Which makes it the one thing out here that's better at leaving evidence than we are."

Keo laughed again. Teva looked at the fly with its wings out, in the middle of whatever it had been doing, and did not let herself think about what lasts and what does not, and looked at Keo instead.

"Your turn," she said.

"I don't have one."

"Everybody has one. A cheap one."

Keo spread his hands, split across the palms again from the climb and bee-bitten. He looked at them and then at her, and grinned the way he did when he was not going to give you anything. "We'll be fine," he said.

Benal and Teva said it back to him at the same moment, in his voice, the same weight on the same word. *We'll be fine.* Keo put his face in his hands.

Then Teva said, "Teach us what you did to Ben's arm."

He took his face out of his hands.

"Yesterday," she said. "On the leaves. When it was counting." She did not look at Benal. "If it's me next time, or him, and you're not there. Or you're there and your hands are worse than this."

Keo looked at her for a long time. Then he moved along the branch to her and took her forearm in his hands, the one with the display, and turned it over.

It was not much. He showed her where to put her fingers, what to do and in what order, and how hard, slowly. Then he made her do it back on her own arm, over and over, the last time with her eyes shut. His hands were sure, and he did not explain anything; he only showed, the way he had shown them how to step into the suits at the tangle, and his voice went quiet and level and certain, the voice he used on Noli. She got it wrong the first time and right the second. Then he went along the branch to Benal and did it all again, and Benal got it right the first time and then looked as if he wished he had not, as if it were a thing a person ought to have to work for.

"That's it?" Benal said.

"That's it."

"How do we know if we've got it right?"

Keo let go of his arm. "You don't," he said. "It doesn't count unless the suit thinks you're dead. You can't practice it for real. You find out if you got it right the one time it matters."

Nobody laughed at that.

Teva ran the motions through on her own arm while the other two were talking, under her breath, the way she ran the fields. Fingers here. Then this. Then hold. She thought of yesterday, Benal on his back in the leaves with his eyes open and nothing in them, and the even little pulses running up his arm, and Keo's hands already there, and she ran it through again. Her hands would have it by tonight. She did not know if that meant anything. You could have a thing perfectly and still not have it when it counted. She knew that better than either of them.

After a while Benal said, looking at his arm, "It's me, though, isn't it. Next time. It's always going to be me." He held his arm out and turned it in the sun. "The webs this morning went for my face. Yesterday the snake. The bees will be next. I think it's possible that I'm delicious."

Keo looked him up and down, all the way up. "You're definitely delicious," he said. "Look at the size of you. You'd feed a pack for a week."

"I'd feed a pack for a week," Benal agreed, gravely. "Possibly two, if they were careful."

"Delicious," Keo said, and reached across and took his arm again, and ran through the motions on it one more time, hold still, delicious, and Benal held still. Teva looked away from the two of them, because her mouth was doing something and she did not want them to see it.

"We should go," somebody said, later.

None of them moved for a while longer. The shadows went on getting longer and the breeze went on in the needles, and below them Noli woke and stretched and looked up at them as if to ask what was keeping them.

Then they climbed down, Keo first and Benal after, slowly, with his chest. Teva went last. Before she let go of the trunk she looked once at the amber, the fly with its wings out, and then she followed them down.
