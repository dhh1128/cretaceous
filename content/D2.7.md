The first person out pushed the hide back, and the kiva trailed behind them.

Benal smelled it as they came: wet hair, wet fiber cloth, breath and stale sweat, a whole chamber's worth shut in stone for an evening. It rolled over him where he stood against the rock beside the cleft. Under it, faint and acrid, was the pulp smell clinging to his legs from the basin.

He had come down late. Treading his family's assigned batch had taken until the light went, and left him weary. By the time he arrived they were shouting, and what came through the hide was noise with his father's voice inside it, loud and then louder. Then Elana went past him without seeing him, and the shouting died.

People continued to walk past him in ones and twos. Some gave him the look people gave Marek's son. *Poor thing.* He held still for it, as always.

His parents were slow to emerge. Normally he would have known them by the cadence in his father's step — brisk, with just a little double-rub as he rolled his heel. But tonight, a tardy pair of steps that came toward the hide were slow and uneven, and he did not know the owners until the hide moved. His mother had her arm through Marek's, and Marek walked as though the ground had gone soft under him.

Benal had never seen him like that. At home Marek argued in his Council voice, and let Benal finish every sentence, however long, before answering something else. Now his face was pinched in the last of the dusk, his mouth shut, his eyes on no one. He looked like a man who had shouted himself out in front of the whole village and lost.

*Do something real.*

The words came back before he could stop them, with a satisfaction he was ashamed of. So that was what doing something real got you. You shouted in front of everyone until your voice went, and they let you.

Marek saw him and stopped. He put out a hand, not quite to Benal's shoulder, and let it drop.

"Go home, Benal." His voice was a hoarse scrape. "You've been on your feet all afternoon. You'll be ill."

Even now, his father looked at him and saw a body that might break.

"I'm not ill," Benal said. "The batch is in. I trod it to the last count. You're the one with no voice left. You should possibly go home first."

Marek opened his mouth, and no voice came.

Lunet looked at Benal, tired.

"He was arguing for you in there," she said. "For all of us. That's what that was."

It was what a mother would say.

"He was arguing," Benal told the ground. "I think the whole village heard that much."

Then Lunet turned Marek toward the nests, and they went, her arm still through his.
