The first person out pushed the hide back, and the room came out ahead of them.

Benal smelled it before he saw anyone: wet hair, wet fiber cloth, breath and stale sweat, a whole room of people shut in stone for an evening. It rolled over him where he stood against the rock beside the cleft. Under it, faint and acrid, was the pulp still on his own legs from the basin. While he waited he had worked out how long a room that full and that shut would take to smother everyone in it, and decided the Council would adjourn first. It usually did.

He had come down late, slow with fatigue, after the batch. By then they were shouting, and what came through the hide was noise with his father's voice inside it, loud and then louder. Then Elana went past him without seeing him, and the shouting died. It did not come back.

People came out past him in ones and twos, quiet and tired and still damp. One or two looked at him the way people looked at Marek's son. Benal knew that look. It said *poor thing*. He held still for it, as he always did.

His parents came out late, after most of the room. He would have known his father's step on any platform in the Enclave. The step that came toward the hide now was slow and uneven, and he did not know it until the hide moved and they were there: his mother with her arm through Marek's, and Marek walking as though the ground had gone soft under him.

Benal had never seen him like that. At home Marek argued in the voice he used to the Council, and let Benal finish every sentence, however long it ran, before he answered something else. Now his face was gray in the last of the dusk, his mouth shut, his eyes on no one. He looked like a man who had shouted himself out in front of the whole Enclave and lost.

*Do something real.*

The words came back to Benal before he could stop them, with a satisfaction he was ashamed of. So that was what doing something real got you. You shouted in front of everyone until your voice went, and they let you.

Marek saw him. He stopped.

For a moment Benal thought he was going to walk on. Then his father put out a hand, not quite to Benal's shoulder, and let it drop.

"Go home, Benal." His voice was a hoarse scrape. "You've been on your feet all afternoon. You'll be ill."

Even now, his father looked at him and saw a body that might break.

"I'm not ill," Benal said. "The batch is in. I trod it to the last count, and then I came down." It came out like a report. "You're the one with no voice left. You should possibly go home first."

Marek opened his mouth to argue, and no voice came.

Lunet looked at Benal, her face tired and plain.

"He was arguing for you in there," she said. "For all of us. That's what that was."

It was what a mother would say.

"He was arguing," Benal said to the ground. "I heard that much. I think the whole Enclave heard that much."

Lunet waited a moment longer. Then she turned Marek toward the nests, and they went, slowly, her arm still through his.

Benal stayed against the rock beside the hide, in the dark that had come down while he wasn't watching, with the room still emptying behind him.
