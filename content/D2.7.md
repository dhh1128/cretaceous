The first person out pushed the hide back, and the room came out ahead of them.

Benal smelled it before he saw anyone: wet hair, wet fiber cloth, breath and stale sweat, a whole room shut in stone for an evening. It rolled over him where he stood against the rock beside the cleft. Under it, faint and acrid, was the pulp still on his legs from the basin. While he waited he had worked out how long a room that full would take to smother everyone in it, and decided the Council would adjourn first. It usually did.

He had come down late. The batch had taken until the light went, and left him slowed by fatigue. By then they were shouting, and what came through the hide was noise with his father's voice inside it, loud and then louder. Then Elana went past him without seeing him, and the shouting died.

People came out past him in ones and twos, still damp. One or two gave him the look people gave Marek's son. *Poor thing.* He held still for it, as he always did.

His parents came out late. He would have known his father's step on any platform in the Enclave. The step that came toward the hide now was slow and uneven, and he did not know it until the hide moved and they were there: his mother with her arm through Marek's, and Marek walking as though the ground had gone soft under him.

Benal had never seen him like that. At home Marek argued in his Council voice, and let Benal finish every sentence, however long, before answering something else. Now his face was gray in the last of the dusk, his mouth shut, his eyes on no one. He looked like a man who had shouted himself out in front of the whole Enclave and lost.

*Do something real.*

The words came back before he could stop them, with a satisfaction he was ashamed of. So that was what doing something real got you. You shouted in front of everyone until your voice went, and they let you.

Marek saw him and stopped. He put out a hand, not quite to Benal's shoulder, and let it drop.

"Go home, Benal." His voice was a hoarse scrape. "You've been on your feet all afternoon. You'll be ill."

Even now, his father looked at him and saw a body that might break.

"I'm not ill," Benal said. "The batch is in. I trod it to the last count. You're the one with no voice left. You should possibly go home first."

Marek opened his mouth, and no voice came.

Lunet looked at Benal, tired.

"He was arguing for you in there," she said. "For all of us. That's what that was."

It was what a mother would say.

"He was arguing," Benal told the ground. "I think the whole Enclave heard that much."

Then Lunet turned Marek toward the nests, and they went, her arm still through his.

Benal stayed against the rock beside the hide, in a dark that had come down while he wasn't watching.
