Noli stopped well short of the scent nozzles, where she always stopped.

Keo felt it before he saw it, a drag on the bond like a cord pulled taut, and when he looked down she had set her feet. Ahead of them the tangle went up into the low sun, fifteen meters <!-- @WF-tangle.height: 15m --> of felled trunks woven through with living thorn and vine, and along its foot the nozzles were weeping amber. She would not go one step nearer. On her own, she never had.

He had not slept much. It was behind his eyes and in the slowness of his hands, and he had come out anyway, because this was the hour he came out.

In daylight she was the color of mud, until she moved and the feathers took the light like oil on water. She held her sickle claws clear of the ground, her tail rigid behind her, and every feather on her body had lifted a little away from the skin. Her raised feathers were her complaint. They made her look twice her size.

He pushed calm at her.

There was no word in it; you could not push a word. He pushed the warmth of a rock in the sun, and the picture of her standing still, exactly where she was, with nothing behind her and nothing coming, and then he took a step. She followed, and he pushed it again. What came back down the bond was fear. It always came the same way, rough and wordless, more a weight than a thought, and he knew what it was without having to look at her. The pressure began behind his eyes.

Keo knew the Barrier's smell as well as his own nest's. It was musk, a big tyrannosaur's, laid over something gone rotten, so thick at the nozzles it was nearly a taste, and to him it meant one thing: here was where home stopped. To Noli it meant a rex standing close by, hurt and angry. Knowing why she was afraid didn't help.

At ten meters she stopped again, and this time no amount of calm would move her.

No animal went out past the tangle with an Explorer party unless it could walk by the Barrier on its own. His father had told him so a year ago, in one sentence, and every dawn since then Keo had brought her a step closer than the dawn before. Today it would be inside the line.

So he pinned her, for a minute. It was a different thing from the bond, heavier and closer and stupid, a weight laid down over everything in her that wanted to run. It cost him at once. She came forward on stiff legs, three steps and then four, and then she was inside the line, where nothing would have kept her but him.

Along the tangle to his left, the rebuild crew was already out, four of them dragging the night's thorn hurdle out of the work gap on its poles. He had watched them haul it shut every evening for a week. One of them lifted a hand to him. Keo didn't look away from Noli. Behind him, well back on the buffer, an uneven step stopped in the grass, and he knew whose it was.

"Stay there," he said, without turning. "She's inside the line."

"I am there," said Topo. "I've been there since before you pinned her." He waved to the crew, for both of them.

Noli stood inside the line, her feathers still raised. He held her there and the pressure climbed, a slow tightening under the bone, and every breath she spent inside the line cost him. All of her steadiness was on loan.

And she was not even watching the tangle.

A cycad cone had rolled down into the grass a body length to her right, the size of a fist and brown and doing nothing at all, and she had one eye on it. The feathers on that side of her stood straight up. This close to what smelled like a wounded rex, with every instinct she owned screaming at her to run, she had found the cone, judged it, and decided it was the danger here.

Keo pushed *good-pack-good-food* at her, the full belly and the others close, which was as near as he could get to telling her she was a fool. She kept watching the cone.

He wasn't cruel, so he let go before Noli truly panicked.

The pin went out of him all at once, and the pressure popped and left the ache behind, pounding now instead of pressing. Beneath the ache, the old bond stayed where it had been for years, light as a hand on her back, costing him nothing he could feel. He had the pouch of crawlers open in his hand.

Noli went straight past it.

She bolted out of the line in three bounds, and when the smell was only a smell she still did not slow down. She was going home, across the buffer toward the ring, and he knew what home took her past first. So did she.

There was one rule he had ever made stick with her. She left the crawler beds alone, the long troughs of rotting wood at the edge of the ring where the grubs everyone roasted grew fat. She kept that rule perfectly, every day, except in the moment after he dropped a pin, when his head was pounding and he could not push anything at her worth having.

He went after her. It was his way home too. He was not halfway across the buffer when it came down the bond, warm and entirely unashamed, and he knew without seeing it which trough she had her head in. Keo laughed before he could stop himself. It made his head worse.

Topo caught him up short of the beds, and Keo slowed without thinking about it. Topo's bad ankle made him swing that foot a little wide, and he did not ask anybody to wait.

"Four steps," Topo said. "Inside. I counted."

"Four." Keo could not keep it out of his voice.

"You're walking like your head hurts."

"I'm walking slow so you can keep up."

"I'm walking fast so you can," said Topo.

Ahead of them the Enclave was waking, faint beyond the groves, somebody calling a name and somebody answering, and Topo's voice dropped. "Everybody's saying the ones at the opening just stood there," he said. "When Elana brought her in. They all just stood and watched."

"Somebody should have done something."

"Like what?"

Keo opened his mouth and found nothing to put in it. "Something," he said.

"Teva was in there. It was Omya at the tray."

He had not known that. He had seen Teva at the returning, at the front beside Elana with her arms folded hard across her ribs, and had thought it was only the returning that made her stand like that.

"Is she all right?"

"Ask her."

"I'm not going to ask her."

Topo looked at him. "You asked me."

Keo let that go by. They walked a while, slower than Topo needed.

"Are you going tonight?" Topo said.

"Everyone's going."

"Ben won't get there till it's half over. He'll be up in that corner doing his —"

"Algebra," said Keo, and Topo grinned. Somebody ought to go up Ben's ladder and fetch him. Nobody would, and Ben would come in late and stand at the back with his shoulders up, trying to take up less of the room than there was of him.

Where the buffer met the ring, Topo went off toward the nests, swinging the bad foot wide. Keo watched him go.

Someone was going to have to do something. He could feel how strong his legs were under him.

At the edge of the ring Noli came out from the beds to meet him, rotten wood in her feathers, and leaned her whole weight against his leg the way she had since she was small. He was still holding the crawlers she had walked past.

He put his hand on her back. "We'll be fine," he whispered.
