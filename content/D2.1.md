Noli stopped well short of the nozzles, where she always stopped.

Keo felt it before he saw it, a drag on the bond like a cord pulled taut, and when he looked down she had set her feet. Ahead of them the tangle went up into the low sun, fifteen meters <!-- @WF-tangle.height: 15m --> of felled trunks woven through with living vine, and along its foot the nozzles were weeping amber the way they did every morning. She would not go one step nearer. On her own, she never had.

He had not slept much. It was behind his eyes and in the slowness of his hands, and he had come out anyway, because this was the hour he came out.

In daylight she was the color of mud, until she moved and the feathers took the light like oil on water. She held her sickle claws clear of the ground, her tail rigid behind her, and every feather on her body had lifted a little away from the skin. Her raised feathers were her complaint. They made her look twice her size.

He pushed calm at her.

There was no word in it; you could not push a word. He pushed the warmth of a rock in the sun, and the picture of her standing still, exactly where she was, with nothing behind her and nothing coming, and then he took a step. She followed, and he pushed it again. What came back down the bond was fear. It always came the same way, rough and wordless, more a weight than a thought, and he knew what it was without having to look at her. The pressure began behind his eyes.

Keo knew the Barrier's smell as well as his own nest's. It was musk, a big tyrannosaur's, laid over something gone rotten, so thick at the nozzles it was nearly a taste, and to him it meant one thing: here was where home stopped. To Noli, he was fairly sure, it meant a rex standing close by, hurt and angry. Knowing why she was afraid didn't help.

At ten meters she stopped again, and this time no amount of calm would move her.

So he pinned her. It was a different thing from the bond, heavier and closer and stupid, a weight laid down over everything in her that wanted to run, and it cost him at once. She came forward on stiff legs, three steps and then four, and then she was inside the line, where nothing would have kept her but him.

Along the tangle to his left, the rebuild crew was already out, four of them dragging the night's thorn hurdle out of the work gap on its poles. He had watched them haul it shut every evening for a week. One of them lifted a hand to him. Keo did not take his eyes off Noli.

Noli stood inside the line for a minute, her feathers still raised. He held her there and the pressure climbed, a slow tightening under the bone, and every breath she spent inside the line was his to pay for. All of her steadiness was on loan.

And she was not even watching the tangle.

A cycad cone had rolled down into the grass a body length to her right, the size of a fist and brown and doing nothing at all, and she had one eye on it. The feathers on that side of her stood straight up. This close to what smelled like a wounded rex, with every instinct she owned screaming at her to run, she had found the cone, judged it, and decided it was the danger here.

Keo pushed *good-pack-good-food* at her, the full belly and the others close, which was as near as he could get to telling her she was a fool. She kept her eye on the cone.

He held her until the ache behind his eyes was a pulse he could count. Then he let go.

The pin went out of him all at once, and the pressure popped and left the ache behind, the way it always did, pounding now instead of pressing. Beneath the ache, the old bond stayed where it had been for years, light as a hand on her back, costing him nothing he could feel. Noli bolted out of the line in three bounds and did not slow down until the smell was only a smell.

Then she started to dig.

There was one rule he had ever made stick with her. She did not dig in the buffer, because the herd grazed it, and a hole could turn a hadrosaur's foot, and four tonnes of animal that went lame was four tonnes nobody could use. She knew the rule. She kept it perfectly, every day, except in the moment after he dropped a pin, when his head was pounding and he could not push anything at her worth having.

Dirt flew out behind her in a steady fan. She did not look back at him once.

"Noli," he said.

The dirt kept coming. What came back down the bond was satisfaction, warm and entirely unashamed, and Keo laughed before he could stop himself, which made his head worse.

He let her dig. He would fill it in.

Behind him the Enclave was waking, faint across the buffer and the groves, somebody calling a name and somebody answering, and the sound brought yesterday back, the way it had come back every time he stopped moving. Alira was dead. The Keepers could not make the rust-red any more, and the way people were telling it, the grown men and women in that room had stood there and watched her die.

Someone was going to have to do something. He could feel how strong his legs were under him.

Noli came back when she was finished, filthy to the shoulders, and leaned her whole weight against his leg the way she had since she was small.

He put his hand on her back. "We'll be fine," he told her.
