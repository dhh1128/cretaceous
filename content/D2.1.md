Noli stopped well short of the scent nozzles, where she always stopped.

Keo felt it before he saw it, a drag on the bond like a cord pulled taut. She had set her feet. Ahead the tangle went up into the low sun, fifteen meters <!-- @WF-tangle.height: 15m --> of felled trunks woven through with living thorn and vine, and along its foot the nozzles wept amber. She would not go a step nearer on her own.

He had slept badly. It was behind his eyes and in the slowness of his hands.

In daylight she was the color of mud, until she moved and the feathers took the light like oil on water. She held her sickle claws clear of the ground, her tail rigid, and every feather had lifted a little away from her skin. It made her look twice her size.

He pushed calm at her.

There were no words in it. He pushed the warmth of a rock in the sun, and a picture of her standing still with nothing behind her and nothing coming, and took a step. She followed. He pushed again. What came back down the bond was fear, rough and wordless, more weight than thought. The pressure began behind his eyes.

Keo knew the Barrier's smell as well as his own nest's: a big tyrannosaur's musk laid over something gone rotten, so thick at the nozzles it was nearly a taste. To him it meant home stopped here. To Noli it meant a rex standing close by, hurt and angry.

At ten meters she balked again, and no amount of calm would move her.

No animal went out past the tangle with an Explorer party unless it could walk by the Barrier on its own. His father had told him so a year ago, in one sentence, and every dawn since, Keo had brought her a step closer. Today she would stand inside the line.

So he pinned her, for a minute. It was a different thing from the bond, heavier and closer and stupid, a weight laid down over everything in her that wanted to run. She came forward on stiff legs, three steps and then four, and she was inside the line.

Along the tangle to his left, four of the rebuild crew were dragging the night's thorn hurdle out of the work gap on its poles. He had watched them haul it shut every evening for a week. One lifted a hand to him. Keo kept his eyes on Noli. Behind him, well back on the buffer, an uneven step had stopped in the ferns some while back, and he knew whose it was.

"Stay there," he said, without turning. "She's inside the line."

"I am there," said Topo. "Been there since before you pinned her." He waved to the crew, for both of them.

Noli stood there, feathers raised. He held her there and the pressure climbed, a slow tightening under the bone, and every breath she spent inside the line cost him. All of her steadiness was on loan.

And she was not even watching the tangle.

A cycad cone had rolled into the ferns a body length to her right, the size of a fist and doing nothing at all, and she had one eye on it. The feathers on that side of her stood straight up. This close to what smelled like a wounded rex, she had found the cone, judged it, and decided it was the danger here.

Keo pushed *good-pack-good-food* at her, the full belly and the others close, which was as near as he could get to telling her she was a fool. She kept watching the cone.

He wasn't cruel, so he let go before Noli truly panicked.

The pressure popped and left the ache, pounding now instead of pressing. Under it the old bond stayed, light as a hand on her back, costing him nothing he could feel. He had the pouch of crawlers open in his hand.

Noli went straight past it.

She bolted out of the line in three bounds and kept going, across the buffer toward the ring, and he knew what home took her past first. So did she.

He had only ever made one rule stick with her: leave the crawler beds alone, the long troughs of rotting wood at the edge of the ring where the grubs everyone roasted grew fat. She kept it perfectly, except in the moment after he dropped a pin, when his head was pounding and he could push nothing at her worth having.

He went after her; it was his way home too. He was not halfway across the buffer when it came down the bond, warm and entirely unashamed, and he knew which trough she had her head in. Keo laughed before he could stop himself. It made his head worse.

Topo caught up with him short of the beds, and Keo slowed without thinking about it. Topo's bad ankle swung that foot a little wide, and he did not ask anybody to wait.

"Four steps," Topo said. "Inside. I counted."

"Four." Keo couldn't keep it out of his voice.

"You're walking like your head hurts."

"I'm walking slow so you can keep up."

"I'm walking fast so you can," said Topo.

Ahead the Enclave was waking beyond the groves, somebody calling a name and somebody answering. Topo's voice dropped. "Everybody's saying the ones at the opening just stood there. When Elana brought her in. Just stood and watched."

"Somebody should've done something."

"Like what?"

Keo opened his mouth and found nothing to put in it. "Something," he said.

"Teva was in there. It was Omya at the tray."

He hadn't known. He had seen Teva at the returning, at the front beside Elana with her arms folded hard across her ribs, and had thought it was only the returning.

"Is she okay?"

"Ask her."

"I'm not going to ask her."

Topo looked at him. "You asked me."

Keo let that go by.

"You going tonight?" Topo said.

"Everyone's going."

"Ben won't get there till it's half over. He'll be up in that corner doing his —"

"Algebra," said Keo, and Topo grinned. Somebody ought to go up Ben's ladder and fetch him. Nobody would, and Ben would come in late and stand at the back with his shoulders up, trying to take up less room than there was of him.

Where the buffer met the ring, Topo went off toward the nests.

Someone was going to have to do something. He could feel how strong his legs were under him.

At the edge of the ring Noli came out from the beds to meet him, rotten wood in her feathers, and leaned her whole weight against his leg the way she had since she was small. He was still holding the crawlers she had walked past.

He put his hand on her back. "We'll be fine," he whispered.
