When the cloud began to thin they went along the shore at Keo's pace. Teva had his arm over her shoulders, and every time he hopped his weight came down through it onto her neck and jolted the ache behind her eyes. The wind shoved at her back. Benal went a step ahead, picking the way over the shell.

Across the flood on her left the crown stood up out of the low ground. On this side it rose in one long easy slope, bare to its brow. From down here the brow hid whatever was on top.

On her right was the sea.

All yesterday it had been under the boat. Now it lay out past the reef to the edge of the sky, heaving, brown-green with whatever the storm had torn up off the bottom, and white wherever it reached the reef. At home "sea" was a word in the Keepers' stories, the thing the founders had come over. The stories had not said it was brown. They had not said it never stopped moving.

It had taken almost all of the boat.

A building stood where the shore met the low ground, close now: a long arched roof, salt-white, one end fallen in. It was as big as several nests back home. Stone with no joins in it, shell and grit set hard together. Concrete, probably. She had been taught the word as a small child, along with "chert" and "glass," in a catechism of things that never go away and must never be made, and she had never seen any. Red-brown stains ran down the wall from the doorway to the shell.

This was the place. She had brought them to it, all the way from the Enclave.

They walked straight in. Where the doors had been, a crust lay along the sill and broke under her boots. It was dry and red, and it smelled like a cut hand.

Benal crouched and rubbed a flake of it between finger and thumb. "This was metal, I think. Has to be rust. Keo, isn't that what you said the wrappings would make of the machete, if they ever woke?"

"Metal to rust and the rest to sludge," Keo said. "Mom says it every time she does the balm."

Teva looked at the red on Benal's fingers, the color of the culture she had watched dimming in her grandmother's tray. "The rust-red," she said. "I never once thought where the name came from."

The enclosure was sunk into the shore, and below the sill its floor was gone under swirling water. Long gray-white backs broke the surface in rows, crusted with shell, like turtles come up to breathe, with lumps of rust between. Along one wall, above the water, ran a high walkway, with a concrete stair up to it. At the seaward end the roof was open to the sky, and daylight came in over a heap of fallen concrete.

Birds went up off the walkway, all at once.

The clatter of their wings struck the roof and came back down, and came back again. The surge slapped on the crusted backs, and every slap returned off the curve of the roof. The place sounded bigger than it was, with background noise only highlighting the emptiness.

"Where is everything?" Keo said.

The roof gave his last word back, a moment late, from somewhere above the water. Keo turned toward it, quick, as though somebody had answered him. Then he turned back. Just an echo, sharper than the one he sometimes heard in the kiva.

"Those were the machines," Benal said, looking at the rows in the water. "I bet this was their system for making water out of the sea. It's in the records."

"Then where did they keep what they came for?" Teva said.

"Not in a water building."

Keo looked up at the walkway. "What's up there?"

"We'll look," Teva said.

The stair's rail had rusted to stubs, worn to points. Teva went up first. Keo came behind her on his hands and his good knee, the bad foot held clear, and Benal behind him. At the top the stink of the birds caught in her throat until her eyes ran. Under it was old fish, and under that only the sea.

Their mess lay white and thick under the roof nearest the broken end, where they roosted. The inner end, against the wall, was clean.

"Sit there," she said.

"I can look."

"Sit."

He sat, got the ankle up on the concrete, and leaned his head back on the wall.

Then she looked. Under the mess the walkway was bare concrete, and along its edge stubs stood up in pairs and rows. There were no nests, so the birds only slept here. No eggs. Nothing to eat. Nothing that would burn, and nothing anybody had written on.

Her hand had gone to the pocket on the chest of her suit. Through the film she could feel the flower inside, pressed flat, where it had been since the day Keo picked it. She took the hand away without opening it.

"Anything?" Keo said.

"No."

Benal was crouched over the nearest pair of stubs. "Something stood on these. Fixed down, all along. They took it with them, or it rusted away like the doors."

She stood at the edge of the walkway with the water knocking under her.

Her head hurt. It had hurt behind the eyes since the mash. She had felt north every day of her life. At the reef, where it was the one thing they needed of her, she could not hold it, because she would not wait a night for the mash.

She had eaten it first, so that if she was wrong it would only be her.

It had not only been her.

They went down, Keo on his hands again, out over the sill and round the landward end of the building. Past it the ground dropped away into flood that ran all the way to the foot of the crown's slope, with scrub standing up out of it here and there.

"They'll have come ashore on this side," Teva said. "This side's the easy one. They went up from here, across that hill."

"Can we?" Keo said.

"Not today." Benal was watching the water. "The swell's holding it in. When the swell drops it should go off."

They stood at the edge above it. None of them went down.

"We'll find another way up," Keo said.

"We'll be—" Teva said.

It came out flat, the old line, and halfway through it her voice went.

Keo looked at her, then away at the crown. Benal kept his eyes on the flood.

They sat out the afternoon on the shore, waiting for the flood to go down. The cloud broke, and the wind dropped a little and kept on.

"Is it going down?" he said.

"Not yet," Benal said. "The reef's still as hard as it was this morning. I can hear it."

"How long?"

"Until the sea drops. Tonight, I think. Possibly not."

She sat with her arms round her knees, and after a while she noticed that nothing had bitten her face or her hands all day. She could not remember the last day like it, at home or on the walk. The wind kept the fliers down. Under the surf and the wind there was no insect wall, and she kept listening for it.

The light went yellow and began to fade.

Benal stood up. "We need a roof before night," he said. "Anywhere with a roof. And off the ground."

He looked back at the water building, at the walkway under the part of the roof that still stood.

The wind came over the shore onto their soaked backs. The air wasn't cold, but Teva shivered. There was driftwood all along the shore, and nothing to light it with.

The kindler had been in the fieldpack.
