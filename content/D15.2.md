When the cloud began to thin they went along the shore at Keo's pace. Teva had his arm over her shoulders, and every time he hopped she felt his weight come down through it onto her neck and jolt the ache behind her eyes. The wind shoved at her back. Benal went a step ahead, picking the way over the shell.

Across the flood on her left the crown stood up out of the low ground. On this side it rose in one long easy slope, bare all the way to its brow, and from down here the brow hid whatever was on top.

On her right was the sea.

All yesterday it had been under the boat. Now it lay out past the reef to the edge of the sky, heaving, brown-green with whatever the storm had torn up off the bottom, and wherever it reached the reef it went white. At home "sea" was a word in the Keepers' stories, the thing the founders had come over. The stories had not said it was brown. They had not said it never stopped moving, anywhere, as far as she could see.

It had taken almost all of the boat.

A building stood where the shore met the low ground, close now: a long arched roof, salt-white, with one end of it fallen in. She had never seen anything like it. It was as big as several nests back home. Stone with no joins in it, shell and grit set hard together. That would probably be "concrete". She had been taught the word as a small child, along with "chert" and "steel" -- part of a catechism of things that never go away and must never be made, and she had never seen any. Red-brown stains ran down the wall from the doorway to the shell.

This was the place. She had brought them to it, all the way from the Enclave.

They walked straight in. Where the doors had been, a crust lay along the sill and broke under her boots. It was dry and red, and it smelled like a cut hand.

Benal crouched and rubbed a flake of it between his finger and thumb. "This was metal, I think. It has to be rust. Keo, isn't that what you said the wrappings would make of the machete, if they ever woke?"

"Metal to rust and the rest to sludge," Keo said. "My mother says it every time she does the balm."

Teva looked at the red on Benal's fingers. It was the color of the culture she had watched dimming in her grandmother's tray. "That's the rust-red," she said. "I never once thought where the name came from."

The enclosure was sunk into the shore, and below the sill its floor was gone under water swirling round lumps of mineral that stood up out of it in rows. Along one wall, above the water, ran a high walkway, with a concrete stair up to it. At the seaward end the roof was open to the sky, and the daylight came in there over a heap of fallen concrete.

Birds went up off the walkway, all at once.

The clatter of their wings struck the roof and came back down, and came back again. The surge slapped on the lumps, and every slap returned off the curve of the roof. The place sounded bigger than it was, with background noise only highlighting the emptiness.

"Where is everything?" Keo said.

The roof gave his last word back, a moment late, from somewhere above the water. Keo turned toward it, quick, as though somebody had answered him. Then he turned back. Just an echo, more distinct than the one he heard sometimes in the kiva. 

"Those were the machines," Benal said. He was looking at the lumps in the water. "I bet this was their system for making water out of the sea. It's in the records."

"Then where did they keep what they came for?" Teva said.

"Not in a water building."

Keo looked up at the walkway. "What's up there?"

"We'll look," Teva said.

The stair had had a rail once. Its stubs stood out of the concrete at the edge of every few steps, worn to points. Teva went up first. Keo came behind her on his hands and his good knee, with the bad foot held clear, and Benal behind him. At the top the stink of the birds met her and caught in her throat, sharp, until her eyes ran. Under it was old fish, and under that only the sea.

The walkway's floor was white with their mess under the roof nearest the broken end, where the birds roosted, thick where no rain had reached it in years. Out at the broken end itself the rain had washed it bare, and at the inner end against the wall it was clean.

"Sit there," she said.

"I can look."

"Sit."

He sat and got the ankle up on the concrete in front of him. He leaned his head back on the wall.

Then she looked. The walkway was bare concrete under the mess, and along its edge, all the way down, stubs stood up out of it in pairs and rows where something had been fixed. There were no nests, so the birds only slept here, and there were no eggs. There was nothing to eat. There was nothing that would burn, and nothing anybody had written on.

Her hand had gone to the pocket on the chest of her suit. The zip was still closed. Through the film she could feel the flower inside, pressed flat, where it had been since the day Keo picked it. She took the hand away without opening it.

"Anything?" Keo said.

"No."

Benal was crouched over the nearest pair of stubs. "Something stood on these. Fixed down, all along. They took it with them, or it rusted away like the doors."

She stood at the edge of the walkway with the water knocking under her.

Her head hurt. It had hurt since the mash, behind the eyes. She had felt north every day of her life. At the reef, where it was the one thing they needed of her, she could not hold it, because she would not wait a night for the mash.

She had eaten it first, so that if she was wrong it would only be her.

It had not only been her.

They went down, Keo on his hands again, out over the crust on the sill and round the landward end of the building. Past it the ground dropped away, and the flood began there and ran all the way to the foot of the crown's slope, with scrub standing up out of it here and there.

"They'll have come ashore on this side," Teva said. "This side's the easy one. They went up from here, across that hill."

"Can we?" Keo said.

"Not today." Benal was watching the water. "The swell's holding it in. When the swell drops it should go off."

They stood on the edge of the shore above it. None of them went down.

"We'll find another way up," Keo said.

"We'll be—" Teva said.

It came out flat, the old line, and halfway through it her voice went.

Keo looked at her, and then away, at the crown. Benal kept his eyes on the flood.

They sat out the afternoon on the shore, waiting for the flood to go down. It did not. The cloud thinned and broke, and the wind dropped a little and kept on. Keo sat with the bad leg out in front of him and his hands round the knee of the other.

"Is it going down?" he said.

"Not yet," Benal said. "The reef's still as hard as it was this morning. I can hear it."

"How long?"

"Until the sea drops. Tonight, I think. Possibly not."

She sat with her arms round her knees, and after a while she noticed that nothing had bitten her face or her hands all day. She tried to remember the last day like it, and there had not been one, not at home and not on the walk. The wind kept the fliers down. Under the surf and the wind there was no insect wall to hear at all, and she kept listening for it.

The light went yellow under the cloud and then began to fade.

Benal stood up. "We need a roof before night," he said. "Anywhere with a roof. And off the ground."

He looked back at the water building, at the walkway under the part of the roof that still stood.

The wind came over the shore onto their soaked backs, and although the air wasn't cold, Teva shivered. There was driftwood all along the shore, and nothing to light it with.

The kindler had been in the fieldpack.
