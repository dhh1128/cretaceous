Keo woke shaking.

It was dark. Water dripped close to his ear, and the rain hissed on the fronds. It took him a moment to have it: the overhang, the rock at his back, his leg, which smelled of salve. He was hot all through, then cold, and his teeth chattered.

He blinked, and the sound of rain disappeared. The drip by his ear had stopped too. His skin prickled, and the hairs lifted on his arms and the back of his neck, the way they did when a storm was about to break. There was a mark in front of his open eyes, bright and sharp-edged against the dark: a ring with a gap in it, and a line running from one side of the gap straight across to the far side. Under the salve there was a smell like the air after lightning, and salt.

He blinked again and it was gone, and the rain was hissing on the fronds.

Fever, he thought.

At the mouth of the overhang, against the faint gray of the rain, sat a shape, all knees and shoulders, that looked as though the next gust would fold it up. After a moment he knew it was Benal, sitting up with the razor-club across his knees and his face turned out toward the gulch.

"Ben."

"Here."

"My watch. Wake me for the last one."

"Teva has the last one. With that ankle you'd be as much use on watch as one of the stakes." He seemed to think about it. "Less, possibly. The stakes don't shake."

"I'm not—"

"Go to sleep. I have this."

The rain went on. Keo had nearly gone when Benal said, "Probably."

Then Benal was coming back under the fronds on his knees, one hand pressed flat to his chest, with a skin.

"Drink first."

"Not thirsty."

Benal got a hand under Keo's head and lifted it. "You lost a lot of blood on the scree, and the fever's sweating you dry. Drink."

He held the skin to Keo's mouth. Keo swallowed, and choked, and swallowed again, and Benal waited for a third before he took it away. He laid Keo's head down and went back on his knees to the stakes.

Benal began to hum. The sound was very low, just audible under the rush of water. Keo recognized his mother's song, the one with the strange words, that he had bellowed across the savanna yesterday, and he realized Ben was aiming it at him, just at the limits of his hearing but not enough to carry back to Teva, asleep at the back of the overhang.

A feeling rose in him then, from somewhere under the shaking, so strong it hurt in his throat. It was for Ben, whom he had been kindly sorry for all his life. For Ben floating terrified in the river, Ben awake in the rain with a chest that would not let him breathe right, Ben holding a skin to his mouth, Ben humming his mother's song.

"Ben."

"Still here."

There was nothing he could say that would do it justice.

The song went around again to the beginning. He was going under, and he let the fever take him. The last he had was the shape at the mouth of the overhang, thin against the gray, still sitting up, still humming.
