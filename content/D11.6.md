He came up out of it shaking.

For a while he did not know where he was. There was rock over him, close. There was a sound that went on and on like a long breath let out, and under it the bitter smell of salve on his leg. The dressed leg lay heavy and useless. Then it was his, and it hurt, and with the hurt came the clay going out from under him.

He was hot, and a moment later he was cold, with his teeth going until he clamped them.

Then, for one breath, there was something in front of him that was not the dark. A mark, clean-edged and gleaming, hanging on nothing, a shape he had never seen and could not have drawn. And a smell, sharp, of ozone, and salt.

Then it was gone.

Fever, he thought.

At the mouth of the overhang, against the faint gray of the rain beyond the stakes, there was a shape sitting up. Long, all knees and elbows, with something laid across its lap. For a moment Keo could not make it into anyone. Then it moved its head, and it was Benal, with the razor-club across his knees, sitting very straight with his back against the rock.

"Ben," Keo said. It came out thick. "Is it my watch?"

"It is not." Benal's voice was low. "You've been excused from watches. Teva decided, and I agreed with her, and you were unconscious, so I'd call it a majority. Roughly."

"I can take it."

"You can't take your own boot off. Go to sleep. I have this." A pause, while he thought about it. "Probably."

He got up onto his knees, slowly, one hand flat against his chest, and came over with the skin. He slid his other hand under Keo's head and lifted it, and held the skin to his mouth.

"Drink."

Keo drank. The water was rain and tasted of the hide. When he stopped, Benal waited and tipped the skin again, until Keo had drunk more than he wanted.

"Teva said you drink every time you wake," Benal said. "I'm under orders. It's very restful, being under orders. I see why you all like it."

He eased Keo's head back onto the rock. Then he went back to his place at the mouth of the overhang and folded himself up again with the club across his knees.

Keo lay and listened to the rain on the fronds. Somewhere behind him Teva was breathing slow and even, asleep at last.

After a while there was another sound under the rain. Benal was humming. It was very low, hardly more than a breath, pitched so it would not carry past the stakes or wake her. Keo knew the tune before he knew he was listening. It was Liaso's song, the long one, the one Liaso hummed on the watchers' platform at home. Keo had sung all of it on the savanna yesterday, with breath to spare, and Benal had come in on the chorus a breath late.

Benal was not late now. He had the whole of it.

It came up in Keo then, out of nowhere, so strong it hurt worse than the leg: for Ben. For the boy he had carried loads for since they were small, and walked slower for, and been sorry for. Ben, sitting up in the dark with a sore chest and a club he could barely swing, keeping the watch that had always been Keo's, keeping him.

He wanted to say so. He could not find the front of the sentence. It went around in him and had nowhere to go.

The rain went on. The fever came up over him like water, warm, and he let it.

The last thing was Benal at the mouth of the overhang, still sitting up, still humming.
