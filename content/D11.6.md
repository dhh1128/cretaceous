He came up out of it shaking.

There was rock over him, close, and a sound that went on and on like a long breath let out, and the bitter smell of salve. The leg lay heavy. Then it was his, and it hurt, and with the hurt came the clay going out from under him.

He was hot, then cold, his teeth going until he clamped them.

Then, for one breath, there was something in front of him that was not the dark. A mark, clean-edged and gleaming, hanging on nothing, a shape he had never seen and could not have drawn. And a smell, sharp, of ozone, and salt.

Then it was gone.

Fever, he thought.

At the mouth of the overhang, against the faint gray of the rain, a shape sat up, all knees and elbows. Then it moved its head, and it was Benal, with the razor-club across his knees, sitting very straight against the rock.

"Ben," Keo said. It came out thick. "Is it my watch?"

"It is not." Benal's voice was low. "You've been excused from watches. Teva decided, and I agreed with her, and you were unconscious, so I'd call it a majority. Roughly."

"I can take it."

"You can't take your own boot off. Go to sleep. I have this." A pause, while he thought about it. "Probably."

He came over on his knees with the skin, one hand flat against his chest, slid the other under Keo's head and lifted it.

"Drink."

Keo drank. When he stopped, Benal waited and tipped the skin again, until Keo had drunk more than he wanted.

"Teva said you drink every time you wake," Benal said. "I'm under orders. It's very restful, being under orders. I see why you all like it."

He eased Keo's head back down and went to fold himself up again at the mouth of the overhang.

Keo lay and listened to the rain on the fronds. Somewhere behind him Teva was breathing slow and even, asleep at last.

After a while Benal was humming, hardly more than a breath, pitched so it would not wake her. Keo knew the tune before he knew he was listening. It was Liaso's song, the long one. Keo had sung all of it on the savanna yesterday, with breath to spare, and Benal had come in on the chorus a breath late.

Benal was not late now. He had the whole of it.

It came up in Keo then, out of nowhere, so strong it hurt worse than the leg: for Ben. For the boy he had carried loads for since they were small, and walked slower for, and been sorry for. Ben, sitting up in the dark with a sore chest and a club he could barely swing, keeping the watch that had always been Keo's, keeping him.

He wanted to say so. He could not find the front of the sentence.

The rain went on. The fever came up over him like water, warm, and he let it.

The last thing was Benal at the mouth of the overhang, still sitting up, still humming.
