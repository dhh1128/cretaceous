The ground was dry and open and easy underfoot. Keo had the heading off Teva's arm once at the bottom of the tree and did not need it again. The light came long and gold through the oaks and lay on everything. The breeze kept on, and the gear tied to the packs dried and lightened as they went. Noli ranged out ahead and came back and ranged out again. Behind him he could hear the other two keeping up, Teva's step and Benal's longer, slower one. Once Benal said *delicious* to himself, apropos of nothing, and Teva snorted.

He had his spike easy in his hand and his sense out ahead of him, light, not reaching, as his mother had taught him. He did not think anything in particular. It was how he had walked out of the tangle that first morning, before the fog closed, when it had seemed for a while as if he might know what he was doing.

Then the ground moved.

It came up through his boots, slow, a weight set down and lifted and set down again, so deep that he felt it in his knees before he heard anything. Everything in him went still. He put his hand out flat behind him without looking and felt the other two stop. That was the oldest fear there was. Every child in the Enclave knew it before it could walk: the ground that moved meant something big, and something big meant run.

He reached with his sense, and what he found was not an animal. It was too much for that. It was like reaching into weather: many large presences ahead of him, filling the whole space, and under his boots the slow footfalls that told him they were in no hurry.

"What is it?" Teva said, very low.

"Big," Keo said. "A lot of them. Slow." He went forward, crouching, to where the trees thinned and the ground lifted into a ridge. At the top he lay down on his belly and looked over.

Beyond the woodland below the ridge lay a lake, broad and shallow and gold with the evening, and the saropo herd was walking through it.

There were perhaps twenty of them. They walked through the water slowly, their long necks rising and dipping, and every step they took he felt in his chest through the ground. The young ones walked in the middle. The water ran off their sides in sheets. Their reflections walked upside down under them, and the light lay over the whole lake so that it was hard to tell where they ended and the water began.

He had seen saropo a handful of times, from the top of the tangle, far off, and had prized every one, though nobody else thought anything of them. They were too big to work and too big to eat and too big to be afraid of. They were simply there, like hills.

He had never seen them like this, and he had never heard anyone from home describe this lake. Nothing was hunting anything. Nothing was chasing them, and nothing came up out of the water. The three of them lay on the ridge with their chins on their hands and watched, and for the first time since the tangle Keo could not think of a single thing that could hurt them.

"They don't even notice it," Benal said quietly, about the water. "Look at them. It's up to their chests and they don't notice it."

"Has anyone from home seen this?" Teva said.

Keo thought about it. His parents had been past the tangle more times than anyone he knew. They had never described a lake. "Not that they ever said."

"Then it's us," Benal said. "Possibly the First Walk came by here, going the other way, but they had other things to look at." He watched the herd. "So it's us. Nobody we know has ever stood here." He said it quietly, and kept looking.

Teva was looking too, steadily, at the whole of it, one animal and then the next, and Keo understood that she was keeping it.

He was thinking the next thing after it. What he thought, watching the herd walk through the lake without noticing it was there, was: you could ride one of those across a river.

He did it without deciding to, out of habit. He reached for the nearest of them and pushed, gently, the way he pushed at Noli: *this way*.

It was like pushing a wall that did not know he was there. He leaned on it with everything he had for a breath, two, and there was nothing, not even a flicker of attention, only the slow enormous warmth of the animal going on being what it was. The pressure came up behind his eyes and stayed there.

He let go. A wild hadrosaur had yielded to him briefly before, and it had cost him. This was not that. This was the size where he stopped. He knew it now, in his body, and he would not forget it.

He did not say anything about it. His head ached. He rubbed his eyes and got up, and the other two got up with him. There was a small flower in the grass by his hand, pale, no bigger than his thumbnail, and he picked it without deciding to and put it inside the neck of his tunic.

They went down into the trees below the ridge in the last of the light. At the bottom, something was cropping at the low growth with its back to them: broad and low to the ground, armored all over its back in plates and studs, with a tail like a thing made to break bones. Keo felt it with his sense before he saw it, a dull slow presence, like a stone that breathed. It did not lift its head. They walked wide of it, all three of them watching it the whole way, and it went on eating.

They camped in a hollow where the rock of the ridge came down into the trees and walled them in on three sides. The breeze dropped at dusk, as they were putting the fence across the fourth, and the midges came up off the lake.

They came in a fine gray haze, and they went for whatever skin there was. The others had their faces. Keo had his face and his neck and his arms and the backs of his hands where they were split from the climb, and the midges found all of it. He drove the stakes in a cloud of them, slapping and muttering, while they laid cut thorny growth between them, and Teva, gelling the stakes beside him, watched him with an expression he could not read and then handed him the pot to do the last few so he would have something to do with his hands.

"They like you," she said.

"Everything likes me." He slapped his neck. "It's Ben they love."

"I had noticed," Benal said, from somewhere in his own cloud of them.

Nearly an hour later, the breeze came back. It came across the lake and up the slope, light and steady, and it shook the midges out of the air like dust from a cloth. Keo stood with his hands on his knees and breathed.

Noli would not come in past the gel. She found a hollow at the edge of where the smell thinned, and turned around in it three times and settled with her head tucked down, facing them.

Full dark came quickly. The beetles came out in the brush below the ridge, a few at first and then more, small green lights that came on and went out and came on again, on some rhythm none of them could follow. They lay inside the fence and watched them.

Then Keo looked up, and the sky was clear.

It was enormous. He had not looked at it properly since the third night. The stars were thick across the whole of it, the founders' stars, and Teva was already looking up, he saw, with her lips moving very slightly. He knew what she was looking for, and he did not ask.

He asked something else instead.

"Where is it?" he said.

Benal did not pretend not to know what he meant. "Nobody knows," he said. "The founders had a list of bodies that might be it, and nobody knows which one. And whichever one it is, it's a rock a long way out, too far out and too faint for us to pick out. We'll only see it with our eyes when it's close." He considered the sky. "So the good news is that we'll definitely see it eventually."

Teva made a sound in the dark that might have been a laugh.

"There are instruments at Genesis," Benal said. "Or there were. That would find it. If they still work. If anyone knew how to make them work." He was quiet a while. "That's one more reason to go, I suppose."

"Those aren't ours," Benal said, about the beetles.

"They look like ours," Keo said.

"Ours are quicker. These are slower than a breath. And they don't keep time with each other, and if you watch the ones on the left you can see that they—"

"Ben," Teva said.

"—possibly answer the ones on the right, which would mean—"

"They're different beetles," Teva said. "We believe you."

"It was a good reason," Benal said.

"It was a long one."

The beetles went on and off in the brush. Benal fell asleep first, and then it was Keo's watch.

He sat it out at the edge of the fence with his spike across his knees and his eyes open, all three hours of it, and did not once close them. When it was over he went and touched Teva's shoulder, the good one, and she woke at once and sat up and took the spike from him.

For a moment they were both awake, sitting side by side inside the fence with the breeze on them and Noli breathing in her hollow beyond the gel.

He had it ready. He had had it ready for a long time, longer than the journey, longer than he liked to think about. It was about her, and about why he had come. It was not something you could take back once you had said it. There was nothing to stop him. Benal was asleep. The night was clear and quiet. She was right there, looking out at the dark with the spike on her knees.

He thought about it, and he decided not to.

He had the flower still. It had gone soft in his tunic. He took it out and held it out to her on his palm.

Teva looked at it, and then at him. She took it without any change in her face. "Omya likes these," she said.

So it would go to Omya, into her plait with the others. He had brought Omya flowers himself, before any of this. That was all right. It was a kind of answer, and he found that he could stand it.
