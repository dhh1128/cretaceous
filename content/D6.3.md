Keo took the heading off Teva's arm once at the bottom of the tree and didn't need it again. The light came long and gold through the oaks. Noli ranged ahead and came back. Once Benal said *delicious* to himself, out of nowhere, and Teva snorted.

He carried his spike easy and his sense out ahead, light, not reaching, as his mother had taught him. He had walked out of the tangle like that the first morning, before the fog closed, when it had seemed for a while as if he might know what he was doing.

Then the ground moved.

It came up through his boots, slow, a weight set down and lifted and set down again, so deep he felt it in his knees before he heard anything. Everything in him went still. He put his hand out flat behind him without looking and felt the other two stop. Ground that moved meant something big, and something big meant run.

He reached with his sense, and what he found was too much to be an animal. It was like reaching into weather: many large presences ahead, filling the whole space, and under his boots the footfalls of things in no hurry.

"What is it?" Teva whispered.

"Big," Keo said. "A lot of them. Slow." He crept forward to where the trees thinned and the ground lifted into a ridge, and lay on his belly at the top to look over.

Past the woodland below lay a lake, broad and shallow and gold with evening, and a saropo herd was walking through it.

Perhaps twenty of them, long necks rising and dipping, the young in the middle. He felt every step in his chest. Water sheeted off their sides. Their reflections walked upside down under them, and the light lay over the whole lake so that he couldn't tell where they ended and the water began.

He'd seen saropo a handful of times from the top of the tangle, far off, and prized every one, though nobody else thought anything of them. Too big to work, too big to eat, too big to fear. They were simply there, like the oldest trees.

Nothing was hunting anything, and nothing came up out of the water. The three of them lay on the ridge with their chins on their hands and watched, and for the first time since the tangle Keo couldn't think of a single thing that could hurt them.

"They don't even notice the water," Benal murmured. "Look. It's up to their chests."

"Has anyone from home seen this?" Teva said.

Keo's parents had been past the tangle more than anyone he knew. They had never described a lake. "Not that they ever said."

"Then it's us," Benal said. "Possibly the First Walk came by here, going the other way, but they had other things to look at. Nobody we know has ever stood here."

Teva was looking steadily at the whole of it, one animal and then the next, and Keo understood that she was keeping it.

He was already thinking the next thing: you could ride one of those across a river.

Out of habit, without deciding to, he reached for the nearest and pushed, gently, as he pushed at Noli: *this way*.

It was like pushing a wall that didn't know he was there. He leaned on it with everything he had for a breath, two, and got not a flicker of attention, only the slow enormous warmth of the animal going on being what it was. The pressure came up behind his eyes and stayed.

He let go. A wild hadrosaur had yielded to him briefly before, and it had cost him. This was the size where he stopped. He knew it now, in his body.

He didn't say anything about it. His head ached. He rubbed his eyes and got up, and the other two got up with him. By his hand a pale flower no bigger than his thumbnail grew in the ferns. He picked it and tucked it inside the neck of his tunic.

In the last of the light they went down into the trees and camped in a hollow where the rock walled them in on three sides, and fenced the fourth. The breeze came up the slope off the lake, light and steady.

Noli found a hollow at the edge of where the gel's smell thinned, and settled with her legs folded under her, facing them.

Full dark came quickly. Beetles came out in the brush below the ridge, a few and then more, small green lights coming on and going out on some rhythm none of them could follow.

Then Keo looked up, and the sky was clear.

He hadn't looked at it properly since the third night. Stars lay thick across all of it, and Teva was already looking up, he saw, her lips barely moving. He knew what she was looking for, and he did not ask.

He asked something else.

"Where is it?"

Benal didn't pretend not to know what he meant. "Nobody knows," he said. "The founders had a list of bodies that might be it, and nobody knows which. Whichever it is, it's a rock way out, too far and too faint to pick out. We'll only see it when it's close." He considered the sky. "So the good news is we'll definitely see it eventually."

Teva made a sound in the dark that might have been a laugh.

"There are instruments at Genesis," Benal said. "Or there were. They could find it. If they still work. If anyone knew how to make them work." He was quiet a while. "One more reason to go, I suppose."

"Those aren't ours," Benal said, about the beetles.

"They look like ours," Keo said.

"Ours are quicker. These are slower than a breath. And they don't keep time with each other, and if you watch the ones on the left you can see that they—"

"Ben," Teva said.

"—possibly answer the ones on the right, which would mean—"

"They're different beetles," Teva said. "We believe you."

"It was a good reason," Benal said.

"It was a long one."

Benal fell asleep first, and then it was Keo's watch.

He sat all three hours of it at the edge of the fence with his spike across his knees, and did not once close his eyes. Then he touched Teva's shoulder, the good one, and she woke at once, sat up and took the spike from him.

For a moment they were both awake, side by side, with the breeze on them and Noli breathing in her hollow beyond the gel.

He had it ready. He'd had it ready longer than the journey. It was about her, and about why he had come. Once said, it couldn't be taken back. Benal was asleep. The night was clear. She was right there, looking out at the dark with the spike on her knees.

He thought about it, and decided not to.

The flower had gone soft in his tunic. He took it out and held it out to her on his palm.

Teva looked at it, then at him. She took it without any change in her face. "Omya likes these," she said.

So it would go to Omya, into her plait with the others. He had brought Omya flowers himself, before any of this. It was a kind of answer, and he found he could stand it.
