He shouldered past the ceratops hide that draped the cleft entrance and emerged from the subterranean chill into the hot, wet press of the night. The paralysis of the chamber fell away, replaced by the living wall of insect noise from the jungle beyond the border tangle.

He sucked in a breath of thick air, tasting magnolia and damp earth. It did nothing to slow his hammering heart. He paused. His proximity sense reached out to feel a familiar, tightly coiled mind.

*There*.

Teva stood half-hidden in a giant cycad's spiky shadow, just beyond the light circle of the kiva's ventilation shaft. The storm had passed, but the air remained a sauna. Sparks of bioluminescence drifted around her, the insects returning as the rain tapered off. She wasn't looking at the entrance. Her gaze fixed on the border line. Her profile cut a silhouette against the flicker of the village's cooking fires.

She had been waiting for him.

"It's useless," Keo said. The word tasted sour on his tongue. "They're just... broken."

"I know." Her voice carried no more echo than a stone dropped in mud.

He walked over and stopped a few feet away, still resonating with the chamber's toxic energy. "They're going to talk. Hesh and Sila will talk, Marek will yell, and my father... they'll all just talk. Until the last culture dies."

"Let them talk," she said.

He kicked at a root. "Someone has to do something."

"I am."

Keo stopped. The final power of the words cut through his frustration. He studied her. "What?"

Teva turned to face him. Her eyes, shadowed pools, reflected the drifting insect-light. The numb grief he'd seen after Alira's death was gone, burned away. In its place sat a structural certainty—a look he had never seen on her. It was terrifying.

"I'm going," she said.

"Going where? On patrol?" He knew it was a stupid question before he'd even finished.

"Genesis."

The word was a physical blow. Keo’s stomach hollowed. He heard Elder Hesh's voice in his head: A hundred and six souls. With old-tech rifles. With plasma cutters.

"Teva," he whispered. "That's... no. You heard Hesh. It's eighty kilometers. It's a swamp. It's..."

"Suicide," she finished for him. Her voice was laced with a chilling contempt aimed not at him, but at the word itself. "Alone, yes. For them, yes. But not for us."

"Us?" The air felt too thin to breathe.

"You and me." She took a step closer. She wasn't asking him; she was telling him. "They're paralyzed by the First Walk. They think it's still a hundred and six dead. But the Founders didn't have us. They didn't have our upgrades."

She ticked the points off. Her voice was low and precise, like a Keeper reciting a protocol. "We have the baseline immunities. We can eat what we find, drink the water. We have venom resistance. My magnetic sense is perfect—we won't get lost. My balance," she tapped her temple, "my proprioception... I can cross a ravine in the dark. I can heal fast. And you... you can sense a herd from five kilometers. You can feel a razortail pack before it smells you. You can hold Noli's mind. You can feel the world, Keo."

The realization hit him. She hadn't just decided to go. She had strategized. She wasn't talking to Keo, her childhood friend. She was conscripting an asset.

His loyalty, his stupid, hopeful crush, his lifelong habit of being near her—it all twisted into a spike of panic. He couldn't go. He couldn't. He thought of the border tangle, and the real world beyond it. Not the stories, but the things he’d seen on short patrols with his parents. The gore of a T-Rex kill. The ground rattling from a ceratops herd. Flat, murky water broken by hundreds of croc snouts. The calculating chirps of a razortail pack, so much bigger and faster than Noli.

But he also knew, with a sickness in his gut, that she was right. The adults were broken.

"Teva, no," he said, but the word was a puff of air. "We can't. Your grandmother..."

"My grandmother is why I'm going!" she snapped, the first crack in her rigid control. "She's gone, Keo. Rano can't save us. Hesh can't save us. This... this stasis," she said, spitting the word he'd just thought, "is a luxury. We don't get to be broken. We're the only ones who aren't."

She held his gaze, eyes challenging him. "I am going. With or without you."

She meant it. She would walk out of the border tangle, alone, with nothing but her magnetic sense and that terrifying rage, and the crocs would have her before the first river.

The panic in his chest didn't go away. It just changed. It was no longer the fear of going. It was the terror of letting her go.

He looked at her, at the granite certainty in her face, and his own fear felt thin and useless. His bravado wasn't a mask; it was an impulse, an instinct.

"You're not going alone," he said. His voice was hoarse but steady.

For a second, the iron in her expression flickered. Relief? No. Something else. Just a human easing of the tension in her shoulders.

"Good," she said, turning away to face the jungle. "We need Benal."
