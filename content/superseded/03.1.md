Keo hung back by the Council Chamber's arched entrance. The dim throat trapped the air and the smell of too many bodies. From here, he could see the raised stone shelf acting as a dais and the shadowed mass of nearly two hundred adults packed onto the floor. The mycelial lattices cultivated in the walls lit their forms with a blue glow.

The stone under his palm felt slick with condensation. The Founders, in their terror of the temporal paradox, had forbidden any markings. The walls remained bare. No mark, no graffiti, no history. A monument to erasure.

"—is not a theory!" The voice belonged to Marek, Benal’s father. It cracked on the last word—a sound too thin for such a broad man. Keo craned his neck and spotted him near the front. Marek radiated desperation that pushed against the room’s stillness.

"The yazhi in the ground is not a theory," he repeated. "We are failing." Marek pointed not at the Elders, but at a group of figures slumped on a bench near the wall. "The Keepers know it."

Keo’s gaze followed Marek’s finger. Rano sat with his head in his hands. Beside him, the other journeymen bowed their shoulders in defeat.

"They have worked for three weeks," Marek pressed, voice rising. "They are Masters in all but name, and their best effort... their best effort is not enough. The cultures are dimming. The protocol Omya held in her head is gone."

He turned to the three Elders on the natural rise of stone. "The protocols are at Genesis. On metal tablets. In the Archive. It's like a basket of pan, and we are starving because we are afraid to lift the lid!"

"He's right!" a voice shouted from the darkness in the back. Keo couldn't see the speaker. "The Brancher texts are clear! The paradox is a lie! We're on a new branch!"

"Heresy!" The word cut through the rising murmur. Elder Sila. She sat at the center of the dais, rigid. Where others looked weary, the crisis energized her. "The Brancher texts are poison. They tell you a comforting lie: that nothing we do matters to the future we left. We are a seed, planted in the past to save the future. Our one, sacred duty is to avoid paradox. One lost lightcell, one metal knife, one footprint where it should not be, and we could erase the entire future we are trying to save."

Elder Hesh, seated to her right, raised a placating hand. Gray-haired and patient, he wore responsibility on his face. "Sila is right. The Mission is our law. But even if you would ignore that," he said, his gaze finding Marek, "let us speak of the 'First Walk'."

The chamber stilled. This was their origin story, and their nightmare.

"You speak of Genesis as if it is a supply room," Hesh continued, his voice tapping each word like a chisel. "You forget it took the Founders three years to find this place. Three years of scouting parties that never returned. Three years of fighting for every kilometer of swamp just to find this defensible kiva, this clean water.

"They left Genesis with two hundred colonists," Hesh said, voice low. "All of them in environmental suits. They had old-tech rifles. They had plasma cutters. Desperation drove them to use hard-tech, a known paradox risk. And when they finally established this Enclave... they were ninety-four. We lost over a hundred souls, Marek. Despite all that hard-tech."
Hesh leaned forward. "And you want us to send a team back? Back through that green hell? With what? Croc-spikes and a prayer?"

"And who would we send?" a woman called out from the crowd, her voice rough with practicality. "Our best Explorers? Our Watchers? We strip our own palisade of its defenders for a journey that killed our Founders? It's suicide."

"It is suicide to stay!" Marek shot back. "What future is Sila 'saving' if our yazhi die in their nests! There is no mission if we go extinct first!"

"Better to die with a clean soul," Sila said, "than to live as contaminants."

The chamber erupted. Shouts of "Heresy!" and "Murderer!" clashed—a wave of noise beating against Keo’s ears. He saw Benal’s mother trying to pull Marek back, to quiet him.

Keo looked away, chest tightening. His gaze swept the crowd, looking for his parents. He spotted them easily. They stood near the edge, just as he did, defined by their stillness. Explorers. The ones who knew the world outside the palisade. They, of all people, knew the cost Hesh spoke of.

His father’s face hardened. He sat with crossed arms. His mother watched the Elders with a blank expression. They were silent. They, who had taught Keo to track a razortail and read the sky, offered nothing. They were as paralyzed as the Elders.

A hand gripped the entrance arch. Elana, Alira’s mother, pulled herself into the chamber.

The noise stopped. Not all at once, but in a ragged wave of shame.

Grief had hollowed her out and sunk her eyes into a bleached face. Unsteady, she walked into the center of the room. She looked at Sila. She looked at Hesh.

"My... my daughter," she whispered. The chamber’s acoustics, cruel to the arguments, carried her whisper to every corner. "She did not die from a croc. She did not fall from a nest."

Elana looked down at her hands. "She died... warm. In her own bed. Because the monsters... the monsters in the blood... are back. And we had nothing."

She looked up. Her gaze swept the crowd, passing over Keo without seeing him.

"Your paradox," she whispered to Sila. "Your First Walk," she said to Hesh. "Your... Mission." Her voice was empty. "They are just words. They are air. My daughter is gone."

She turned and walked out, bare feet silent on the stone.

Absolute silence settled in her wake.

Keo watched her go. He thought of her words. The monsters in the blood. He thought of Alira's still form. He thought of his own, useless "everything will be fine" from that morning.

It was a lie. They were all liars.

Marek spoke again, voice hoarse. "Do you see? Do you... see? We must..."

Sila was already shaking her head. "The grief is raw. But the protocol..."

Keo couldn't breathe. He couldn't listen to another word. This was not a debate; it was a funeral. They weren't just mourning Alira; they were mourning themselves. They had already given up.

This was the choice. Not just shock, but a choice. They chose to be broken.

He looked one last time at his father. His father, his hero, the man who had faced down a saropo herd, met his gaze. And in his father's eyes, Keo saw it. Not anger. Not resolve. Just fear. Deep, paralyzing fear.
His father looked away.

Keo's heart thudded. He pushed off the wall.

He didn't run. He didn't shove. He just turned his back on the Council, on his parents, on the suffocating weight of their failure, and slipped out of the chamber. He walked past the main kiva's dimming central light, ignored the looks from the door watchers, and headed for the surface.
He needed air.
