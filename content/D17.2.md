Benal came back to the plate with his hands smelling of the rocks. Keo handed him clams out of the knotted tunic, and he tipped them raw into his mouth standing, his eyes on the metal. It lay flat on the bench under the panels, where he had stood over it all morning, but for the hour on the rocks.

"What's that one say?" Keo said.

Benal read him the stamp in its corner. <!-- @WF-plates.stamp: +33mo --> "*Jump plus thirty-three months. Deposited after the coalition left. For whoever comes back.*"

"Whoever comes back," Keo said. "That's us."

"It's my equation," Benal said. "Somebody cut it into metal two hundred years ago."

The founders' figures ran across the top of the plate in their own notation, cut as cleanly as the protocol next door. The three lines for place came first; they had always come out. Under them ran the line for time, his line, which he had carried in his head since his board went to the bottom of the sea. Every term of it was where he would have put it. At its end stood the drift factor with its question mark, the one he had drawn and rubbed out and drawn again for years.

Under all four lines a stroke ran the width of the plate, and under the stroke, one more term: an R and his own sign for when they had landed, shut together in a pair of angled brackets. He had seen it once before, two nights ago, asleep in the water building.

At the plate's head someone had cut a mark: a ring with a gap in it, and a line from one side of the gap straight across to the far side.

The lab was longer than the archive. The panels gave off their warm dust, and under it was something burnt and old, like fat scorched onto the stones of a cook pit. It came from a frame at the far end, taller than Keo, hung with metal boxes. Every box had been opened, its sides hanging loose, its insides pulled half out and broken: cords, and small flat pieces scorched at the edges.

"What's all that?" Keo said.

"No idea." Benal had looked at it once and gone no closer. "I wouldn't know if I looked at it all day."

The benches down both sides were heaped with soft sheets like the ones in the outer rooms, stained and stuck in blocks that came apart in his fingers. Among the blocks lay leaves of metal and tablets, and those he could read.

The dust on the bench beside the plate lay fine as ash. He wrote his own line out in it with a finger, as he had had it on his board, from the first term to the question mark. He looked from his line to theirs. They matched, all but the one under the stroke.

The lower half of the plate was smaller figures, row on row, cut so fine they swam. His eyes had ached with them all morning. On the bench, half under a block of sheets, lay a round of hard clear stuff set in a ring of metal, a palm across.

"Glass."

He had known the word all his life, as the name of a thing that lasted and was never to be made. He picked it up, cool and clear all the way through, and held it over the small figures. They came up at him large, every stroke, as the hairs on his arm had come up under a drop of water on the coast. He read the rest of the plate through it.

The small figures were the working of the new term. He went through them twice. Then he put in himself as a traveler and calculated a homeward journey.

It came out divided by nothing.

He tried it again, by another way home, and then another. Each time the term under the stroke went to zero. The whole system above it went with it. There was no day at the end. There was no place either.

So the mistake was theirs, and it was not the one he had looked for all his life. He could not think why anyone would cut it into metal and leave it for whoever came back.

Teva came straight down the room from the archive with the register open along her forearm, and turned it so he could see the leaf. Keo held out an opened clam, and she ate it without looking.

A woman's face looked out of the metal, young, with her hair pulled back off it.

"Ben. Look at her."

He looked.

"She was in the water building," Teva said. "Two nights ago, in what I saw. At the door, with a load on her back." She kept a finger on the leaf. "It was a real day, Ben. What does that mean?"

He still had the glass in one hand. He laid the other flat on the plate.

"This is m-my—" He stopped. The banked walls gave nothing back, and he heard the word come out of him broken. "This is my dream. The one in the water building, the equation in somebody else's hand. It's this, Teva. T-term for term. And that one at the bottom, under the line, I've never written. Not once. I dreamed it before I ever saw it."

Teva bent over the plate. She had never loved algebra. She looked anyway, along each line in turn and then at the brackets, before she looked up at him.

"What does that one do?"

"It breaks the rest. Put somebody in it, and run it toward home, and that term goes to nothing. Everything above it goes with it. You don't get a day at the end, and you don't get a place. I've run it four ways this morning and it does the same every time."

"So they got it wrong," Teva said.

"You've been saying that since you were small, Ben," Keo said. "That they got it wrong."

"I've been saying they left something out. That if somebody found the missing piece and put it back, somebody could go home. That's what I've been doing on my board for years." He touched the brackets. "They didn't leave this out. They put it in. They knew, and they cut it into metal so it would last."

She kept her eyes on him and waited for the rest. He did not have the rest yet.

Keo had been at a stack of leaves on the far bench, turning them over one at a time. There was a picture on the top one. Now he held it up.

"Ben. I've seen that."

It was the mark from the head of the plate, cut at the top of the leaf.

"The ring with the gap in it," Keo said. "And the line across. The night I fell, under the overhang. It was right in front of my eyes, sharp, like that." He put the leaf down. "I thought it was the fever. I didn't tell anybody. What was there to tell?"

Benal took the stack and went through it with the glass, from the bottom up. The bottom leaves were the station's log, later than the one Teva had read in the archive. In among them a line said that two wigs had been lost to the sky, and the drones with them, whatever wigs and drones were. He went on past it.

The top leaves had the mark at the head of every one. They had set what they called the apparatus to go back instead of forward. They had made ready. They had counted who would go, name by name.

"They set it to go back," he said.

"Home?" Teva said.

"No. The other way. They turned the apparatus round. Not forward, to where they came from. Back, deeper in the past than we are, further from where they started." He turned to the last leaf.

"How far?" Keo said.

Ben was staring at nothing. He turned the leaf over. Its back was bare, too.

"It doesn't say. It stops."

He laid the leaf beside the plate, mark beside mark. Keo's ring. Teva's woman at the door. His own term, in his sleep, before he had ever seen it cut. A circle with a gap and a one-way line.

"I think it's a drawing," he said. "The mark, Keo. The ring is time, going on. The gap is where you leave it." He drew the line across with his finger, in the dust over his own equation. "And that's the way back across."

Keo looked at the line in the dust. Teva looked at Benal.

"Nobody's been dreaming," he said. "Not either of you. Something was done here, at this place. They jumped further back in time from here. And what we've been seeing is what that jump left behind in the place, like the smell of lightning after a strike."

"The weird flashes?" Teva said.

"Since the night Keo fell. And worse every day we came closer to this island. All of us."

Keo was looking past him at the gutted frame at the end of the room.

"It's like they just left," he said. "They had all of it. That, and the plates, and everything. And they went the wrong way." His voice went up. "Why couldn't they just go home?"

Ben's heart began to pound.

He was suddenly remembering something his father had said about all the dead symbols on his board. *Those aren't anything, Ben. You're just modeling a theory. What might be. Spend some time with something that you actually live.*

His heart pounded faster.

"Going home isn't a direction," he whispered. "It's a place where you've been before."

Teva and Keo looked at him blankly.

"I've got it."

They waited.

He put his finger on the plate, under the R in its brackets. "It's a divisor," he said. "The return divisor. R is whoever goes. The equation has to hold on to something at the far end, somewhere the traveler has already been. A founder was born in that time. Put a founder in and it gives a day, because they've been there before. Put me in—" He spread his fingers in the dust. "There's nothing at the far end for me to hold on to. You divide by zero. It gives nothing."

The rest of it was there, all of it. His mouth would not hold it. He started it and lost it and started it again. His chest caught under the ribs where the constrictor had had him.

"They c-could have gone home. Any one of them. They'd been there."

"And us?" Teva said.

He had to stop and breathe. "Nobody b-born here has."

Teva was looking past him. Over the benches a leaf was fixed to the wall by its corners. Its stamp said *Jump minus eleven months*. <!-- @WF-ep5.stamp: -11mo --> Did that make it older than anything else in the room? Benal read it aloud.

"*The engineered vector platform is not life support. It is not an aid to settlement. It is the product.*" He went on down it. "*Personnel modification is a test bed and a delivery method, nothing more. What returns is the platform.*" A name stood at the foot. "*C. Arroyo.*"

Teva turned the register on her arm and looked from the face on the leaf to the name on the wall.

"Vector," Benal said. The last plates next door had been headed with it. "Vector handling. That's Vitarium language. It's the Seeding. What's on those plates is the thing they came here to make. Not to keep themselves alive with. To make." He looked at his own hands, which were too long, like the rest of him. "Every child at home has it in them."

"The upgrades are the cure the founders wanted?" Teva said.

Under the plate's edge, by Keo's leaf, lay a tablet with the same stamp in its corner, cut as fine as the working on the plate. He read it through the glass.

"*Impact,*" he said, "*narrowed down to sometime in the next two hundred and twenty years.*" <!-- @WF-dating.fix.margin: 220y -->

"From when?" Keo said.

"Now would be when they stamped it. Thirty-three months after arrival. Year three." <!-- @WF-plates.stamp: +33mo --> He did not need the dust for it. "Two hundred and twenty from year three is two hundred and twenty-three. This is two hundred and six. Seventeen years left." <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y --> He heard himself go on. "Seventeen at the most. It could be any one of them. It could be this year."

"Seventeen," Keo said.

"The Council says we still have centuries till impact," Teva said. "Every session I've ever sat through."

"The Council has the number the founders brought." He put his finger on the tablet. "This sounds like someone got better information."

Beside the tablet lay a leaf ruled into columns, and Teva took it up before he could. She read the first line under her breath, and stopped. Her eyes widened.

"That's the Watch," she said. "That's Omya's sky. Which stars the moon goes over, and when." She read on. "Word for word. As far as I've got it."

Across the top, in the founders' plain letters, it said to check the table against the sky every clear night, for as long as it took.

"She's been checking it for sixty years," <!-- @WF-omya.watch.years: 60 --> Teva said. "Every clear night. Nobody ever told her what for."

"I know what for," Benal said. "The drift factor. The founders' tablet says sometime in the next two hundred and twenty years. <!-- @WF-dating.fix.margin: 220y --> It doesn't say which one. You watch the moon go over a star, and you lay it against the table, and you know how far off they were. Every one you see makes the window smaller." He looked at the leaf under her hand. "Sixty years <!-- @WF-omya.watch.years: 60 --> of watching. It's all in Omya's head."

She laid the leaf on the bench and kept her hand on it.

Teva laid it out for all of them, slowly, the way she laid out a protocol. "The cure is in every child at home. They made it to be carried back. Nobody alive can carry it, and nobody who could is left. And the sky has seventeen years. At the most." <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y -->

She closed the register on her arm.

"And nobody told us," Keo said. "Any of it."
