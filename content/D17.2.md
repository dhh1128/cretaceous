The spray had dried to salt on Benal's forearms by the time he stood at the plate again. Keo handed him clams out of the knotted tunic. He ate them standing, tipping them raw into his mouth, with his eyes on the metal.

He had stood over it since first light, but for the hour on the rocks. It lay flat on the bench under the panels.

"What's that one say?" Keo said.

Benal read him the stamp in its corner. <!-- @WF-plates.stamp: +33mo --> "*Jump plus thirty-three months. Deposited after the coalition left. For whoever comes back.*"

"Whoever comes back," Keo said. "That's us."

"It's my equation."

The founders' figures ran across the top of the plate in their own notation, cut as cleanly as the protocol next door. The three lines for place came first, and he gave them a glance. They had always come out. Under them ran the line for time, his line. He had carried it in his head since his board went to the bottom of the sea. Every term of it was where he would have put it. At its end stood the question mark he had drawn and rubbed out and drawn again for years, cut into metal by somebody else.

Under all four lines a stroke ran the width of the plate. Under the stroke there was one more term: an R and his own sign for when they had landed, shut together inside a pair of angled brackets. He had seen it once before, two nights ago, asleep in the water building.

Over everything, at the plate's head, someone had cut a mark. It was a ring with a gap in it, and a line from one side of the gap straight across to the far side.

The lab was longer than the archive and had been shut longer. The panels gave off their warm dust, and under that was something burnt and old, like fat scorched onto the stones of a cook pit. It came from the far end. A frame taller than Keo stood there on the floor, with metal boxes hung in it. Every box had been opened. Their sides hung loose. What had been inside them was pulled half out and broken, cords and small flat pieces scorched at the edges.

"What's all that?" Keo said.

"I don't know." Benal had looked at it once and gone no closer. "I wouldn't know if I looked at it all day."

The benches down both sides were heaped with soft sheets like the ones in the outer rooms. They were stained and stuck together in blocks that came apart in his fingers. Where a line could still be made out, it was in the lab's own language. Among the blocks lay leaves of metal and tablets, and those he could read.

The dust on the bench beside the plate lay fine and dry as ash. He put a finger in it and wrote his own line out, as he had had it on his board, from the first term to the question mark. The dust took every stroke and held it. He looked from his line to theirs. They matched, all but the one under the stroke.

The lower half of the plate was smaller figures, row on row, cut so fine they swam when he bent to them. His eyes had ached with them all morning. On the bench, half under a block of sheets, lay a round of glass set in a ring of metal, a palm across.

He had known the word all his life, as the name of a thing that lasted and was never to be made. He had never held any. He picked it up. It was cool to the hand and clear all the way through. He held it over the small figures. They came up at him large, every stroke of them, as the hairs on his arm had come up under a drop of water on the coast. He read the rest of the plate through it.

The small figures were the working of the new term. He went through them twice. Then he put in a traveler, someone like himself, and took the way home.

It came out divided by nothing.

He tried it again, by another way home, and then another. Each time the term under the stroke went to nothing. The whole system above it went with it. There was no day at the end. There was no place either.

So the mistake was theirs, and it was not the one he had looked for all his life. He could not think why anyone would cut it into metal and leave it for whoever came back.

Teva came in from the archive with the register held open along her forearm. She came straight down the room to him and turned the register so he could see the leaf. Keo held out an opened clam, and she ate it without looking at it.

A woman's face looked out of the metal, young, with her hair pulled back off it.

"Ben. Look at her."

He looked.

"She was in the water building," Teva said. "Two nights ago, in what I saw. She was at the door with a load on her back, and people in front of her." She kept a finger on the leaf. "It was a real day. She was there." She looked up at him. "What does that mean?"

He still had the glass in one hand. He laid the other flat on the plate.

"This is m-my—" He stopped. The banked walls gave nothing back, and he heard the word come out of him broken, exactly as it was. "This is my dream. The one in the water building. It's this, Teva. T-term for term. That one at the bottom I'd never written. Not once. And they cut it."

Teva bent over the plate. She could not read a line of it. She looked anyway, along each line in turn and then at the brackets, before she looked up at him.

"What does that one do?"

"N-nothing. It b-breaks the rest."

"So they got it wrong."

"You've been saying that since you were small, Ben," Keo said.

"They left one out. That's what I've been s-saying. This one they put in."

She kept her eyes on him and waited for the rest. There was no rest.

Nobody said *fever*.

Under the plate's edge lay a tablet with the same stamp in its corner, cut as fine as the working on the plate. He read it through the glass.

"*Impact,*" he said. "*From now, within two hundred and twenty years.*" <!-- @WF-dating.fix.margin: 220y -->

"From when?" Keo said.

"Now is the stamp. Thirty-three months. Year three." <!-- @WF-plates.stamp: +33mo --> He did not need the dust for it. "Two hundred and twenty from year three is two hundred and twenty-three. This is two hundred and six. Seventeen." He heard himself go on. "Seventeen at the most. It could be any one of them. It could be this one."

"Seventeen," Keo said.

"The Council says centuries," Teva said. "Every session I've ever sat through."

"The Council has the number the founders b-brought." He put his finger on the tablet. "This is the one after."

Beside the tablet lay a leaf ruled into columns, and Teva took it up before he could. She read the first line under her breath, and her lips went on to the second before her eyes had got there.

"That's the Watch," she said. "That's Omya's sky. Which stars the moon goes over, and when." She read on. "Word for word. As far as I've got it."

Across the top of the leaf, in the founders' plain letters, it said to check the table against the sky every clear night, for as long as it took.

"She's been checking it for sixty years," <!-- @WF-omya.watch.years: 60 --> Teva said. "Every clear night. Nobody ever told her what for."

She laid the leaf on the bench, square to its edge, and kept her hand on it.

Over the benches a leaf was fixed to the wall by its corners. Its stamp said *Jump minus eleven months*, <!-- @WF-ep5.stamp: -11mo --> which made it older than anything else in the room. Benal read it aloud.

"*The engineered vector platform is not life support. It is not an aid to settlement. It is the product.*" He went on down it. "*Personnel modification is a test bed and a delivery method, nothing more. What returns is the platform.*" A name stood at the foot. "*C. Arroyo.*"

Teva turned the register on her arm and looked from the face on the leaf to the name on the wall.

"Vector," Benal said. The last plates next door had been headed with it. "Vector handling. That's the Seeding. What's on those plates is the thing they came here to make. Not to keep themselves alive with. To make." He looked at his own hands, which were too long, like the rest of him. "Every child at home has it in them."

"The upgrades are the cure," Teva said. She said it flat, to hear how it sounded.

She looked around the room at the frame and the benches and the leaves lying loose on them.

"Nothing here was ever wrapped," she said, to nobody. "The wrapping archaea are in the lineages. Year three. At home."

Benal heard what came after it and kept it to himself. The wrapping had never been the founders'. It had been grown at the Enclave.

Keo had been at a stack of leaves on the far bench, turning them over one at a time. There was a picture on the top one, and a picture was something he could look at. Now he held it up.

"Ben. I've seen that."

It was the mark from the head of the plate, cut at the top of the leaf.

"The ring with the gap in it," Keo said. "And the line across. The night I fell, under the overhang. It was right in front of my eyes, sharp, like that." He put the leaf down. "I thought it was the fever. I didn't tell anybody. What was there to tell?"

Benal took the stack from him and went through it with the glass, from the bottom up. The bottom leaves were the station's log, day after day of it, later than the one Teva had read in the archive. In among them a line said that two wigs had been lost to the sky, and the drones with them, whatever wigs and drones were. He went on past it.

The top leaves had the mark at the head of every one. They had set what they called the apparatus to go back instead of forward. They had made ready. They had counted who would go, name by name.

"They set it to go back," he said.

"Home?" Teva said.

"No. Back. The other way." He turned to the last of them. "Further back than here."

"How far?" Keo said.

He turned it over. Its back was bare.

"It doesn't say. It stops."

He laid the leaf beside the plate, mark beside mark. Keo's ring. Teva's woman at the door. His own term, in his sleep, before he had ever seen it cut.

"It's a drawing," he said. "The mark, Keo. The ring is time, going on. The gap is where you leave it." He drew the line across with his finger, in the dust over his own equation. "And that's the way back across."

Keo looked at the line in the dust. Teva looked at Benal.

"Nobody's been dreaming," he said. "Not you, not me, not Keo. Something was done here. Here, at this place. And what we've been seeing is what it did to the place."

"Since when?" Teva said.

"Since the night Keo fell. All of us."

Keo was looking past him at the frame at the end of the room, its boxes opened and their insides pulled out.

"It's like they just left," he said. "They had all of it. That, and the cure, and the plates, and everything. And they went the wrong way." His voice went up. "Why couldn't they just go back?"

His father called all of this dead symbols. Marek had once said, in passing, of something nobody had looked at yet: *It isn't anywhere yet, Ben. It's only what might be, and you can't go to a place that isn't there.* Benal had not thought it worth arguing with.

"Going home isn't a direction," he said. "It's a place. You have to have b-been there."

Teva put her finger on the plate, under the R in its brackets. "And this?"

"R is whoever goes," he said. "Put a founder in and it gives a day. Put me in—" He spread his fingers in the dust. "And it gives nothing."

The rest of it was there, all of it. His mouth would not hold it. He started it and lost it and started it again. His chest caught under the ribs where the constrictor had had him.

"They c-could have gone home. Any one of them. They'd b-been there."

"And us?" Teva said.

He had to stop and breathe. "Nobody b-born here has."

"The cure's in every child at home, and it was meant to go back, and nobody alive can carry it, and the sky's got seventeen years. At the most." <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y -->

None of it broke in his mouth.

Teva closed the register on her arm.

"And nobody told us," Keo said. "Any of it."

Benal looked from one face to the other and saw what they had heard: a thing kept from them. His own line still lay in the dust beside the plate. What he had heard was a job, and there was nobody alive to do it.
