Keo tied the cord around her waist himself, twice around, with a knot she did not know. Then Benal, the same knot, several body lengths of cord behind her, the slack looped over her shoulder. Then Keo took the end and wrapped it once around his fist. He did not tie it.

He showed her the tail of her knot. "Pull that and it comes free. Try it." It came free, and he tied it again.

She looked at his fist and understood. If something took him, he could open his hand. If he were tied, whatever took him would take the cord, and the cord would take them. He did not look at her.

"Keo," she said.

"Watch the cow."

The cow stood at the edge of the pool fifty paces upstream, knee-deep, drinking, a pale scar along her flank. The rest of the herd waited in the shade behind her. Beside Teva on the first stone, Keo stood with his eyes half shut.

The cow took a step. Drank. Took another. The water came up her legs to her belly. She lifted her head and stood there, streaming, and Teva could see her wanting to turn back. She took another step instead, and another, until the bottom dropped away and she was swimming, her great head up, out in the deep slow middle.

The blood had gone out of Keo's face. He breathed through his teeth.

Below them a low wake turned on the river. Then another, upstream of the pool, and another, out of the far reeds, all turning the same way. Benal had counted thirty-two yesterday. She could not count these.

The cow began to scream.

High and huge and bellowing at once, and it went on. On the bank the herd broke. The ones at the edge shoved back through the shallows, the ones behind crowded down and were shoved in turn, all of them honking, churning the water to mud and foam along the whole bank.

"Now," Keo said.

He opened the pot. The smell hit her in the back of the throat, carrion over something sharper: the Barrier at home. He smeared it with two fingers on the back of her neck and her hands, then on Benal's, then on his own face and neck and arms. He scraped the rest onto his chest, because nobody was going to leave any.

Then they went in.

The first step off the stone was the worst thing she had ever done. Everything she had been taught since she could walk rose up in her and said *no*. The water climbed her legs, warm. She found the bottom with her feet and the next stone with her hands, and held on.

Waist. Chest. The current leaned on her, steady and hard, and she leaned back into it. Her feet knew where they were even when she could not see them; they always had. Behind her the cord went taut and slack and taut: Benal. Behind him, Keo on the skins.

They walked where there was bottom and held the stones. Nobody spoke. Nobody kicked.

The suit gripped her everywhere, tight and hot. The river was cooler than she was, and it made no difference. Her face was out in the sun, and sweat ran into her eyes where she could not wipe it.

Then the first chute: dark fast water between two stones a few body lengths apart. She could see the bottom right up to the edge, and then nothing. The next stone stood downstream.

You did not swim a chute. You let it put you where it put you, and you grabbed.

She let go.

The river took her. No weight, nothing under her feet, the cord dragging at her waist. The water closed over her face, warm, in her mouth and nose, tasting of mud, and she could not breathe. Every story she had ever been told was true. Then the suit brought her up, enough and no more, and she came up gasping at the sky.

The next stone slammed her shoulder. She got her arms around it.

Benal was in the fast water on the end of the cord, and the current wanted to take him past. She braced her feet against the rock under the surface and hauled. Hand over hand she hauled, and her shoulder, the one the snake had thrown her on, burned, and she hauled.

He came out of the chute against the stone beside her with his eyes shut, and she got a fist in the front of his suit.

"Don't kick," she whispered. "Hold on. I've got you."

"I know," he said, eyes still shut. He was shaking so hard she felt it through her fist. He had not kicked once, with the water over his face. Every part of her had wanted to.

Then Keo came through the chute on the skins and hit the same stone, the cord still wrapped around his fist.

"Tie it," she whispered.

"Go," he said.

Their own small ripples slid away downstream, swept off by the quick water and gone. *They find you by the surface,* she had told the yazhi, a hundred times. Upstream the cow screamed and the herd churned the shallows, and the pool was the loudest thing in the world.

*Don't splash.*

The screaming went higher, broke, and went on.

Then the blood came down through the narrows, brown threads at first, then clouds, red in the clear shallows around her legs. The water smelled of it. A breath of wind came off the near bank, the only one all day, and broken ginkgo leaves skidded out onto the still water above the narrows and lay there, turning.

She held the stone and watched the blood go past. She did not think anything at all.

She was hugging the last stone before the second chute, Benal behind her, getting her breath, when something passed along her leg under the water.

Rough, and long, and it went on for a long time.

A log, maybe. Logs came down rivers. She could not look. The drill said *do not move*. She held the stone with her cheek against it, and the cord behind her went still too. Then it was gone.

A body length upstream the water broke. A ridged back came up through it, streaming, longer than she was, and went on upstream without turning.

It had not been for her.

Her grip went weak on the stone. The back went on toward the animal Keo had sent out into the pool.

Then she let go into the second chute, into the water it had come out of, because there was no way to know if there was another, and staying was worse.

The chute put her on the stone below. She hauled Benal in. They crossed the last shallows, and then there was mud, stinking, her hand flat in it and then her knees.

She went up the mud and the steep bank on hands and feet, the cord dragging at her waist. At the top she turned, dug in her heels, and hauled while Benal climbed, until he was over the edge beside her, face down in the ferns.

Noli came running along the top of the bank and stopped short a body length off. She backed away from the gel, sat down, and whined.

"You're out," Teva said to Benal. "Untie. We need the cord."

He did not move. Then he did. His fingers would not work the knot, so she did it for him.

She took the cord in both hands and turned around.

Keo was still in the water.

He was in the second chute, awkward on the skins, slow. The water around him was brown and red. Upstream, the screaming had stopped.

His skins slipped. One went sideways out from under his arm and he went down after it. The river closed over his head.

The cord went slack in her hands.
