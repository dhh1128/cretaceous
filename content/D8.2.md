Keo tied the cord around her waist himself, twice around and a knot she did not know, and pulled on it to show her it would hold. Then around Benal, the same knot, with an arm's length of cord between them. Then he took the end in his own hand and wrapped it once around his fist. He did not tie it.

She looked at his hand, and understood it, and wished she had not. If something took him, he could open his fist. If it was tied, whatever took him would take the cord too, and the cord would take them. He did not look back at her.

"Keo," she said.

"Watch the cow."

She watched the cow. It stood at the edge of the pool fifty paces upstream, up to its knees, a big gray-green animal with a pale scar along its flank, drinking. The rest of the herd stood in the shade behind it. Keo stood on the first stone beside her with his eyes half shut, and nothing happened that she could see.

Then the cow took a step forward. It stopped to drink and took another. The water came up its legs to its belly. It lifted its head and stood there, streaming, and she thought it would turn back now; she could see it wanting to. It took another step instead, and another, out past where it had meant to stop, until the bottom went out from under it and it was swimming, slowly, its great head up, out in the middle of the slow deep water where it could not easily get back.

Keo's face had gone gray. He was breathing through his teeth.

Something moved on the river below them. A low dark wake, turning. Then another, upstream of the pool, and another, out of the reeds on the far side, all of them turning the same way. She had not known there were so many. She had known there were sixteen. She had not known what sixteen looked like when they all moved at once.

The cow began to scream.

It was a sound she had never heard an animal make, high and huge and bellowing at once, and it went on. On the bank the rest of the herd broke. They came crowding down out of the shade and into the shallows along the pool's edge, all of them at once, honking, shoving, the water going brown and white under them all along the bank.

"Now," Keo said.

He had the pot open. The smell came up out of it and hit her in the back of the throat, rank, carrion over something sharper, the smell of the Barrier at home. He scooped it with two fingers and smeared it on the back of her neck and on her hands, fast, and then on Benal's. He did his own face and neck and arms last. Then he scraped the rest out onto his chest, because nobody was going to leave any.

Then they went in.

The first step off the stone was the worst thing she had ever done. Everything she had been taught since she could walk stood up in her and said *no*. The water came over her boots and up her legs, warm, pulling at her. She found the bottom with her feet and the next stone with her hands, and held on.

Waist-deep, then chest-deep. The current leaned on her the whole time, steady and hard, wanting to take her downstream, and she leaned back into it. Her feet knew where they were even when she could not see them. That was the one thing her body had always done well, and she was grateful for it now, stone by stone, foot by foot. Behind her the cord went taut and slack and taut. Benal was there. She could feel him being there, the weight of him on the line, not kicking. Behind him came Keo, on the skins.

Nobody spoke, and nobody kicked. They walked where there was bottom and they held the stones.

The suit pressed on her everywhere, tight and hot. The river was cooler than she was, and it made no difference. She was hot from the inside, from her own heart going and her own breath. Her face was out in the sun at the top of the water, and the sweat ran into her eyes where she could not wipe it.

Then the first chute.

The water ran dark and fast between two stones a few body lengths apart, and there was no bottom in it. She could see the bottom right up to the edge and then nothing. The next stone stood below it, downstream, waiting.

You did not swim a chute. You let it put you where it put you, and you grabbed.

She let go.

The river took her. For a moment she was nothing, no weight, nothing under her feet, the cord dragging at her waist, the stone gone. The water closed over her face. It was in her mouth and her nose and it was warm and it tasted of mud and she could not breathe. Every story she had ever been told was true. Then the suit brought her up, slowly, enough and no more, and she came up gasping with her face to the sky.

The next stone hit her in the shoulder. She got her arms around it.

She braced. The cord was running out behind her, hard, with Benal on the end of it in the fast water, and the current had him and wanted to take him past. She put her feet against the rock below the surface and hauled. Hand over hand she hauled, and her shoulder, the one the snake had thrown her on, screamed, and she hauled.

He came out of the chute against the stone beside her, white, his eyes shut, and she got a fist in the front of his suit and held him there.

"Don't kick," she whispered. "Hold on. I've got you."

"I know," he said, without opening his eyes. He was shaking so hard she could feel it through her fist. He had not kicked once. Every part of him had wanted to, she knew, because every part of her had, and he had lain still on the end of the cord with the water over his face and let it take him, because she had told him to.

Then Keo, on the skins, came through the chute behind them and hit the same stone. His end of the cord was still wrapped around his fist.

"Tie it," she whispered.

"Go," he said.

Their own small ripples went away from them downstream. She could see them go, tiny, swept off by the quick water and gone. *They find you by the surface,* she had said to the yazhi, with her own mouth, a hundred times. *Whatever moves the water.* Upstream the cow was still screaming and the herd was churning the shallows along the whole length of the bank, and the pool was the loudest thing in the world.

She did not think any of this in words. She thought *don't splash*.

The screaming changed. It went higher and broke and went on.

A little after that the blood came down. It came through the narrows in long brown threads at first, then in clouds, red in the clear shallows around her legs. The water smelled of it. A breath of wind came off the near bank, the only one all day, and a scatter of broken ginkgo leaves skidded out onto the flat water above the narrows and lay there, turning.

She held the stone and watched the blood go past her and did not think anything at all.

They went on, shallows and stones and the cord. The water pushed them a little farther down with every stone, so that the far bank came at them on a long slant, and she did not care where on it they came out, only that they came out.

Then the last stone before the second chute.

She was holding it with both arms, Benal behind her, getting her breath, when something passed along her leg under the water.

It was rough and long, and it went on for a long time.

It might have been a log. Logs came down rivers. She could not tell and she could not look, and the drill said *do not move*, so she did not move. She held the stone with her cheek against it and did not move, and on the cord behind her Benal felt her go still and went still himself. The rough thing went on along her leg, on and on, and was gone.

A body length upstream, the water broke. A ridged back came up through it, dark and wet, longer than she was, and went on upstream toward the blood without turning.

It had not been for her.

The relief was so big it frightened her. It had not been for her. It had been going to the animal Keo had sent out into the pool.

Then she let go into the second chute, into the water it had come out of, because there was no way to know if there was another, and staying was worse.

The chute took her and put her on the stone below. She hauled Benal in. The last shallows. Then mud, slick and black and stinking, with her hand flat on it and then her knees. She was out of the river and climbing.

She did not stop; nobody stopped near the water. She went up the mud and up the steep bank on her hands and feet with the cord dragging at her waist. At the top she turned and dug her heels in and hauled while Benal climbed. She did not let herself stop until he was over the edge beside her, face down in the ferns.

Noli came running along the top of the bank and stopped short a body length off. She backed away from the smell of the gel on their skin, sat down, and made a small noise.

"You're out," Teva said to Benal. "Untie. We need the cord."

He did not move. Then he did. His fingers would not work the knot, so she did it for him.

She took the cord in both hands and turned around.

Keo was still in the water.

He was in the second chute, last, the skins awkward under him, slower than either of them had been. He was the last one in the water, and the water around him was brown and red. Upstream, the screaming had stopped.

His skins slipped, and she saw it happen. One went sideways out from under his arm and he went down after it. The river closed over his head.

The cord went slack in her hands.
