Noli was waiting outside the fence when the light came, resting with her legs folded under her twenty paces off, where the reek of the gel thinned out. Keo climbed out over the low place in the fence and went to her.

She held her shoulder stiffly. The two holes had crusted over. He put his hand on her neck, and what came up the bond was hunger.

"Me too," he told her.

The first shower hissed in the crowns while they were pulling the stakes from between the rock and the trees, and then it was running off Keo's hair into his eyes. Benal kicked the ashes apart and scattered them into the ferns. The last strip of journey-pan was still in Keo's pouch. He left it there.

It was the first day that was only walking. Keo went first with the spike. Teva called the heading when he drifted, without looking at her arm, and Benal came behind. The showers came and went. By the second one Keo was soaked through and his boots squelched. Fern sap stung the bites on his hands until his hands were one sting. Before long his heel had begun to burn where the boot rubbed. He walked on it.

The other two walked dry inside their suits. He did not think about that.

Behind him Benal said, "Stop."

They dropped into the ferns. Keo had his spike out and his sense out past the end of it: birds, small and high, and a grounder somewhere underground. Benal crouched at his back with his head cocked and his eyes shut.

Nothing came.

"It was large," Benal said at last. "I think. Possibly a branch coming down. A long way off." He opened his eyes. "Sorry."

"Better a branch than a pack," Keo said. "Up."

Benal had been right about the razortails and right about the frogs. Keo had stopped thinking of him as somebody who could be wrong. He thought about it now, for a few steps.

Then the snails. They were out on everything after the rain, up the fern stems and under the fronds, glistening. Keo knew where to look before he had decided to look. He picked them off two and three at a time, showed Teva where they hid under the leaves, and showed Benal how to pull without crushing the shell. At home his family had gone out for snails after rain since he could walk, all four of them.

There was a hollow to their left where the water had pooled. He kept them on the high side of it without saying so. At the edge he put the butt of his spike into the puddle, and it went in ankle-deep and touched leaves. Then he went on picking.

Teva was standing still, looking down at the puddle, her face closed and careful.

"How deep does that get," she said, "after a real rain?"

His stomach turned over. She knew. Everybody knew how Yara had died. Everybody knew it had been while gathering snails. He looked at the puddle and not at her.

"Deeper than this," he said. "We won't be here for it."

She nodded once and went back to the stems, a little farther from the water.

After that Benal talked about snails.

"How far do you think one goes in a night?" He had one on his palm, pulled back into its shell. "They're always somewhere else in the morning, so either it's farther than it looks or there are way more of them than anybody counts. And do they have teeth? I've never found out."

"They don't bite," Keo said.

"That's not the same thing."

Keo let him go on, and didn't hear much of the rest.

Nearly mid-morning Benal stopped them again. This time Keo heard it too: something big crashing through the understory close by, then a thud, and another. They went flat. Noli dropped beside Keo with every feather down. Keo pushed his sense toward it. One animal, large and busy, coming no closer.

He raised his head a little, then a little more, and looked through the fronds.

It was a bird. A bird half again as tall as he was, black-feathered, on long scaly legs, with a crest of bone standing up on its head like a shell set on edge. It stood under a tree with its back to them, hooked a branch with the claws on one hand and shook it, and fruit came down. It bent and ate the fruit off the ground with a great curved beak, and reached for the branch again.

Keo watched it do that three times. Something rose in his chest that he could not keep down. He pressed his face into his arm and shook.

Teva's lips were pressed tight, and he could not tell whether she was trying not to laugh. Benal lay between them with his face in the ferns. "A bird," he said into them. "I stopped us for a bird."

Noli had decided it was food. Keo felt the want of it come up the bond, bright and simple, and she was already creeping forward when he caught her. He pushed *down-stay-good-pack* at her, hard. She sank, sullen, and glared at him over her shoulder.

The bird had a beak that could take his arm off. Instead of walking up to it Keo pushed, not at it but beside it: the picture of fruit somewhere else, more of it, and nobody near. It cost him a dull pressure behind the eyes. The bird lifted its head, looked around with one yellow eye, and walked off through the trees as if it had thought of going all by itself.

Under the tree Keo found a handful of the stonefruit, split and bruised and pecked, and two that the bird had missed in the leaves. Teva cut them open, white and soft inside and full of dark seeds. She gave her suit its share of the sweetness before she ate, squeezing the pulp in her fist over the well under her ribs. Benal did the same, and Keo watched him give his suit more than he kept for himself.

"It was large," Benal said to the fruit in his hand. "I did say it was large."

The mushrooms were in a rotting stump a little farther on, pale and frilled. Keo broke one off and turned it over. In the gray light it was pink underneath, near enough, like the young ones at home.

"These are good," he said. "We eat these."

Benal was crouching beside him before he had finished. "Did you smell it?"

"I know what they are."

"Smell it anyway. Cut the root first."

Keo nicked the root with his knife and held it to his nose, mostly to show him. It smelled of rotten wood.

"The ones we eat at home smell like leche when you cut the root," Benal said. "That one smells of nothing. And I think it's white underneath, not pink. It's the light. The white ones are the ones we were taught to leave alone." He pointed. "That one, and that one. Smell those. Possibly yours is fine. I don't know it."

Keo smelled the ones Benal had pointed at. Leche.

He looked at the mushroom in his hand. He was the Explorer. Knowing what was safe to eat was the training his parents had poured into him on every trip, and he had looked once, in bad light, and decided. He put it back on the stump.

"All right," he said. "You smell them. From now on everything gets smelled."

"Everything gets smelled," Benal agreed. "Twice, if it's pretty."

"You'd starve sitting on a heap of food."

"Yes," said Benal. "But slowly, and of something I could name."

They lay up when the heat came, under a big conifer. Keo propped the spikes in its roots, off the wet ground, and the rain dripped through on them anyway. The dead twigs under the crown caught from a pinch of the kindler's resin, and he laid the snails in the coals with Benal's mushrooms beside them. While they cooked he pulled off his boots. His feet had gone white and soft, wrinkled like fingers left too long in water.

Prying snails out of their shells with a knife point took most of the lay-up.

Benal ate one, then another, and stopped with the third halfway to his mouth. "They're good," he said, as though it were bad news. "That's terrible. Now we'll have to do this every day."

Keo took the last strip of journey-pan out of his pouch and broke it into three. They ate it. That was the end of what they had brought.

Benal had gone still over his arm, the way he did before numbers. Keo didn't want to hear it.

"Fourteen kilometers since the tangle," Benal said. "Eighty to Genesis, so sixty-six to go. If we keep this pace, which we possibly won't, that's seven days. Possibly eight." He turned his hand over and looked at the empty palm. "And for food we have what we found this morning. Which we've eaten."

Teva didn't look up from her snail. "I know."

The rain started again overhead.

Keo had the words ready. He had always had them ready. *We'll be fine.* He opened his mouth, and the rain dripped, and Benal looked at him, waiting, and so did Teva.

He closed his mouth and picked up another snail.
