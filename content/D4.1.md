Noli was waiting outside the fence when the light came, sitting on her haunches twenty paces off, where the reek of the gel thinned out. She would not come any closer to it. Keo climbed out over the low place and went to her.

She held her shoulder stiffly. The two holes had crusted over. He put his hand on her neck, and what came up the bond was hunger, plain and total, the only thing in her this morning.

"Me too," he told her.

The first shower came through while they were pulling the stakes. It hissed in the crowns before it reached them, and then it was running off Keo's hair into his eyes. Teva kept working. Benal kicked the ashes apart until there was nothing to show that anybody had slept there. The strip of journey-pan was still in Keo's pouch. He felt it there when he slung the pack, and left it where it was.

Then they walked.

It was the first day that was only walking: no fog, no pack in it, no croc in the horsetail, only the next step into the ferns and the step after that. Keo went first with the spear. Teva called the heading off her arm when he drifted, and Benal came behind, ducking where the fronds were low. The showers came and went. Keo was wet through by the second one and stayed wet, and his boots squelched at every step. Where he pushed the ferns aside their sap stung the bites on his hands, a small hot sting each time, until his hands were one sting. Before long a place on his left heel had begun to burn at every step, where the wet boot rubbed. He walked on it.

The other two walked dry inside their suits. He did not think about that.

Behind him Benal said, "Stop."

They went down into the ferns without a word. Keo had his spear out and his sense out past the end of it. He found birds, small and high, and a grounder in a burrow. He waited. Teva waited beside him. Benal crouched at his back with his head cocked and his eyes shut.

Nothing came.

"It was large," Benal said at last. "I think. Possibly it was a branch coming down. Possibly a long way off." He opened his eyes. "I'm sorry."

They got up. Benal had been right about the razortails and right about the frogs. Keo had stopped thinking of him as somebody who could be wrong. He thought about it now, for a few steps, and then the snails took it out of his head.

They were out on everything after the rain, up the fern stems and under the fronds, brown and slow. Keo knew where to look before he had decided to look. He crouched and picked them off the stems two and three at a time. He showed Teva where they hid under the leaves, and Benal how to pull without crushing the shell. His hands knew all of it. They had gone out for snails after every rain since he could walk, all four of them. Yara had been six.

There was a low place to their left where the water had pooled. He kept them on the high side of it without saying so. When he came to the edge he put the butt of his spear into the puddle, and it went in to the depth of his ankle and touched leaves. Then he took it out and went on picking.

Teva was standing still, looking down at the wet ground. She had stopped picking. Her face had gone closed and careful, and his stomach turned over, because he understood that she knew. Everybody knew how Yara had died. Everybody knew it had been while gathering snails. He waited for her to say it, and she did not. She went back to the stems and picked, a little further from the water than before.

After that nobody said anything for a long time, and Keo did not notice the silence until it was already old.

It was nearly the middle of the morning when Benal stopped them again. This time Keo heard it too. Something big was going through the understory close by, not caring who heard. Then came a thud, and another. They went flat. Noli went flat beside Keo with every feather down. Keo pushed his sense toward the noise. One animal, large and busy, was not interested in them at all.

He raised his head an inch, and then another, and looked through the fronds.

It was a bird. It was a bird half again as tall as he was, black-feathered, on long scaly legs, with a crest of bone standing up on its head like a plate set on edge. It stood under a tree with its back to them. It hooked a branch with the claws on one hand and shook it, and fruit came down. Then it bent and ate the fruit off the ground with a great curved beak, and reached for the branch again.

Keo lay in the wet ferns and watched it do that three times. Something rose in his chest that he could not keep down. He pressed his face into his arm and shook.

They had lain in the mud with their hearts going, spears out, for a bird knocking fruit off a tree. Teva watched it with her lips pressed tight, and he could not tell whether she was trying not to laugh. Benal lay between them with his face in the ferns and said nothing at all.

Noli had decided it was food. Keo felt the decision come up the bond, bright and simple, and she was already creeping forward when he caught her. He pushed *down-stay-good-pack* at her, hard. She stopped and sank, sullen, and glared at him over her shoulder.

The bird had a beak like an ax and claws on its hands, and Keo was not going to walk up to it. He reached for it instead and pushed, not at it but beside it, the picture of fruit somewhere else, more of it, and nobody near. It cost him a dull pressure behind the eyes. The bird lifted its head and looked round with one yellow eye, and then it walked off through the trees as if it had thought of going all by itself.

What it had left under the tree was not much. Keo found a handful of the stonefruit, split and bruised and pecked, and two that the bird had missed in the leaves. Teva cut them up. She gave her suit its share of the sweetness before she ate. Benal did the same, and Keo watched him give his suit more than he kept for himself.

"It was large," Benal said to the fruit in his hand. "I did say it was large."

The mushrooms were in a rotting stump a little further on, pale and frilled. Keo reached for one.

"Not that one." Benal was crouching beside him before he had finished saying it. "That one, and that one, but not the one you have. Look at the underneath. The ones we eat at home are pink underneath when they're this young, and they smell of — they smell like leche, roughly, when you've just cut the root. Yours is white underneath and smells of nothing, which is not reassuring, because the ones that kill you mostly smell of nothing. Possibly yours is fine. I don't know it."

Keo looked at the mushroom in his hand. He was the Explorer. Knowing what was safe to eat was the training his parents had poured into him on every trip, and he had reached for the wrong one without even looking underneath. He put it back on the stump and picked the ones Benal pointed at.

They lay up when the heat came, under a big conifer. Keo propped the spears in its roots, off the wet ground. The rain dripped through on them anyway. He could not think of anything else to do about it. The crown had kept the dead twigs under it dry enough to catch with a pinch of resin from the kindler, and he laid the snails in the coals.

Getting a snail out of its shell was slow work with the point of a knife, and they did it for most of the lay-up.

Benal ate one, then another, and stopped with the third halfway to his mouth. "They're good," he said, as though it were bad news. "That's terrible. Now we'll have to do this every day."

Keo took the last strip of journey-pan out of his pouch and broke it into three. They ate it. That was the end of what they had brought.

Benal had been looking at his arm. He had a way of going still before he said something with numbers in it. Keo saw him do it now and found that he did not want to hear it.

"Fourteen kilometers since the tangle," Benal said. "Eighty to Genesis, so sixty-six to go. If we keep this pace, which we possibly won't, that's seven days. Possibly eight." He turned his hand over and looked at the empty palm. "And for food we have what we found this morning. Which we've eaten."

Teva did not look up from the snail she was working at. "I know."

The rain started again overhead.

Keo had the words ready. He had always had them ready. *We'll be fine.* He opened his mouth, and the rain dripped, and Benal looked at him, waiting, and so did Teva.

He closed his mouth and picked up another snail.
