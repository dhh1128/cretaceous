Noli was waiting outside the fence when the light came, sitting on her haunches twenty paces off, where the reek of the gel thinned out. She would not come any closer to it. Keo climbed out over the low place in the fence and went to her.

She held her shoulder stiffly. The two holes had crusted over. He put his hand on her neck, and what came up the bond was hunger, plain and total, the only thing in her this morning.

"Me too," he told her.

The first shower came through while they were pulling the stakes out of the gaps between the rock and the trees. It hissed in the crowns before it reached them, and then it was running off Keo's hair into his eyes. Teva kept working. Benal kicked the ashes apart and scattered them into the ferns. The strip of journey-pan was still in Keo's pouch. He felt it there when he slung the pack, and left it where it was.

Then they walked.

It was the first day that was only walking: no fog, no razortails, no croc in the horsetail, only the next step into the ferns and the step after that. Keo went first with the spear. Teva called the heading when he drifted, without looking at her arm, and Benal came behind, ducking where the fronds were low. The showers came and went. Keo was wet through by the second one and stayed wet, and his boots squelched at every step. Where he pushed the ferns aside their sap stung the bites on his hands, a small hot sting each time, until his hands were one sting. Before long a place on his heel had begun to burn at every step, where the wet boot rubbed. He walked on it.

The other two walked dry inside their suits. He did not think about that.

Behind him Benal said, "Stop."

They went down into the ferns without a word. Keo had his spear out and his sense out past the end of it. He found birds, small and high, and a grounder somewhere underground. He waited. Teva waited beside him. Benal crouched at his back with his head cocked and his eyes shut.

Nothing came.

"It was large," Benal said at last. "I think. Possibly it was a branch coming down. Possibly a long way off." He opened his eyes. "I'm sorry."

"Better a branch than a pack," Keo said. "Up."

They got up. Benal had been right about the razortails and right about the frogs. Keo had stopped thinking of him as somebody who could be wrong. He thought about it now, for a few steps, and then the snails took it out of his head.

They were out on everything after the rain, up the fern stems and under the fronds, brown and slow. Keo knew where to look before he had decided to look. He crouched and picked them off the stems two and three at a time. He showed Teva where they hid under the leaves, and Benal how to pull without crushing the shell. His hands knew all of it. At home his family had gone out for snails after rain since he could walk, all four of them. Yara had been six.

There was a hollow to their left where the water had pooled. He kept them on the high side of it without saying so. When he came to the edge he put the butt of his spear into the puddle, and it went in to the depth of his ankle and touched leaves. Then he took it out and went on picking.

Teva was standing still, looking down at the wet ground. She had stopped picking. Her face had gone closed and careful.

"How deep does that get," she said, "after a real rain?"

His stomach turned over, because he understood that she knew. Everybody knew how Yara had died. Everybody knew it had been while gathering snails. He looked at the puddle and not at her.

"Deeper than this," he said. "We won't be here for it."

She nodded once and went back to the stems, a little further from the water than before.

After that Benal talked about snails.

"How far do you think one goes in a night?" He had one on his palm, pulled back into its shell. "Nobody's ever been able to tell me. They're always somewhere else in the morning, so either it's further than it looks or there are a great many more of them than anybody counts. And I've never found out whether they have teeth. Do they have teeth?"

"They don't bite," Keo said.

"That isn't the same thing at all."

Keo let him go on, and did not hear much of the rest.

It was nearly the middle of the morning when Benal stopped them again. This time Keo heard it too. Something big was going through the understory close by, not caring who heard. Then came a thud, and another. They went flat. Noli went flat beside Keo with every feather down. Keo pushed his sense toward the noise. One animal, large and busy. The noise came no closer.

He raised his head an inch, and then another, and looked through the fronds.

It was a bird. It was a bird half again as tall as he was, black-feathered, on long scaly legs, with a crest of bone standing up on its head like a plate set on edge. It stood under a tree with its back to them. It hooked a branch with the claws on one hand and shook it, and fruit came down. Then it bent and ate the fruit off the ground with a great curved beak, and reached for the branch again.

Keo lay in the wet ferns and watched it do that three times. Something rose in his chest that he could not keep down. He pressed his face into his arm and shook.

They had lain in the mud with their hearts going, spears out, for a bird knocking fruit off a tree. Teva watched it with her lips pressed tight, and he could not tell whether she was trying not to laugh. Benal lay between them with his face in the ferns. "A bird," he said into them. "I stopped us for a bird."

Noli had decided it was food. Keo felt the want of it come up the bond, bright and simple, and she was already creeping forward when he caught her. He pushed *down-stay-good-pack* at her, hard. She stopped and sank, sullen, and glared at him over her shoulder.

The bird had a beak that could take his arm off and claws on its hands, and Keo was not going to walk up to it. He reached for it instead and pushed, not at it but beside it, the picture of fruit somewhere else, more of it, and nobody near. It cost him a dull pressure behind the eyes. The bird lifted its head and looked around with one yellow eye, and then it walked off through the trees as if it had thought of going all by itself.

What it had left under the tree was not much. Keo found a handful of the stonefruit, split and bruised and pecked, and two that the bird had missed in the leaves. Teva cut them open. Inside they were white and soft, with dark seeds all through them. She gave her suit its share of the sweetness before she ate, squeezing the pulp in her fist over the well under her ribs. Benal did the same, and Keo watched him give his suit more than he kept for himself.

"It was large," Benal said to the fruit in his hand. "I did say it was large."

The mushrooms were in a rotting stump a little further on, pale and frilled. Keo broke one off and turned it over. It was pink underneath, near enough, in the gray light, like the young ones at home.

"These are good," he said. "We eat these."

Benal was crouching beside him before he had finished saying it. "Did you smell it?"

"I know what they are."

"Smell it anyway. Cut the root first."

Keo nicked the root with his knife and held it to his nose, mostly to show him. It smelled of wet wood.

"The ones we eat at home smell like leche, roughly, when you've just cut the root," Benal said. "That one smells of nothing. And I think it's white underneath, not pink. It's the light. The white ones are the ones we were taught to leave alone." He pointed. "That one, and that one. Smell those. Possibly yours is fine. I don't know it."

Keo smelled the ones Benal had pointed at, and they smelled of leche.

He looked at the mushroom in his hand. He was the Explorer. Knowing what was safe to eat was the training his parents had poured into him on every trip, and he had looked once, in bad light, and decided. He put it back on the stump.

"All right," he said. "You smell them. From now on everything gets smelled."

"Everything gets smelled," Benal agreed. "Twice, if it's pretty."

"You'd starve sitting on a heap of food."

"Yes," said Benal. "But slowly, and of something I could name."

They lay up when the heat came, under a big conifer. Keo propped the spears in its roots, off the wet ground. The rain dripped through on them anyway. He could not think of anything else to do about it. The crown had kept the dead twigs under it dry enough to catch with a pinch of resin from the kindler, and he laid the snails in the coals with Benal's mushrooms beside them. While they cooked he pulled off his boots. The skin of his feet had gone white and soft, wrinkled like fingers left too long in water.

Getting a snail out of its shell was slow work with the point of a knife, and they did it for most of the lay-up.

Benal ate one, then another, and stopped with the third halfway to his mouth. "They're good," he said, as though it were bad news. "That's terrible. Now we'll have to do this every day."

Keo took the last strip of journey-pan out of his pouch and broke it into three. They ate it. That was the end of what they had brought.

Benal had been looking at his arm. He had a way of going still before he said something with numbers in it. Keo saw him do it now and found that he did not want to hear it.

"Fourteen kilometers since the tangle," Benal said. "Eighty to Genesis, so sixty-six to go. If we keep this pace, which we possibly won't, that's seven days. Possibly eight." He turned his hand over and looked at the empty palm. "And for food we have what we found this morning. Which we've eaten."

Teva did not look up from the snail she was working at. "I know."

The rain started again overhead.

Keo had the words ready. He had always had them ready. *We'll be fine.* He opened his mouth, and the rain dripped, and Benal looked at him, waiting, and so did Teva.

He closed his mouth and picked up another snail.
