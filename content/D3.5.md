The fire had burned down to a red seam, and the others were asleep.

Teva sat with her back to the rock and the spike across her knees, and listened to them. Keo's breathing had gone slow and even. Benal lay curled toward the coals, and twice he said something in his sleep that was not a word. Past the fence the night went about its business. Once something heavy pushed through the ferns beyond the stakes. It stopped near the reek of the gelled fence, and then it went on. She sat very still until she could not hear it any more.

With the fire down to coals she could make out the stars at the edges of the gap in the crowns, and knew that some of them were still past her, as they always were.

Omya's question came back to her as she looked up. She had not been able to answer it then. Tonight she was the only one awake to try.

She started where Omya always started, with the Organs, under her breath. The bright pair was there in the gap. The rest of the field ran behind the crowns, so she gave its stars their places from memory, and counted the faintest of them, which she had never seen from anywhere. Then Trinity's four. She named the ones the leaves hid as readily as the ones overhead.

Then the field after that. Then the next. Her lips moved on the counts. It came easily, the way the first positions of the protocol always did, a long way, further than she had ever needed it to go. She could still hear Omya reciting while Teva plaited her hair, even and sure, one field and then the next.

After the sixth field came the seventh. She had its name. Its count would not come.

She waited for it. She could feel the shape of the place where it ought to be, the way you can feel a step in the dark that is not where your foot expects. It did not come.

She went back to the beginning, the Organs and Trinity and on. She went faster this time, the counts falling over each other, because if she came at it with enough speed she would carry straight through. She came to the seventh field and stopped at the same name, without its count.

She did not try a third time.

It was not that she had forgotten it. You could not forget a thing you had never had. Omya had given it to her a hundred nights, in the same even voice, and somewhere past the sixth field Teva had always let her go on alone, because it was the useless half, and there would always be tomorrow night, and Omya would always be there to say it again. Her grandmother still held the rest of it. All of it. Teva did not know how long she would hold it. No one else living held the whole.

For the rest of her watch she checked the patch of sky between the crowns, and the fence, and the dark beyond it, and the sky again. Stars went out of the gap and others came into it. There was nothing new in the fields she had, that she could see. She did not know whether that meant anything. She watched anyway.

When her watch was done she went across the ring and put her hand on Benal's shoulder. He woke all at once, frightened, and then saw it was her.

"Your watch," she said.

She lay down by the coals with her back to them both, and did not tell him anything.
