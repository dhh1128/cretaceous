Benal rubbed out the last line of the term with the side of his thumb and looked at what was left.

What was left was most of an equation, written across the board in gypsum chalk. The first part he could have written in his sleep, and some nights he nearly had. It ran clean and certain from one edge of the board almost to the other, the founders' own figures, the ones that had brought two hundred people <!-- @WF-founders.count: 200 --> to the right place on the right world, and then it came to the part that was his, and stopped. Everything the founders had written down agreed with itself. It was only when you asked it when they had arrived, and then went out at night and looked up, that it stopped agreeing, and nobody but Benal seemed to think that was a thing worth sitting in a corner about. He had filled his part and rubbed it out more times than he could count. This morning there was a question mark in it. He had drawn that too, and he was not sure yet whether it was the most honest thing on the board or the most useless.

His knees were against the vine wall. The sleeping space behind the fern screen had been made for a boy, and he had stopped fitting it a long time before he stopped being one. He sat in it anyway, folded into the corner with the board across his thighs and gypsum dust white in the creases of his fingers.

The Enclave came in through the walls whether he wanted it or not, as it always had. That was the thing nobody understood when they called his hearing a gift. Nothing in him sorted what came in. Everything the village did arrived at once and at the same weight, near and far together, and it all had to be listened to, because there was no part of him that knew how not to. He could put his hands over his ears like anyone. Then he heard his own blood.

Under all of it, steady as breathing, came the treading at the basin: a dozen pairs of feet in the pulp, and over them somebody calling the count so they kept together, and the somebody was his mother. He would have known her voice in any crowd in the Enclave. The household's batch was going in today. She had gone down at first light to tread it with the others, and it would be days yet before any of it was pan. Nobody ate the batch before it was ready. Raw pulp let you feel fine for hours, but you still regretted it soon enough.

A small flybeak landed on the thatch above his head.

He heard the claws go in, and a dry scrape as it shifted across the thatch, testing. Benal did not look up. He reached over his head and slapped twice at the roof with his knuckles.

"No way in for you," he told it. "Your grandmother, possibly."

The scraping stopped, and then started again further along.

He knew his father's step on the platform before the screen moved. He had time to put the board down and sit up straight, and he did neither.

Marek pushed the fern screen aside with one hand. He looked at the board first. Then he looked at Benal, all of him, folded into the corner with his knees up near his chin, and then down at the bowl of pan by Benal's hip, which had been there since last night and had not been touched.

"Have you eaten?"

"I will."

Marek looked at the bowl a moment longer. Then his face softened into pity, and Benal looked at the board.

"Still staring at those dead symbols?"

"They're not —" Benal stopped and started again. "It isn't — I think I've — I've found where it —"

His father waited. He always waited, as if there were plenty of time and all of it was for Benal, and that was worse than if he had walked away, because it meant Benal had to finish.

"The founders anchored to a point in space," Benal said. "Not a point in spacetime. Everything after that follows. The anchor is — possibly — unmoored."

Marek let him finish. Then he answered something else.

"A child died last night." He said it the way he said everything that mattered, as though he were speaking to a room. "The Keepers can't make the rust-red. Omya can't remember the fold, and the two who could went in the flood, and the Seedings have stopped, and the cultures are going gray in their pots while we watch them. That is what is happening, Benal. That. Not where they anchored." He breathed. "We came out of the jump into a branch of the world, not the world they left. Nothing we solve here reaches it. It doesn't matter where the anchor is."

"It matters to the mission," Benal said. "If nobody solves the anchor, nothing can ever be sent forward. That's — that's the whole —"

Marek put a hand on his shoulder. His palm covered most of it.

Benal held still. The hand was warm, and heavy, and it stayed a long time, and he could hear his father breathing above him, slower than his own.

"Your mother's at the basin," his father said. "They're treading our batch today, and they want feet." He paused. "Not the tangle. The basin."

He took his hand away. At the screen he turned back.

"Do something real."

He did not wait for an answer. The screen fell to behind him, and his steps went across the platform and down the ladder, rung by rung, until Benal could no longer pick them out from the treading and his mother's voice calling the count over it.

The flybeak had gone quiet on the roof. The pan sat by his hip where it had been. Down at the basin the treading went on, and Benal picked up the chalk and drew the line again.
