Benal rubbed out the last line of the term with the side of his thumb and looked at what was left.

What was left was most of an equation, chalked across the board in gypsum. The founders' figures, which he could have written in his sleep, ran clean and certain almost to the far edge, the ones that had brought them to the right place on the right world. Then came the part that was his, and it stopped. Everything the founders wrote agreed with itself, until you asked it when they had arrived and then went out at night and looked up. Nobody but Benal thought that was worth sitting in a corner about. This morning there was a question mark in it, and he wasn't sure whether it was the most honest thing on the board or the most useless.

The sleeping space behind the fern screen had been made for a boy, and he had stopped fitting it a long time before he stopped being one. He sat in it anyway, folded into the corner with the board across his thighs and gypsum dust in the creases of his fingers.

The Enclave came in through the walls whether he wanted it or not. That was what nobody understood when they called his hearing a gift: nothing in him sorted it. Everything the village did arrived at once and at the same weight, near and far together. He could put his hands over his ears like anyone. Then he heard his own blood. Last night at the returning it had given him every name said, the whole way round the crowd, and Teva's from the front, so flat it hardly sounded like hers.

Under all of it, steady as breathing, came the treading at the basin: a dozen pairs of feet in the pulp, and over them somebody calling the count so they kept together, and the somebody was his mother. It would be days yet before any of their batch was pan. Nobody ate the batch before it was ready. Raw pulp let you feel fine for hours, but you still regretted it soon enough.

A small flybeak landed on the thatch above his head.

He heard the claws go in, and a dry scrape as it shifted, testing. Benal did not look up. He reached over his head and slapped twice at the roof with his knuckles.

"No way in for you," he told it. "Your grandmother, possibly."

The scraping stopped, then started again farther along.

"Ben." His friend's voice came up from the foot of the ladder. "You talking to the roof again?"

"It started it."

"The big tree ferns in the gardens are unrolling. Three at once. Come see."

"Later."

"They won't be unrolling later. That's what unrolling is." She waited, then went, quick and light. Most people their age thought she was odd. She was the only one who came to the foot of his ladder to tell him a fern was doing something, and she'd probably be back tomorrow to tell him what it had done.

He knew his father's step on the platform before the screen moved. He had time to put the board down and sit up straight, and he did neither.

Marek pushed the fern screen aside. He looked at the board first. Then at Benal, all of him, folded into the corner with his knees near his chin, and then at the bowl of pan by Benal's hip, untouched since last night.

"Have you eaten?"

"I will."

Marek looked at the bowl a moment longer. Then his face softened into pity, and Benal looked at the board.

"Still staring at those dead symbols?"

"They're not —" Benal stopped and started again. "It isn't — I think I've — I've found where it —"

His father waited. He always waited, as if there were plenty of time and all of it was for Benal, and that was worse than walking away, because it meant Benal had to finish.

"The founders anchored to a point in space," Benal said. "Not a point in spacetime. The anchor is — possibly — unmoored."

Marek let him finish. Then he answered something else.

"A child died last night." He said it the way he said everything that mattered, to the room rather than to Benal. "The Keepers can't make the rust-red. Omya can't remember the fold, the two who could went in the flood, the Seedings have stopped, and the cultures are going gray in their pots, one in three of them already. That is what is happening, Benal. Not where they anchored. We came out of the jump into a branch of the world, not the world they left. Nothing we solve here reaches it."

"It matters to the mission," Benal said. "Even if you're right about the branch. Say it's one chance in three you're not, and then it's the only thing that matters at all. If nobody solves the anchor, nothing can ever be sent forward. That's — that's the whole —"

Marek put a hand on his shoulder. His palm covered most of it.

Benal held still. The hand was warm and heavy, and it stayed a long time. He could hear his father breathing above him, slower than his own.

"Your mother's at the basin," his father said. "They're treading our batch today, and they want feet. Not the tangle. The basin."

The tangle was where Keo was every dawn, with that animal of his. Benal had run from it the first time he saw it, and somebody had seen.

Marek took his hand away. At the screen he turned back.

"Do something real."

He didn't wait for an answer. The screen fell to behind him, and his steps went down the ladder, rung by rung, until Benal couldn't pick them out from the treading and his mother's count.

The flybeak had gone quiet. The pan sat by his hip. Benal picked up the chalk and drew the line again.
