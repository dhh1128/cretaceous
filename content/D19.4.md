The wig's shadow raced along the water beside them. Keo's hands had stuck to the stick where the knuckles had opened, and every time the hull bucked on the air the rib caught him under the arm. The coast was gone behind the tail. Where the plain had been there was brown water from one side of the world to the other, with fern and palms standing up out of it to their crowns.

The propulsor filled the cabin. It came up through the seat and the floor into his teeth, and anything anybody said had to be shouted over it.

Somewhere over the plain a weight he had carried in his head since Genesis lifted, and he noticed only that something had gone.

Benal leaned forward between the seats with his eyes on the panel. "Call it ninety-five now, by its figure," he shouted. "Home's eighty, and the wall after. Ten over. Roughly."

Ten was what they had to spend on everything that was not flat water.

"State the cost of a climb to ten meters," Keo said. "Against a kilometer flat."

"Climb to ten meters from cruise: about one point two percent of charge. Cruise, per kilometer: about zero point eight percent."

"A climb's a kilometer and a half, then," Benal said. "Every one."

A palm crown came at the windscreen out of the glare and went by the wingtip on Teva's side, close enough that she jerked back in her straps.

Somewhere under the spread was the ground he had carried her over, and he could not tell which water it was.

"Left," Teva said. "More."

He lifted the nose first. The propulsor's note dropped as the wig climbed, and only with the height under him could he lay the wing over, because at a meter the low tip would have gone into the water. A wide water came round into the windscreen, running inland between banks that were only lines of fern.

"There. Hold that."

"Surveyed river line," the voice said. "Inland."

"That's ours," Benal said. "The narrows river. It's the river on the leaf."

All his life the river had been the one thing at home nobody went near. Keo put the wig down over the middle of it, where it rode lowest and cost least, and the note came back up to cruise.

After that she called the bends. "Cut this one. There's water all the way across." He took every one she gave him straight, the hull slapping over the dead water inside the bends and throwing spray up the windscreen.

On both banks the fern stood out of the shallows under an empty sky, and he knew what country this was.

Under them his sense was a blur of small minds, every fish and frog in the spread at once, so thick he had stopped listening to it.

A second shadow came up the water from behind and slid over theirs, wider.

In the same breath his sense found it, one mind, big and clear, overhead. He was already reaching to push at it. He stopped. He had pushed at one before, on the fern, with everything he had.

The shadow drew back along theirs and dropped onto the tail.

"Tail!" Benal shouted.

Keo hauled back. The nose came up and the note fell away under him, and he laid the wing over and felt the whole hull swing. The big shadow went over the place where theirs had been and slid off across the water. The wig came down onto the cushion with a slap that went up his spine.

Benal was twisted round in his seat at the side window. "That was a kilometer and a half." He did not turn back. "The founders lost two of these. To the sky. It was in the station's log."

"Identify the animal following this craft," Keo said.

"No identification available. Flight log, survey season. Craft lost in flight, contact ended. Second craft lost in flight, contact ended."

It read them in the voice it had used for the fill line of every jar.

Ahead, the water was moving. A saropo herd was wading out across the spread, belly-deep, their backs higher than the palm crowns round them, closing the water from bank to bank as he watched.

Over them was a climb. Round them was land.

"Right. The bank. Now."

He put the wig at the bank. The moment the fern was under the hull the cushion went out from under it. The whole craft sagged. He pulled it up into a hop before the fern could take the belly, and the note dropped and labored and the flank of the nearest saropo went by on Teva's side, close enough to see the mud drying on it. At the top of the hop the shadow was on them again. It was bigger. Then the river was under them past the herd and the hull hit it, and the spray came over the windscreen and ran off.

"It's behind us," Benal said. "Above. Higher than it was."

Over the land it had gained on them. Keo did not know how.

"Sky or water," Benal said.

"Neither," Teva said.

Benal turned back to the panel and looked at it a long time.

"Distance since the coast."

"Distance flown since landfall: about thirty-five kilometers."

"It's spent about forty since the coast, by its own figure," Benal said. "Three climbs. Four and a half. That's why it's more than we've flown." He had to stop for breath, and went on. "Its figure's for new cells. These are two hundred years old. They don't hold what it thinks they hold. And it figured the straight line. Eighty-eight. We're flying the river." He looked out at the bend coming. "Nobody counted the bends. I didn't."

"So what's over?"

"Nothing's over. We reach the wall if we don't climb again and the woods are kind." He breathed. "Possibly."

The number on the panel was the one thing in the cabin Keo could read without help. After that he watched it, and let the voice's kilometers go.

"If it doesn't reach the wall," he said. "State where this craft can come down."

"Most of the floodwater below is deep enough to set the hull down today. The flood is falling. Within days a hull set down on it will rest on mud. The river channel holds its depth. A craft set down on the channel remains operable for months."

"How do you know?"

"Not understood."

"State how you know the depth of the water."

"Depth below the hull is measured in flight. The flood level is falling at a measured rate."

"And the charge?"

"Range is calculated from rated cell capacity."

He looked down at the middle of the spread, where the water ran darker and quicker over the channel, and held the nose on it. It was the river, which his people called death, and he had it on the word of a machine they called an abomination. He did not say it. The shadow was behind them.

"Ben," he said. "How many more climbs can we buy?"

"None," Benal said. "Not with the woods to hop, and the units for the wall."

Keo looked away from the panel. The flybeak had come up abeam of them off Teva's side, low over the water, near enough to see whole past her head. Its wings were working, stroke after stroke. He had watched flybeaks from under the canopy all his life. Over open ground they went up on the warm air in long circles and hardly moved their wings at all. Over the water nothing rose to hold it up, and it was paying for every meter. Over the land it had gained on them for nothing.

"No more climbing," he said. "Not for anything. Flat and low, on the water."

Benal opened his mouth.

"Teva. Give me the deep middle. Keep us off the land, even round the bends."

She looked at the water ahead. "Wide left, then. Stay on the dark water."

"That's the long way round," Benal said. "It's most of what's left."

Keo took it. He went wide round the next bend and left the short way over the fern alone, and the bend after that, and the one after that. He was not getting away from it. He held the wig on the water and let the flybeak pay.

"It's lower," Benal said, from the side window. "Every time I look, it's lower."

Ahead the spread narrowed at a bend, and across it from bank to bank lay a pile of trunks the storm had brought down, roots and all, with the river heaped up against them.

"Trunks!" Teva said. "Right across!"

There was no water round it. There was the fern on the bank.

"Obstacle ahead. Clearance on present heading requires the lift units."

"No," Keo said.

He took it on speed, as low as the wig would go. He held the nose at the bank until the fern was under the hull and the cushion went. Then he pulled. The wig went up off its own speed, the note dropping and dropping and the fern tops going by under the belly, closer than the voice wanted. The shadow came onto theirs again, fast, from behind and above.

"It's coming down!" Benal said.

The hop came down toward the water. In the shallows ahead, at the edge where the fern gave out, a ceratops reared up out of the river, streaming, horns and the great frill lifting, and behind her a calf stood in the water to its chest. Her horns were not at the wig. They were up over it, at something behind and above him.

The shadow on the water broke off sideways.

"It pulled out!" Benal said. "It's pulled out!"

The hull hit the water. Off Teva's side the flybeak went by low over the river, laboring, every stroke of its wings dragging it a little way and no farther, and turned off for the trees on the bank.

Teva's hands stayed where they were on her knees.

"Forty-odd, by its figure," Benal said.

His voice was level. Keo had heard him stammer over a good deal less. He had barely stammered since the coast.

"The narrows," Teva said. "We're on top of them."

They were over and past before she had finished saying it. Somewhere under the flood were the stones and the chutes, and the water that had taken them two days.

Then the woods came in on both banks. The spread ran narrower between the stands, and the bends came too tight to cut. A stand of low canopy lay right across one of them, and he had to hop it and pay. At the top of the hop he could see over the trees.

"Ben. Behind us. Is it empty?"

Benal looked. "Empty."

The big conifers stood up out of the stands along the banks, twice the height the wig could climb, and came at the windscreen edge-on, faster than he could look at them. Teva called them. On foot she had never been able to see past the next stand. From here she could see the water running between them, and she read it a moment before he needed it.

"Gap, left. Then right, after the big one."

Once she called one late.

"Tree! Right!"

He had no climb to turn on. He put the wig round it flat, with the wing on her side down so low that he waited for it to catch the water. It did not.

Between the hops he looked at her. Her face was the red it had been on the fern, and there was no sweat on it at all. The suit lay on her, gray, and did nothing for her.

"Are you hot?"

"Yes."

There was nothing to give her to drink. He reached across her and found the vent on her side and pushed it open as far as it would go. The air came in on her face, hot, and it stank of mud and drowned fern. It was the smell of the groves at home after heavy rain, and he did not let himself go any further than that.

The last of the woods went by. Benal had stopped reading the figure out.

"Keep reading it, Ben."

"Under ten."

Over the last trees the tangle stood up across the whole way ahead, woven trunks with green along the top of them, fifteen meters high. Fifteen was all the wig would climb. The lift units had not run since the concrete.

"Lift units for obstacle clearance," the voice said. "One full reserve. Reserve: full."

"Five," Benal said. "Possibly less. Then the wall."
