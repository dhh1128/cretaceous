The mash sat under Benal's ribs, a weight that had not shifted since dawn. He had checked it every hundred steps, and he was well.

They were coming down off the limestone in mist so close he could see only Teva's pack and Keo's shape ahead of her. Scree slid from under his boots in small runs. Water dripped off the scrub onto the stones, every drop clear to him under their steps, and the catch in Keo's left foot that had been there since the burrow. The insects had not started yet.

Keo carried only his spike. He was shivering, though the mist was warm.

Teva's breathing changed. Benal heard it a step before she stopped. She stood with her head down, then went to her knees and was sick.

"Teva?" Keo said.

She stayed on her hands and knees until there was nothing more. Then she sat back on her heels and wiped her mouth with her wrist.

"The mash," she said.

Keo had got down beside her, his bad leg out to the side. "The four-hour batch."

Benal counted back. She had eaten at the spring in the middle of the afternoon, and the light was only now coming up through the mist.

"About fifteen hours since you ate it," he said. <!-- @WF-mash.onset: 15h -->

All three of them had trodden mash at home and waited out the days of leaching. Every child in the Enclave knew what the pulp did to people who would not wait.

The weight under his own ribs had a name now, and it was the same one.

"It's in us too," he said. "Ours had the night in the water on top of the four hours."

Keo looked up at him. "Is that enough?"

"I don't know. We'll find out."

Teva put her palm flat on the stones and pushed, and got nowhere.

"Up," she said.

She held her hand out. Keo took it, and she came up on it and stood swaying under her pack and the fieldpack.

"Slowly," Keo said.

They went on down like that, her hand in his and his spike in the other, Keo testing each step before he trusted it. Benal came behind, the razor-club knocking at his hip.

The slope eased. Short palms came out of the mist, their fronds standing straight up from the ground. Twice they passed water standing in a low place.

She stopped again and doubled over, arms across her belly. Then she let go of Keo and went off into the scrub as fast as she could walk.

Benal turned his back. That did nothing; his ears did not care which way he faced. He walked on until he could hear only some of it.

When she came back her face was gray and sweating. He unhooked the skin from her belt and pulled the stopper.

"Drink," he said. "Small mouthfuls, or it comes straight back."

"I know."

She drank. He counted the skins: three, full at dawn from two days of rain. Hers was down a third already, and most of that was going to come back up.

It did. Then she had to stop again, and again, and by the time the mist was yellowing overhead they had come a kilometer onto the flat.

The next time she straightened, her knees went, and Keo caught her. He got her arm across his shoulders and his own around her waist, his spike propped in the crook of his elbow.

"I can take her for a while," Benal said.

"I have her."

Benal let it go. The suit had never properly mended at his left elbow, where the coils had crushed it, and the arm under it was weak. Neither of them knew. If he took her weight on it, it would give, and they would both see.

They walked. Every so often she hung on Keo's shoulder and retched, with nothing left to bring up.

Once, when they were small, she had shared her lunch with him. He had not thought of it in years. He thought of it now, watching her, and he had nothing to give her back.

At the next standing water her feet stopped working altogether. Keo held her up while Benal got the packs off her.

"Get on," Keo said. He went down on his good knee with his back to her.

Benal helped her up. She was lighter than she should have been. She got her arms around Keo's neck, and he got his hands under her knees and stood, good leg first.

"Head over my shoulder," Keo said. "If it comes, let it go to the side."

Benal put Teva's pack on over his own, hung the fieldpack across his chest, and picked up both spikes.

On Keo's back, Teva had her cheek against his neck.

"You were right," she said. "About the day."

Keo hitched her higher. "Can you hold on?"

"Yes."

He started walking.

Within the hour the mist burned off. The sky went white, then blue, and the heat came down on the still plain. Benal filled her skin at a pool between the palms, and when he came back the midges had found Keo.

They came in columns out of the ferns, at his eyes and the corners of his mouth. Keo screwed up his face and blew at them; he could not let go of her to lift a hand. Benal walked beside him with the spikes in one fist and wiped Keo's face with his other palm. The midges came back. He wiped them off again.

Teva's face over Keo's shoulder had gone from gray to red. Her suit pulsed at the wrists, where it still worked, and could not get the heat out of her fast enough.

The insects were a wall now, every wing and every shrilling thing on the plain, straight into his ears. For two days the rain had covered it. It filled his head to the back of his eyes, and he had to dig under it for Keo's steps and Teva's breathing.

The capacitor in his shoulder had died two days ago, in the rain; it had been red-lined since before the tangle. The shock grid had gone with it, and the flash, and the dampening that had let him think since the morning they walked out. The weave still held him; it drew on nothing. The display on his arm still woke, because it was the living layer's own glow.

Of the three of them only he had his hands free. In them he had two spikes he could barely use and a club, and behind them a suit with nothing left.

He had told nobody. He was not going to tell them now.

"Ben," Teva said into Keo's shoulder. "How far?"

He had it all. The count on his arm, less what he reckoned each day's wandering had cost them, said seventy and a half; the First Walk had made it eighty.

"Nine or ten to the sea, maybe," he said. "Two before the heat stops us. Three, possibly. Then five this afternoon. That leaves two for tomorrow, at least. There's water in most of the low places, so water isn't the trouble."

She was quiet for a few steps.

"And you two?"

"If it gets us about when it got you, we'll be sick by nightfall. Fifteen hours from the dawn meal." He wiped Keo's face again, half-expecting someone to roll their eyes at his numbers. "Less of it, I'd think, because ours had the night." <!-- @WF-mash.onset: 15h -->

She shut her eyes against Keo's neck.

He moved out to Keo's left, a step ahead, where he could see her face. Under the insects he could hear the catch in Keo's left foot, worse than at dawn, and his breath snagging on the rib. He could hear her breathing, quick and shallow.

A little way on, Keo's left foot came down on a root under the ferns and turned. He went down on one knee with her on his back, and stayed there, head down, breath hissing through his teeth.

Benal had a hand out, full of spikes.

Keo got up.
