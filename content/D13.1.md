The mash sat under his ribs, a weight that had not shifted since dawn. Benal had checked it every hundred steps since they set out, and he was well.

They were coming down off the limestone in the mist. It lay on the slope so white and close that he could see no farther than Teva's pack ahead and Keo's shape in front of her. Scree slid from under his boots in small runs. The scrub was thinning, and water dripped off it onto the stones. He could hear every drop under their three sets of steps, and the catch in Keo's left foot that had been there since the burrow. The insects had not started yet.

Keo walked with his spike and nothing else on him. He was shivering, though the mist was warm.

Teva's breathing changed. Benal heard it a step before she stopped. She stood with her head down, then went down on her knees and was sick.

"Teva?" Keo said.

She stayed on her hands and knees until there was nothing more. Then she sat back on her heels and wiped her mouth with the back of her wrist.

"The mash," she said.

Keo had got down beside her, with his bad leg out to the side. "The four-hour batch."

Benal counted back. She had eaten at the spring in the middle of the afternoon, and the light was only now coming up through the mist.

"It's about fifteen hours since you ate it," he said. <!-- @WF-mash.onset: 15h -->

All three of them had trodden mash at home and waited out the days of leaching with everyone else. Every child in the Enclave knew what the pulp did to people who would not wait.

The weight under his own ribs had a name now, and it was the same one.

"It's in us too," he said. "Ours had the whole night in the water on top of the four hours."

Keo looked up at him. "Is that enough?"

"I don't know. We'll find out."

Teva put her palm flat on the stones and pushed, and did not get anywhere.

"Up," she said.

She held her hand out. Keo took it, and she came up on it and stood swaying with her pack and the fieldpack on her back.

"Slowly," Keo said.

They went on down the slope like that, her hand in his and his spike in the other, Keo testing each step before he trusted his weight to it. Benal came behind with his pack, and the razor-club knocking at his hip.

The slope eased. Palms came out of the mist on either side, short ones with their fronds standing straight up from the ground. Between them grew ferns, and a gray scrub that smelled of salt when he brushed it. Twice they passed water standing in a low place, flat and brown.

She stopped again and doubled over with her arms across her belly. Then she let go of Keo and went off into the scrub as fast as she could walk.

Benal turned his back. That did nothing; his ears did not care which way he faced. He walked on a little way until he could hear only some of it.

When she came back her face was gray and wet. He unhooked the skin from her belt and pulled the stopper.

"Drink," he said. "Small mouthfuls, or it comes straight back."

"I know."

She drank. He counted the skins while she did it. There were three, full at dawn from two days of rain. Hers was down by a third already, and most of that was going to come back up.

It did. Then she had to stop again, and again, and by the time the mist was going yellow overhead they had come a kilometer onto the flat.

The next time she straightened up her knees went, and Keo caught her under the arm. He got her arm across his shoulders and his own around her waist, and they stood like that for a moment, leaning on each other, his spike propped in the crook of his elbow.

"I can take her for a while," Benal said.

"I have her."

Benal let it go. His chest had been wrong since the constrictor. His left arm was worse. The suit had never properly mended at the elbow where the coils had crushed it, and the arm under it was weak. Neither of them knew. If he took her weight on it, it would give, and they would both see.

They walked. Every so often she hung on Keo's shoulder and retched, with nothing left in her to bring up.

Once, when they were small, she had shared her lunch with him. He had not thought of it in years. He thought of it now, watching her, and there was nothing he could give her back.

At the next standing water her feet stopped working altogether. Keo held her up while Benal got the packs off her.

"Get on," Keo said. He went down on his good knee with his back to her.

Benal helped her onto it. She was lighter than she should have been. She got her arms around Keo's neck, and he got his hands under her knees and stood, good leg first.

"Head over my shoulder," Keo said. "If it comes, let it go behind me."

Benal put Teva's pack on over his own and hung the fieldpack across his chest. Then he picked up both spikes.

On Keo's back, Teva had her cheek against his neck.

"You were right," she said. "About the day."

Keo hitched her higher. "Can you hold on?"

"Yes."

He started walking.

Within the hour the mist had burned off. The sky went white and then blue, and the heat came down onto the plain. The air went still. Benal filled her skin at a pool between the palms while the other two waited, and when he came back the midges had found Keo.

They came in columns, out of the wet ground and the ferns, and went for his eyes and the corners of his mouth. Keo screwed his face up and blew at them. He could not let go of her to lift a hand. Benal walked beside him with the spikes in one fist and wiped Keo's face with the other palm. Every few steps the midges were back, and he wiped them off again.

Teva's face over Keo's shoulder had gone from gray to red. Her suit pulsed at the wrists, where it still worked, and could not get the heat out of her fast enough.

The insects were a wall now, every wing and every shrilling thing on the plain together, and it came into his ears with nothing in the way. For two days the rain had covered it. It filled his head right up to the back of his eyes, and when he tried to think past it, to find Keo's steps or Teva's breathing underneath, he had to dig for each of them.

The capacitor in his shoulder had died two days ago. It had been red-lined since before the tangle, and in the rain it had gone dark. The shock grid had gone with it, and the flash, and the dampening that had let him think since the morning they walked out. The joint assist still worked, because it was only springs and drew on nothing.

He looked at Keo, with both hands under her knees and the midges in his eyes. He looked at Teva, who could not lift her head. Of the three of them he was the one with his hands free. In them he had two spikes he could barely use and a club. The suit behind them had nothing left.

He had told nobody. He was not going to tell them now.

"Ben," Teva said. Her voice came out of Keo's shoulder. "How far?"

He had it all.

"To where we lie up, about two and a half kilometers," he said. "Three, possibly. Then five this afternoon, which leaves two for tomorrow. All three skins are full, and there's water lying in most of the low places, so water isn't the trouble. There's light for nearly ten hours yet."

She was quiet for a few steps.

"And you two?"

"If it keeps your hours, it comes for us around nightfall. Fifteen hours from the dawn meal." He wiped Keo's face again. "Less of it, I'd think, because ours had the night." <!-- @WF-mash.onset: 15h -->

She shut her eyes against Keo's neck. Benal's father had never listened past the first number. She had asked for all of it.

He moved out to Keo's left, a step ahead, where he could see her face. Under the insects he could hear the catch in Keo's left foot, worse than at dawn, and his breath snagging on the rib at the top. He could hear her breathing, quick and shallow. He said nothing about any of it.

A little way on, Keo's left foot came down on a root under the ferns and turned. He went down on one knee with her on his back, and stayed there, with his head down and his breath going in and out through his teeth.

Benal had a hand out, full of spikes.

Then Keo got up.
