They walked out past the tracks in the morning light, and for the first kilometer nobody looked at them. They went around the thicket on both sides of the camp, deep and three-toed in the soft ground, and then they were behind and the woodland closed over them.

"Lucky it didn't come in," Keo said, a long time later, to no one. It was the last any of them said about it.

Benal's legs did not feel like his. The first hour he walked inside a kind of gray haze, the woodland going by at the edges of it, Keo's back ahead of him and Teva's behind, and his chest catching on every deep breath where the snake had held him. Two nights without sleep. The body did the walking anyway, badly, and the mind came along some way behind.

They came to a stream in the middle of the morning, clear and narrow over a sandy bed, and stopped back from it. Teva and Benal watched the water while Keo knelt and filled his skin and Benal's, the two that had carried him over the river full of air, and Teva topped up her own. The spare stayed corked, with the last of the honey in it.

The rain came and went. It was a warm thin rain out of a low sky. When it came it drummed on Benal's helmet and shut the world out, and he was glad for the isolation.

He found he was checking the others as insistently as an equation he could not leave alone. Keo was favoring a heel, coming down on it a little short. Teva was carrying her left arm close against her side, the shoulder the snake had thrown her on, the one she had hauled the cord with yesterday.

"Your heel," Benal said.

"It's fine."

"It's the same heel as before. If a hot spot comes back on the same heel, the odds it stays fine to the end of the walk are possibly poor."

"Keep your odds to yourself, Ben."

"Your shoulder," Benal said to Teva.

"I know."

"And the suits haven't been fed since the honey. The elbow's mending, and the film needs sugar."

"Tonight," Teva said. "There's the last of the honey."

"And the lashings are still damp from the river," Benal said, "which is the worst thing they can be, if it goes on. And you've got bites on your neck again."

Keo stopped walking and turned around and looked at him. "Is there anything else?"

Benal thought. "My chest," he said. "But I knew about that."

Teva's mouth moved. Keo shook his head and went on. Benal went after him and found that his own mouth was doing something it had not done in days.

The walking got easier through the middle of the morning, on flat ground under the trees. Keo talked first. He talked about the work herd at home, which he drove out to the river and back to the basin, and how he liked the animals and hated the gardens.

"I can tell you every hadrosaur in that herd by its walk," he said. "I can't tell you one plant from another. Teva knows. Plants just sit there."

"That's what's good about them," Teva said.

Keo told them about the old cow in the work herd who always drank first, and about Topo, who could not keep up on his bad ankle.

"So you walk slower," Teva said.

Keo looked back at her.

"I've seen you," she said. "Coming in from the river with him. You walk like you've got all day."

Keo did not seem to know what to do with that. He looked ahead again. "He's all right, Topo," he said. "He's better than all right."

"I like him," Teva said.

They walked a while. Then Teva, behind Benal, said, "What do you do, Ben? When you're not working."

He had to think about it.

"Shells," he said.

"Shells?"

"I collect them. Snails, turtles. Beetle carapaces, the ones that shine. I have a shelf." He felt his face go hot. "It's quite a large shelf."

"Ben has a beetle collection," Keo said, delighted.

"Carapaces. The beetles aren't in them anymore. That's the best thing about beetles," Benal said. "They don't talk."

Teva laughed, a short surprised sound, and stopped it. Keo turned around and walked backward for a few steps to look at her, grinning, and nearly went into a cycad.

"Who taught you the math?" Teva said. "Your father?"

"Some of it. Mostly the old woman who used to teach with Omya," Benal said. "The one people call scatterbrained."

"She is scatterbrained."

"She's cleverer than any of them," Benal said, "and they laugh before they listen." He heard what he had said, and stopped.

Neither of them laughed at that.

At midday they stopped on the bank above a dry streambed under tall trees, a flat bed of water-smoothed stones with the shade over it. They spread the bindings and the spear lashings on the hot stones in the sun between showers. Keo sat down against the bank and was asleep before he had finished sitting, his head going sideways, his mouth open. Teva took the watch.

Benal should have slept too. He was going to. He sat on the stones with his knees up. His hands found a flat stone, then another, and he set the second on the first and turned it a little until it sat. Then a third. It went on its own; he hardly had to watch. A fourth, smaller. A fifth on top, the smallest, balanced on a point.

"What's it for?" Teva said.

She was watching him from the bank. He had not known she was.

"It isn't for anything," he said. "It'll fall down. That's the point of it."

She looked at it for a while. Benal lay down and slept, and halfway through the heat he woke and took the watch from her, and she slept.

When they went on, he walked past the stack and did not touch it.

Teva saw. He saw her see it, and she let it be. Keo, ahead, did not look back.

In the afternoon the rain stopped and the heat came down under the trees, close and wet. The insects rose off the ferns in the still air after every shower, and the wall of them came up around Benal's head until the suit took the edge off it and he could think again.

Keo stopped once with his hand to his forehead.

Benal reached him while Teva was still several steps back. "Your head."

"It's going."

"It's not going. You've been doing that since the stream."

"It's going, Ben."

Benal did not argue. He did not go back to his place in the line either. He walked beside Keo for the rest of that stretch, where the trail was wide enough for two, and talked. He talked about beetles, the iridescent ones, and how the color was not in them at all but in how their shells broke up the light. Then about how long a snail would take to cross the floor of a nest. He did not need Keo to answer. He could hear that Keo was listening, and Keo's answers came sooner when Benal talked.

After a while Keo said, "How long would it take? The snail."

"A whole morning. And it would go to the wrong place."

Keo laughed and winced, and went on laughing anyway.

With an hour of light left, the trees began to stand farther apart. The cycads thinned out and the ferns came up between them, waist-high, then higher, and there was more sky overhead than there had been since the narrows. Then they came out from under the last of the big trees, and the country opened in front of them.

It went away low and green to the edge of the world, fern and more fern, with the sky over it, and almost nothing standing up out of it anywhere: a scatter of old cycads, and far off, small with distance, the backs of a herd moving slowly through the fern, too far to say what they were.

"Faster walking," Keo said. "No trees to go around."

Over the fern, flybeaks were hunting in the last of the light, small ones, a dozen or more, wheeling low and dropping into the fronds and rising again with something. Very high above them, so high it was only a mark on the sky, one more turned in a slow circle and did not drop. Benal could not tell how big it was. He watched it go around twice and could not make the size come right.

Under the trees he had never been able to see them coming. Out there there was nothing to hide under, not anywhere, all the way to the sky.

"Faster walking," he agreed, because he wanted it to be true.
