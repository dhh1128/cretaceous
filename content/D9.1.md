They walked out past the tracks in the gray light, and for the first kilometer nobody looked at them. They went around the thicket on both sides of the camp, deep and three-toed in the soft ground, and then they were behind and the woodland closed over them.

"It didn't come in," Keo said, a long time later, to no one. It was the last any of them said about it.

Benal's legs did not feel like his. The first hour he walked inside a kind of gray wool, the woodland going by at the edges of it, Keo's back ahead of him and Teva's behind, and his chest catching on every deep breath where the snake had held him. Two nights without sleep. He had never gone two nights without sleep in his life. He found that the body did it anyway, badly, and that the mind came along some way behind.

They came to a stream in the middle of the morning, clear and narrow over a sandy bed, too small for anything to live in that could hurt them. Keo knelt and filled his skin and Benal's, the two that had carried him over the river full of air, and Teva topped up her own. The spare stayed corked, with the last of the honey in it.

The rain came and went. It was a warm thin rain out of a low sky. When it came it drummed on Benal's helmet and shut the world out, and he was grateful for it every time.

He found he was looking at the others the way he looked at an equation he could not leave alone. Keo was favoring his left heel, coming down on it a little short. Teva was carrying her left arm close against her side, the shoulder the snake had thrown her on, the one she had hauled the cord with yesterday.

"Your heel," Benal said.

"It's fine."

"It's the same heel as before. If a hot spot comes back on the same heel, the odds it stays fine for another seven days are possibly one in five."

"Keep your odds to yourself, Ben."

"Your shoulder," Benal said to Teva.

"I know."

"And the suits haven't been fed since the honey. Mine's mending the elbow. It'll be eating me."

"Tonight," Teva said. "There's the last of the honey."

"And the lashings are still damp from the river," Benal said, "which is the worst thing they can be, if it goes on. And you've got bites on your neck again."

Keo stopped walking and turned around and looked at him. "Is there anything else?"

Benal thought. "My chest," he said. "But I knew about that."

Teva's mouth moved. Keo shook his head and went on. Benal went after him and found that his own mouth was doing something it had not done in days.

The walking got easier through the middle of the morning, on flat ground under the trees. Keo talked first. He did not seem to decide to; it just came, the way talk came on a long walk. He talked about the work herd at home, which he drove out to the river and back to the basin, and how he liked the animals and hated the gardens.

"I can tell you every hadrosaur in that herd by its walk," he said. "I can't tell you one plant from another. Teva knows. Plants just sit there."

"That's what's good about them," Teva said.

He told them about the old cow who always drank first, and about Topo, who came out with him to the herd most mornings and could not keep up on his bad ankle.

"So you walk slower," Teva said.

Keo looked back at her.

"I've seen you," she said. "Coming in from the river with him. You walk like you've got all day."

Keo did not seem to know what to do with that. He looked ahead again. "He's all right, Topo," he said. "He's better than all right."

"He is," Teva said. "He's kind to the little ones at the pool. He doesn't have to be."

They walked a while. Then Teva, behind Benal, said, "What do you do, Ben? When you're not working."

It was a question he had not been asked before, by anyone. He had to think about it.

"Shells," he said.

"Shells?"

"I collect them. Snails, turtles. Beetle carapaces, the ones that shine. I have a shelf." He felt his face go hot. "It's quite a large shelf."

"Ben has a beetle collection," Keo said, delighted.

"Carapaces. The beetles aren't in them anymore. That's the best thing about beetles," Benal said. "They don't talk."

Teva laughed, a short surprised sound, and stopped it. Keo turned around and walked backward for a few steps to look at her, grinning, and nearly went into a cycad.

"Who taught you the math?" Teva said. "Your father?"

"Some of it. Mostly the old woman who used to teach with Omya," Benal said. "The one who talks to herself."

"Everyone says she's scatterbrained."

"She's cleverer than any of them," Benal said, "and they laugh at her anyway. She'll start a sentence about cycads and finish it about orbits, and if you wait, it's the same sentence. Nobody waits." He heard what he had said, and stopped.

Neither of them laughed at that.

The lay-up came in a dry streambed under tall trees, a flat bed of water-smoothed stones with the shade over it. They spread the bindings and the spear lashings on the hot stones in the sun between showers. Keo sat down against the bank and was asleep before he had finished sitting, his head going sideways, his mouth open. Teva took the watch.

Benal should have slept too. He was going to. He sat on the stones with his knees up. His hands found a flat stone, then another, and he set the second on the first and turned it a little until it sat. Then a third. It went on its own; he hardly had to watch. A fourth, smaller. A fifth on top, the smallest, balanced on a point.

"What's it for?" Teva said.

She was watching him from the bank. He had not known she was.

"It isn't for anything," he said. "It'll fall down. That's the point of it."

She looked at it for a while, five stones standing in a crooked column in the sun. Benal lay down on the warm stones and slept the second half, and then took the watch from her.

When they went on, he walked past the stack and did not touch it.

Teva saw. He saw her see it, and she let it be. Keo, ahead, did not look back.

In the afternoon the rain stopped and the heat came down under the trees, close and wet. The insects rose off the ferns in the still air after every shower, and the wall of them came up around Benal's head until the suit took the edge off it and he could think again.

Keo stopped once with his hand to his forehead.

Benal was beside him before Teva had seen. "Your head."

"It's going."

"It's not going. You've been doing that since the stream."

"It's going, Ben."

Benal did not argue. He did not go back to his place in the line either. He walked beside Keo for the rest of that stretch, where the trail was wide enough for two, and talked. He talked about beetles, the iridescent ones, and how the color was not in them at all but in how their shells broke up the light, and about the snail he had once timed across his windowsill. He did not need Keo to answer. He could hear that Keo was listening, and he could hear that it was easier for him than thinking.

After a while Keo said, "How long did it take? The snail."

"A whole morning. It was going to the wrong place, as well."

Keo laughed and winced, and went on laughing anyway.

With an hour of light left, the trees began to stand farther apart. The cycads thinned out and the ferns came up between them, waist-high, then higher, and there was more sky overhead than there had been since the narrows. Then they came out from under the last of the big trees, and the country opened in front of them.

It went away low and green to the edge of the world, fern and more fern, with the sky over it, and almost nothing standing up out of it anywhere.

"Faster walking," Keo said. "No trees to go around."

Benal looked at it. He had been under trees all his life. There was nothing out there to hide under, not anywhere, all the way to the sky.

"Faster walking," he agreed, because he wanted it to be true.

Behind them, at the edge of the trees, there was a thicket of close young trunks, and an hour of light to build in. They turned back to it.
