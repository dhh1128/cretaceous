For the first kilometer none of them looked back at the tracks. Then the woodland closed over them.

"Lucky it didn't come in," Keo said, a long time later, to no one. It was the last anyone said about it.

They filled the skins at a stream in the middle of the morning, two of them watching the water while the third knelt. The spare stayed corked, with the last of the honey in it.

Benal kept checking the others, like an equation he couldn't leave alone. Keo was favoring a heel, coming down on it short. Teva held her left arm close against her side, the shoulder the snake had thrown her on, the one she had hauled the cord with yesterday.

"Your heel," Benal said.

"It's fine."

"Same heel as before. When a hot spot comes back, the odds it stays fine to the end of the walk are possibly poor."

"Keep your odds to yourself, Ben."

"Your shoulder," Benal said to Teva.

"I know."

"And the suits haven't been fed since the honey. My elbow's mending, and the film needs sugar."

"Tonight," Teva said. "There's the last of the honey."

"And Keo, you've got bites on your neck again."

Keo stopped and turned around. "Anything else?"

Benal thought. "My chest," he said. "But I knew about that."

Teva's mouth twitched. Keo shook his head and went on. Benal went after him, and his own mouth was doing something it hadn't done in days.

On the flat under the trees Keo talked first: about the work herd at home, how he liked the animals and hated the gardens.

"I can tell you every hadrosaur in that herd by its walk," he said. "I can't tell one plant from another. Teva knows. Plants just sit there."

"That's what's good about them," Teva said.

Keo told them about the old cow who always drank first, and about Topo, who couldn't keep up on his bad ankle.

"So you walk slower," Teva said.

Keo looked back at her.

"I've seen you," she said. "Coming in from the river with him. You walk like you've got all day."

Keo didn't seem to know what to do with that. "He's all right, Topo," he said. "He's better than all right."

"I like him," Teva said.

After a while Teva, behind Benal, said, "What do you do, Ben? When you're not working?"

"Shells," he said.

"Shells?"

"I collect them. Snails, turtles. Beetle carapaces, the shiny ones. I have a shelf." His face went hot. "It's quite a large shelf."

"Ben has a beetle collection," Keo said, delighted.

"Carapaces. The beetles aren't in them anymore. That's the best thing about beetles," Benal said. "They don't talk."

Teva laughed, a short surprised sound, and swallowed it. Keo walked backward a few steps to grin at her and nearly went into a cycad.

"Who taught you the math?" Teva said. "Your dad?"

"Some of it. Mostly the old woman, the one Omya's age. The one people call scatterbrained."

"She is scatterbrained."

"She's smarter than any of them," Benal said, "and they laugh before they listen." He heard what he'd said, and stopped.

Neither of them laughed.

At midday they stopped above a dry streambed, water-smoothed stones in the shade of tall trees. Keo sat down against the bank and was asleep before he'd finished sitting, his head lolling, his mouth open. Teva took the watch.

Benal should have slept too. He sat on the stones with his knees up. His hands found a flat stone, then another, and he set the second on the first and turned it until it sat. Then a third. A fourth, smaller. A fifth, the smallest, balanced on a point.

"What's it for?" Teva said.

He hadn't known she was watching.

"It isn't for anything," he said. "It'll fall down. That's the point."

She looked at it a while. Then he slept, and halfway through the heat he took the watch from her.

When they went on, he walked past the stack and left it standing.

Teva saw. He saw her see it, and she let it be. Keo, ahead, didn't look back.

Keo stopped once with his hand to his forehead.

Benal reached him before Teva had caught up. "Your head."

"It's going."

"You've been doing that all afternoon."

"It's going, Ben."

Benal didn't argue, but he didn't go back to his place in the line either. Where the trail was wide enough for two he walked beside Keo and talked: about beetles, the shimmering ones, how the color wasn't in them at all but in how their shells broke up the light. Then about how long a snail would take to cross the floor of a nest. He could hear Keo listening.

After a while Keo said, "How long would it take? The snail."

"A whole morning. And it would go to the wrong place."

Keo laughed and winced, and kept laughing anyway.

With an hour of light left the trees thinned, and ferns came up between the cycads, waist-high, then higher. Then they came out from under the last of the big trees, and the country opened.

It ran away low and green to the edge of the world, fern and more fern, almost nothing standing up out of it: a scatter of old cycads, and far off the backs of a herd drifting through the fern.

"Faster walking," Keo said. "No trees to go around."

Over the fern a dozen or more small flybeaks were hunting in the last of the light, wheeling low, dropping into the fronds and rising again with something. Far above them, only a mark on the sky, one more turned in a slow circle and did not drop. Benal watched it go around twice and couldn't make the size come right.

Under the trees he had never been able to see them coming. Out there was nothing to hide under, all the way to the sky.

"Faster walking," he agreed, because he wanted it to be true.
