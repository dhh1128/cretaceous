"Again," said Teva.

Four yazhi stood at the stone lip of the pool in the storm-dark, hot and cross and wanting to be anywhere else, and Teva stood behind them. The air had stopped moving an hour ago. It sat on the back of her neck like a hand. She could feel the heat in her face the way you feel a fever, from the inside, with nowhere for it to go.

The pool lay flat and clear in front of them, as clear as it always was, so that every stone on its bottom showed. It was the only water in the Enclave a child was let near, because nothing could hide in it: the river was brown and slow and you could not see a hand's depth into it, and this was not the river. In the middle, where the spring came up, the surface bulged very slightly and ran away in a slow skin toward the outflow at the far end. People drew their drinking water at the rise. Teva kept the drill at the outflow end, where nobody drew anything.

Teva bent and picked a pebble out of the gravel. "Watch the water," she said. "Not me. The water."

She tossed it underhand out toward the middle of the pool, short of the rise. It went in with a small sound. A ring came off it and came toward them across the flat surface, widening, until it broke against the stone at their feet.

"That," Teva said. "On the bank, a croc can see you. In the water it doesn't have to. It has skin along its jaw that feels the water move, and it waits for the water to tell it where you are." She heard herself, too much and too fast, the way Omya would have said it to a journeyman, and she started again. "If you make a ring, the croc knows where you are. So you don't make rings."

Three of them nodded. Miri did not nod. Miri had backed up until her heels were in the gravel, two steps behind the others, and she was looking at the water as if it might come out after her.

Teva went back and took her hand. Miri's damp fingers gripped hers. She walked Miri forward to the lip with the others and did not let go.

"Lio," she said. "Up toward the spring a little. Splash it."

Lio was delighted. He ran along the lip and slapped the water with both hands, hard, three times, and three rings spread and crossed as the current carried them down toward Miri's feet. Miri squeezed Teva's fingers.

"Now the rule," she said.

They knew the rule. They had known it since they could stand. They gave it back to her in a ragged chorus, all four of them at once, with Joss loudest: still, together, back from the edge, never alone. Teva did not let them just say it. She put each of them where they should stand, a step back from the lip, close enough to touch, Nael beside Joss and Lio back from the outflow and Miri beside her, and she moved Joss twice because he kept edging forward to see better.

"Lio's rings were the biggest," said Joss. "That means the croc comes for Lio first. That's good. Lio's slowest."

Nael laughed so hard he had to sit down. Lio, who could not decide whether to be insulted, laughed too, and then Miri, very small, behind her hand.

Teva did not laugh. She looked at Joss until he stopped grinning, which took a while, and then she looked at the water again.

"Why would a croc even be in here?" Nael asked from the gravel. "We're inside."

"The gratings on the river stop a big croc," Teva said. "They don't stop a little one. A little one goes through, and grows up inside."

Nael thought about that. "Then why don't they shut the river? All the way?"

"Because a river you shut fills up with mud. And sticks, and everything the floods bring down. So the gratings have to come up, sometimes, to let it all through." She looked down at him. "While they're up, bigger things can get through too."

"Oh," said Nael, and seemed satisfied, which Teva envied him.

Then the first drops came down on the pool, fat and far apart, each one making a ring. In a moment there were too many rings to count and the whole surface was moving at once.

"Shelter," Teva said, and the yazhi ran for the fern-thatch on its poles beside the pool, shrieking, as if the rain were chasing them. The thatch leaked at once. It always did. They crowded in under the driest corner and went on shrieking.

Teva did not go with them.

She stood in the open at the lip of the pool and let the rain hit her. It came down cool against her skin, and hard, the first thing all day that had taken any heat out of her at all. It ran down her face and into her collar and down her back, and she stood with her eyes closed and her arms loose and felt her body give the heat up at last, the way it had been trying to since noon.

When she went in under the thatch, dripping, Joss was waiting for her.

"Did a croc get Alira?" he asked.

The others went quiet.

"No," said Teva.

She sat down on the wet ground under the leaking thatch. Miri came and leaned on her without being asked, and Teva put an arm round her and looked out at the pool with the rain on it.

No. A croc had not got Alira. A stingburn had got Alira, a little thing on a wrist that went red and then went bad, and there had been no Seeding to stop it. Two years ago a bite had killed Teva's mother the same way, after her Seeding failed to take. Teva had spent the afternoon with four children teaching them to be afraid of teeth, and the drill was right, every word of it, but teeth were the wrong fear this week. There was nothing in the water that would kill them as surely as what was in their blood, and there was no rule for that one, no stepping back from its edge, and nobody to drill.

She had nothing to teach them about a stingburn. Nobody did, and the Council would sit at dusk and decide, she was sure of it, to wait.

The rain eased the way it always did, all at once, as if somebody had stopped pouring. When it was light enough to see the nests, Teva stood up.

"Done," she said. "Go home."

They ran the short way back, shouting about the rain, to where the grown-ups were already waiting at the foot of the ladders, Lio's father with a fern frond over his head. Miri held on to Teva's hand until the last moment, and then let go and ran.

When they had all gone she walked back out to the lip of the pool. The last of the rain was still coming down, softer now. Every drop that hit the water made its small ring, and the rings crossed and went out across the flat clear water to every edge at once.

Every drop was a ring, and there was nothing in the pool to read them.
