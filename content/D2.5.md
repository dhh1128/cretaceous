"Again," said Teva.

Four yazhi stood at the stone lip of the pool in the storm-dark, hot and cross, and Teva stood behind them. The air had stopped moving an hour ago. It sat on the back of her neck like a hand. Her face burned from the inside, like a fever.

The pool lay flat and clear, every stone on its bottom showing. Nothing could hide in it, so it was the only water in the Enclave a child was let near. In the river you could not see a hand's depth.

Teva picked a pebble out of the gravel. "Watch the water," she said. "Not me. The water."

She tossed it underhand toward the middle. It plopped in, and a ring spread toward them across the flat surface and broke against the stone at their feet.

"That," Teva said. "On the bank, a croc can see you. In the water it doesn't have to. It has skin along its jaw that feels the water move, and it waits for the water to tell it where you are." She heard herself, too much and too fast, like Omya with a journeyman, and started again. "Make a ring and the croc knows where you are. So no rings."

Miri had backed up until her heels were in the gravel, staring at the water as if it might come out after her.

Teva went back for her. Miri's damp fingers gripped hers, and Teva walked her to the lip and held on.

"Lio. Up toward the spring a little. Splash it."

Lio was delighted. He ran along the lip and slapped the water three times, and three rings spread and crossed as the current carried them down toward Miri's feet. Miri squeezed Teva's fingers.

"Now the rule," Teva said.

They gave it back in a ragged chorus, Joss loudest: still, together, back from the edge, never alone. Teva put each of them a step back from the lip, close enough to touch, and moved Joss twice because he kept edging forward to see better.

A handful of older children came along the path, still arguing about a number: Benal's lot, coming from his lesson.

"That's Ben's," said Lio. "Ben's the tallest person there is."

"If a croc got Ben," said Joss, "it'd have to eat him in two goes."

"Three," said Nael.

Nael laughed so hard at his own number that he had to sit down. Lio laughed too. Then Miri, very small, behind her hand.

Teva did not laugh. She looked at Joss until he stopped grinning, which took a while. Ben would probably have laughed, then worked out how many goes it really took and told them, at length. She had sat through years of lessons with him and still had no idea what he did up in that corner all day.

"Why would a croc even be in here?" Nael asked from the gravel. "We're inside."

"The gratings on the river stop a big croc," Teva said. "Not a little one. A little one gets through and grows up inside."

"Then why don't they shut the river? All the way?"

"Because a shut river fills up with mud. And sticks, and everything the floods bring down. So sometimes the gratings have to come up to let it through." She looked down at him. "While they're up, bigger things get through too."

"Oh," said Nael, satisfied. Teva envied him.

Then the first drops smacked the pool, fat and far apart, each one a ring. In a moment there were too many to count, and the whole surface shivered.

"Shelter," Teva said, and the yazhi ran shrieking for the fern-thatch beside the pool. It leaked. It always did.

Teva stayed at the lip and let the rain hit her, cool and hard. It ran down her face and into her collar and down her back, and she stood with her eyes closed and her arms loose and gave the heat up at last.

When she went in under the thatch, dripping, Joss was waiting.

"Did a croc get Alira?"

The others went quiet.

"No," said Teva. "It was a sting, on her wrist."

Joss looked down at his own wrists. So did Lio.

She sat down on the wet ground. Miri came and leaned on her, and Teva put an arm around her and looked out at the rain on the pool.

A stingburn had got Alira, a little thing that went red and then went bad, and there had been no Seeding to stop it. Two years ago a bite had killed Teva's mother the same way, after her Seeding failed to take. All afternoon Teva had taught four children to fear teeth. The drill was right, every word of it, but teeth were the wrong fear this week. Nothing in the water would kill them as surely as what was in their blood. There was no rule for that one, no stepping back from its edge, and nobody to drill.

The Council would sit at dusk and decide, she was sure of it, to wait.

The rain slackened. When it was light enough to see the nests, Teva stood up.

"Done," she said. "Go home."

They ran off shouting about the rain, to the grown-ups waiting at the foot of the ladders, Lio's father with a fern frond over his head. Miri held Teva's hand until the last moment, then let go and ran.

Teva walked back out to the lip. The rain was softer now, and the rings crossed and spread to every edge of the clear water.

Every drop was a ring, and there was nothing in the pool to read them.
