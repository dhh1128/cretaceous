They'd eaten pinyons and a little honey at first light, and the sweetness was still in Benal's mouth when they set out. His chest ached on every breath, but less. The morning was already hot and dead still, and the woodland ran low and green.

The birds changed first.

Under the small ones high in the crowns came others, lower and farther off: long falling calls he had never heard, a harsh knocking, and frogs that weren't the frogs he knew, deeper, slower, more of them. The ground went soft under his boots. Something warm and green and rank hung on the air, like water left too long in a skin.

Then, close ahead and to the left, a sound he understood perfectly: a heavy, unhurried slide, like a wet bundle of fronds dragged down a bank, and a deep splash.

"Stop," he said.

Keo had his spike up before Benal finished the word. Teva went so still he couldn't hear her breathe. Two days ago he had said *wait* in the leaves and they had walked on.

Keo crept forward, and they followed him to where the trees ended at the top of the bank.

The size of it hit first. All his life he had pictured the First Walk's river like theirs at home, something you could throw a stone across. This one lay wider than anything he had ever seen, flat as a sheet of metal under a cloudless sky, the light on it so hard it hurt. The far bank was a low line of mud and green, wavering in the heat.

It looked like the most peaceful thing he had ever seen.

Below them the bank ran down in black mud, slick and soft, strewn with rotting ferns and the broken leaves of the ginkgos that leaned out over the water: fans as wide as his hand, split, browning at the edges. One slid under his boot.

Heat came up off the water into his face. Out in the glare a fish jumped, a silver flick, and the rings spread slowly across the whole surface until he lost them. Behind them, Noli had stopped at the edge of the trees and would come no farther. He didn't blame her.

"Where'd it go?" Teva whispered.

"Under," Keo said.

Benal looked where the sound had been and saw only smooth water. Then he looked at the far bank again.

There were logs lying along the mud in the sun, dark and long, one and another and another, and they were not logs. The nearest lay half out of the water with its jaws propped wide, pale inside against the dark head, as if it had forgotten them. Plates ridged its back like old bark, and its tail ran back into the shallows. It was longer than the three of them laid end to end.

Then the near water, which he had thought was empty. Here and there in the shallows, not far out, small bumps sat on the surface in pairs, a little ridge behind each: nostrils, and eyes. They had been there the whole time. He had looked straight at them and seen water.

Teva held her spike across her body, knuckles white. Keo stood at the last trunk with his spike in both hands, staring.

Benal looked at him, and then away. He knew what Keo was seeing, and it was not the far bank.

"How many?" Teva said at last.

"Let me look."

Every child knew what the crocs felt: not the ground, the surface. Anything that moved it, they found. Today there was no wind at all.

"Anything that touches it today—" he started.

"I know," Teva said. "Loudest thing in the world." She said it to him, not the river, in the voice she must have used on the yazhi.

Yesterday, at the stream, he had seen what a small one looked like. None of these were small.

Behind them Keo said, "Well. At least we know where they all went."

Benal reached for what he'd have said back yesterday and found the start of it.

"Possibly they—" There was no rest of it.

Benal began to count.

He did everything he was afraid of carefully. The far bank first, the ones hauled out in the sun, upstream to down and back again to be sure. Then the eyes in the near water, which was harder, because they sank and came up somewhere else; when one sank he started that part over. His lips moved. After a while he was saying the numbers out loud.

"Eleven on the far bank. Possibly twelve; one might be a log. Five in the water I can see. Possibly six."

"Possibly," Keo said.

"Sixteen I can see. That's the least there are." He didn't look at either of them. "There'll be more under. More up and down from here. I can only give you the ones I can see."

He looked at the big one on the far bank, jaws open in the sun, and did the other sum, because he couldn't help it.

"And it's about fifty of that one wide," he said. "Nose to tail."

Keo opened his mouth. Benal waited for *we'll be fine*. It didn't come.

"Fifty," Teva said.

Out on the bright water, one pair of eyes sank without a ripple and didn't come up, and the river lay still in the sun.
