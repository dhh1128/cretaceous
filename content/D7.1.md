They had eaten pinyons and a little honey at first light, sitting up in their sleeping places, and the sweetness was still in Benal's mouth when they set out. His chest ached on every breath, the same band across it, but less, and the morning was already hot and perfectly still. The woodland ran low and green, and they walked into it easily, without hurry.

The birds changed first.

Until then there had been the ordinary ones, small and high in the crowns. Now there were others under them, ahead, somewhere lower and farther off: long falling calls he had never heard, and a harsh knocking, and a chorus of frogs that was not the chorus he knew, deeper, slower, more of them. He walked on listening to it and trying to make it into something. The ground under his boots had gone soft, and the smell had changed with it: something warm and green and rank lay on the air, like water left standing too long in a skin.

Then, close ahead and to the left, there was a sound he understood perfectly. It was a heavy, unhurried slide, like a wet bundle of fronds dragged down a bank. At the end of it came a deep splash, and then nothing.

"Stop," he said.

They stopped at once, and none of them asked what it was. Keo had his spear up before Benal had finished the word, and Teva had gone so still that he could not hear her breathe. They had all heard it, he realized. Even Teva had heard that one. Something big had just gone into water none of them could see.

Keo went forward first, low, and they followed him to where the trees thinned and ended at the top of the bank, and stopped there, among the last of the trunks.

The first thing was the size of it. He had known about the river the First Walk crossed all his life. In his head it was like theirs at home, something you could throw a stone across. This was nothing like that. It lay in front of them wider than anything he had ever seen, flat and bright and perfectly still under a sky without a cloud in it, and the light lay on it so hard that it was like looking at a sheet of metal. Nothing moved on it, not so much as a ripple. The far bank was a low line of mud and green, wavering in the heat.

It looked harmless. It looked like the most peaceful thing he had ever seen.

Below them the bank ran down to the water in black mud, slick and soft, with rotting ferns lying in it, and everywhere among them, up to their feet, the broken leaves of the ginkgos that leaned out over the water: big flat fans the size of plates, split and torn, brown at the edges. One slid a little under his boot when he stood on it.

The heat came up off the water into his face. It was only the middle of the morning and already the air over the river shook. Somewhere out on it a fish jumped, a small silver flick, and the rings went out from it slowly across the whole flat surface, wider and wider, until he lost them in the glare. He watched them go. He found that he could not stop watching them go. Noli had stopped at the edge of the trees behind them and would not come any farther, and he did not blame her.

"Where did it go?" Teva said, very low.

No one answered. Benal looked at the water where the sound had been and saw only the flat bright surface, closed over and smooth.

Then he looked at the far bank again, and at last he made out what lay on it.

The mud on the far side was not all mud. There were logs lying along it in the sun, dark and long, one and another and another, and they were not logs. As he looked they became what they were, one by one, the way a picture comes out of a pattern once you have seen it. The nearest lay half out of the water with its jaws open, wide, propped and perfectly still, as if it had forgotten them. The open jaw showed pale against the dark head. He could see the plates along its back, ridged and knobbed like old bark, and the great flat length of the tail lying back into the shallows. It was longer than the three of them lying end to end.

And then the water closer in, which he had thought was empty. Here and there along the near shallows, not far out, there were small dark bumps on the flat bright water, in pairs, close together, with a little ridge behind each pair. Nostrils at the tips of the snouts. The eyes farther back. Not moving at all. They had been there the whole time. He had looked straight at them and seen water.

Teva had not moved since they came out of the trees. She stood with her spike held across her body and her knuckles white on it, looking at the water. Keo stood at the last trunk with his spear in both hands, looking at it too, quite still. Benal counted his own heartbeats for a while instead of anything else. It did not help.

For a long time none of them said anything.

He thought about the water, and about what the crocs felt, which every child knew: not the ground, but the surface. Anything that moved it, they knew where it was. Today there was no wind at all. Anything that touched that water today would be the loudest thing in the world.

He did not say it, because when he looked at Teva's face he saw that he did not have to.

Yesterday, at the stream, there had been one that did not matter. He knew now what a small one looked like. None of these were small.

Behind them Keo said, "Well. At least we know where they all went." No one laughed, and after a moment Keo did not seem to expect anyone to.

Benal began to count.

He did it carefully. He did everything he was afraid of carefully. He started with the far bank, the ones hauled out in the sun, going along it from upstream to down and then back again to be sure. Then the near water, the pairs of eyes, which was harder, because they sank and came up somewhere else. He counted only the ones he could see at the same moment, and when one sank he began that part again. His lips moved. After a while he heard himself saying the numbers out loud, under his breath, and then not under his breath.

"Eleven on the far bank," he said. "Possibly twelve; one of them might be a log. Five in the water that I can see. Possibly six."

"Possibly," Keo said.

"Sixteen that I can see. Which is the least there are." He did not look at either of them. "There'll be more under. There'll be more up and down from here that we can't see. I don't know how many. I can only tell you the ones I can see, and that's sixteen."

He looked across the water, at the big one on the far bank with its jaws open in the sun, and did the other sum, because he could not stop himself.

"And it's about fifty of that one wide," he said. "Laid nose to tail."

Keo opened his mouth. Benal waited for *we'll be fine*. It did not come.

Teva said nothing at all.

Out on the flat bright water, one pair of eyes sank without a ripple and did not come up again, and the river lay still in the sun, exactly as harmless as it looked.
