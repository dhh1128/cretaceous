They'd eaten pinyons and a little honey at first light, and the sweetness was still in Benal's mouth when they set out. His chest ached on every breath, but less. The morning was already hot and dead.

The birds changed first.

Under the small ones high in the crowns came others, lower and farther off: long falling hoots he had never heard, a harsh knocking. Then frogs that weren't the frogs he knew, deeper, slower, more of them. The ground went soft under his boots. A warm green rankness hung on the air, like water left too long in a skin.

Ahead and to the left he caught a sound he understood perfectly: a heavy, unhurried slide, like a wet bundle of fronds dragged down a bank, and a quiet splash.

"Stop," he said.

Teva went still. Keo had his spike up before Benal finished the word. He crept forward, and they followed him to where the trees ended at the top of the bank.

The river's size hit first. He had pictured First Walk's water like it was at home, something you could throw a stone across. Here it lay vast and flat under a cloudless sky, the light on it so hard it hurt. The far bank was a line of green, wavering in the heat. There were no stones in it anywhere. Benal had known since the fog that they might come out on a stretch of bank the story never mentioned, and they had.

It looked peaceful.

Below them the bank ran down in black mud strewn with rotting ferns and the leaves of the ginkgos that leaned out over the water: fans as wide as his hand, split, browning at the edges. One slid under his boot.

Heat jumped off the water into his face. Out in the glare a fish jumped, a silver flick, and the rings spread across the whole surface until he lost them. Behind them, Noli had stopped at the edge of the trees.

"Where'd it go?" Teva whispered.

"Under," Keo said.

Benal looked and saw only smooth water. His eyes traced from the splash he'd heard, across the glare, to the far bank.

There were log-like things lying there in the sun, but they were not logs. The nearest lay half out of the water with its jaws propped wide, pale inside against dark scales. Its tail ran back into the shallows. It was longer than the three of them laid end to end.

Looking more closely at nearby water, he now saw, here and there, small paired bumps on the surface. Nostrils. Eyes. His first glance had told him just water.

Teva held her spike across her body, knuckles white.

Keo stared for a long time. Benal stared with him. Yesterday, at the stream, the croc had been so much smaller than these.

"Well," Keo said. "At least we know where they wait for lunch."

Benal began to count.

He did everything he was afraid of carefully. The far bank first, the ones hauled out in the sun, upstream to down and back again to be sure. Then the eyes in the near water, which was harder, because they sank and came up somewhere else; when one sank he started that part over. His lips moved. After a while he was saying the numbers out loud.

"Twenty-three on the far bank. Nine in the water I can see. Possibly ten."

"Possibly," Keo said.

"But there'll be more."

He looked at the big one on the far bank, jaws open in the sun, and did the other sum, because he couldn't help it.

"And I bet the river's fifty of that one wide," he said. "Nose to tail."

Keo opened his mouth. Benal waited for *we'll be fine*.

"Fifty," Teva said.

Out on the bright water, one pair of eyes sank without a ripple and didn't come up.
