The sun stood nearly straight over the brow, and Benal's shadow had drawn in under him until he knelt in it. The rock was hot through the dead suit at his knees. Glare came up off the sea past the drop and made him squint at the hollow.

He had the bar across his thighs. He had taken it off the crate stair, unwatched, once the last unit was up and the voice was reading the fits back. The hard was out of sight below the crown's far side, and nobody knew where he was. Last night, from a few paces back, he had watched a man pack something into this hollow. If it was here, he had seen it.

He lifted the stones off first. They looked like any stones and came up heavy and hot. He set them to one side, where the man had set them. Under them the rubble was packed hard. He drove the bar in and it went a finger's depth with a grate and stopped. He worked it, broke a lump loose, scooped it out with his hand and drove again. The top of it was as hot as the rock. A hand down it came out cool and damp, darker, smelling of the inside of stone.

With the suit dead on him every grate and knock came into his ears whole. Far below, the surf ran in over the reef and drew back. Seabirds went up and down the face, calling, and the wind brought salt up the rock.

By a forearm's depth his weak arm was shaking on the bar. He changed hands. The other was clumsier, and he went slower.

The bar went down through the rubble with a grate and a knock, stone's sounds, and then it rang. It was one clear note, and it hung over the hollow after his hands had stopped. It was metal. He knew that before he had seen anything.

He put the bar down and dug with his fingers. The rubble came away from something smooth and round. He cleared along it until he could get his hands under, and lifted it out.

It was a cylinder about as long as his forearm, gray and dull. There was no red on it anywhere, and none of the white crust that had eaten the arches along the crown. A hand's width from his knee the rust stain ran down the rock from the anchor hole, where the founders' metal had stood and bled away in two hundred years. This had been in the ground longer than that, and it had done neither. He did not know a metal that could.

It was here. Elias Sandoval, whose leaf he had read longer than most and whose name was not on the roll of those who walked, had gone back further than the founders' coming. He had lived to be old on this rock, and he had put this in the ground. That was all it proved.

Words were cut into the lid in plain letters, shallow and clean: *for whoever comes back*. He turned it over. There was nothing else on it. Every plate in the archive began with a date. This had none.

It was a letter. He thought it was written to the two hundred who had walked off and left this man here, and they were all long dead. He did not know whether he was anyone it meant.

He got his fingers round the lid and twisted. His hands were shaking, and they slid. He wiped them on his thighs and tried again, and it did not turn.

The bar lay by his knee. Its point would go under the lid's edge. Whatever was inside would break when the lid gave. He did not know what was inside or how it was kept, and there was no time to do it slowly. At home there would be tools, and days.

"Ben!"

His name came from across the top, close behind him. He turned on his knees. Teva was coming over the bare rock from the arches, fast, her arms stiff at her sides.

"You went off without a word. On the last morning." She stopped over him, breathing hard. "Keo's staying with the wig, so I had to come over the top looking for you."

Her eyes went to the hollow, and the stones beside it, and what he held.

"What is that?"

"I d-don't know."

It was true. He had the man's name ready, and everything behind it. There was no time for it.

"You dug it up. Out of there."

"Yes."

Her face was gray under the sun, and wet. The wet ran off her jaw into the neck of her suit, which had not cooled her in days. She had crossed the whole top in this, with nothing since first light.

"You crossed the top," he said. "With nothing to drink."

"So did you."

He got the cylinder under his arm and the bar in his other hand.

"The charge is as full as we can wait for," she said. "Keo won't lift without you."

"I'm coming."

He pushed himself up off the lip of the hollow. When he was standing, there was a stone set on another stone at the lip, by his foot, and his hand had just left it. At home the first rain took them down. He looked at it a moment longer and left it where it was.

Teva was already going. He ran after her across the top, with the cylinder under his arm.
