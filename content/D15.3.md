When the light began to go they went back inside. Benal went up the stair first. Keo went after him on his hands, his rib catching each time he lifted himself, and Teva came behind, so close he felt her breath on his calf. At the top he sat and looked down.

Each slap of the water came back off the roof a moment after it. Under the slaps, from the seaward end, came something else: a long suck, then a gurgle, then a sigh, and after a while the same again, slower than breathing. He listened to it as he would have listened to an animal he had not seen yet, something big asleep in the next thicket. It was the sea, and the whole building was breathing it in and out.

He had seen the sea all yesterday, and been in it all night. He had not heard it breathe.

"Anything?" Benal said.

Keo looked along the water from the stair to the seaward end and back. Nothing moved on it. He let his sense go out over it too. At home, over any water, his sense came back thick with fish and frogs, a crowd of small minds anything could hide in. Here there was no mind in the water at all. Toward the broken end there were birds, many and small. He had never been anywhere so empty of minds.

"No," he said. "Nothing in it."

It ought to have been a relief. It was, a little.

They went along the walkway away from the birds, Keo hopping with a hand on the wall, and sat with their backs to the inner wall. The wind came in at the broken end and along the walkway, and it found him at once.

His tunic had not dried all day. It hung clammy on his shoulders and stuck to his back, and the hems had gone stiff with salt. Every cut from the shore stung where the cloth moved. The other two had their suits. He had his skin and the cloth on it, and the wind went through both.

He began to shake, and could not stop.

"Sit here," Teva said, shifting along the wall to make a gap.

"In the middle," Benal said. "You've got the least on."

He sat between them, Benal's shoulder against one side of him and Teva's against the other. After a while he noticed that she was shaking too, in long shudders that went through her and into him, and that she had not said a word about it.

Below the walkway's edge the water had slid down the wall, so that a hand's width of bare wall showed under the weed line the night's water had left.

"It's going down," Benal said.

Keo watched it a while and then could not see it.

The light went out of the broken end. More birds came back to it, a harsh gargling racket that went round and round the roof, then a grumble as they shoved along the walkway and settled.

Of the two suits only a faint glow was left, in patches where the film still lived. By it he could see the shape of Teva's arm and not her face.

Nobody said what they had found today.

To stop his teeth he started talking.

"Topo used to watch me drill Noli," he said. "Every morning, from the edge of the fern gardens. One morning she wouldn't pin. She went straight past the line and into the gardens and lay down in the middle of them, and I had to crawl in after her on my hands and knees."

"Into the gardens," Benal said.

"Everybody heard. And after that, every morning, Topo would sit up when I came out and say, *Who's pinning who today?*" His teeth knocked on it, and the roof gave back a little clatter.

Beside him Teva let out a breath through her nose. It was nearly a laugh.

"Every morning," she said.

"For a month. More. He never got tired of it."

He could see Topo sitting up, pleased, with his bad foot out in front of him. He kept his eyes on it until it went.

"Teva," Benal said, "who do you sit with? At home. I was trying to think, and I couldn't."

"Two girls. You know them. One's Sila's daughter."

"Sila's—"

"They're closer to each other than they are to me."

Keo had never heard her say anything like that. He had seen her with them at the pits, and thought she was the one who held back.

"That's the trouble with three," Benal said. "Possibly."

Keo snorted.

Teva was quiet. Then she said, "In the evenings I walk Omya down to the Vitarium. She doesn't need to go any more. She goes anyway." The wind came along the walkway and she waited for it to pass. "All the way down she asks me if the figs are in. Every evening, whatever the month. I tell her not yet, and she says you can't hurry a fig."

"Are they?" Keo said. "In?"

"I don't know. I didn't look."

Somewhere in it his teeth had stopped, and Teva's shudders had come further apart.

Then he was standing at the open edge of the walkway, skin prickling, the smell of lightning in his nose, nothing in front of him but black and no sound at all. It lasted a few seconds. Then he heard the slap of water a long way down. He did not know how he had got there. Benal had him by the arm.

"Keo. Keo. Where are you going?"

"I'm not."

"You went straight to the edge."

The floor tilted under him, slowly, toward the water, though nothing had moved. Then it came back.

"It's the cold," he said.

"Come on," Benal said. "This way. Small steps."

He let Benal turn him. They went back along the walkway, Keo's hand on the wall and Benal's on his arm, toward the faint patches of Teva's suit.

"Keo?" she said.

"Here."

He sat down between them again, and Benal did not let go of his arm. Out at the seaward end the building drew its long breath, and let it go.

It was not even properly night yet.
