The rain still stood in the hollows along the bank, with the last of the light lying on it. Keo got down on his knees and drank with his face in the water.

Beside him Benal took three swallows and sat back.

"Drink more, Ben," Keo said. "There's nothing up there."

Benal bent to it again. When Keo lifted his own head, the edge of the hollow was moving. Things smaller than his palm were running off the rock on too many legs, ticking, all of them sideways and all at once, and going into the cracks. They had the crayfish's claws held up in front and none of its tail. He watched which crack each one chose until the last had gone into the one under Teva's knee.

"What are they?" Benal said.

"I don't know."

Inside, Benal went up the stair first. Keo went after him on his hands, his rib catching each time he lifted himself. Teva came behind, close enough that he felt her breath on his calf. At the top he sat and looked down.

The floor of the house lay well below, under water no deeper than a shin, and the lumps of rust stood up out of it. On the wall a line of weed and splintered stuff ran the length of the house where the water had reached in the night.

Each slap of the water on the rust came back off the roof a moment after it. Under the slaps there was something else, from the end of the house nearest the sea. It was a long suck, and then a gurgle, and then a sigh, and after a while the same again. It was slower than breathing. He listened to it as he would have listened to an animal he had not seen yet, something big asleep in the next thicket. It was the sea. Somewhere in the floor at that end there was a way through to it, and the house was breathing it in and out.

He had seen the sea all yesterday, and been in it all night. He had not heard it breathe.

"Anything?" Benal said.

Keo looked along the water from the stair to the seaward end and back again, slowly. Nothing moved on it. He let his sense go out over it too. At home, over any water, his sense came back thick with fish and frogs, a crowd of small minds that anything could lie hidden in. Here there was no mind in the water at all. Along the gallery toward the broken end there were birds, many and small. He had never been anywhere so empty of minds.

"No," he said. "Nothing in it."

It ought to have been a relief. It was, a little.

Toward the seaward end, where the roof had come down, the gallery under the last of the roof was crusted with droppings. Birds sat along it against the light from the broken end, and the stink came off it on the wind in gusts, sharp enough to make his eyes run. Under it was fish, like the nets off the barrier. It was their roost, and he would no more have taken the other two into it than into a den.

"This end," Teva said.

They went along the gallery the other way, Keo hopping with a hand on the wall, to the inner end. The floor was clean there. In the sags of the floor toward the birds, rain had collected, and it was yellow-white with them.

"I wouldn't drink that," Benal said, "if anybody was thinking of it."

They sat down with their backs to the end wall. The wind came in at the broken end and along the gallery, and it found him at once.

His tunic had not dried. On the river, soaked cloth dried on you in an hour of walking; this had been on him all day. It hung clammy on his shoulders and stuck to his back, and along the hems it had gone stiff and rough with salt. Under it every cut from the bank stung where the cloth moved. The other two had their suits. He had his skin and the cloth on it, and the wind went through both.

He began to shake. He could not stop.

"Sit here," Teva said. She had shifted along the wall to make a gap.

"In the middle," Benal said. "You've got the least on."

He sat between them. Benal's shoulder came against one side of him and Teva's against the other. After a while he noticed that she was shaking too, in long shudders that went through her and into him, and that she had not said a word about it.

Below the gallery's edge the water had slid down the wall, so that a strip of bare wall showed under the weed line, a hand wide.

"It's going down," Benal said.

Keo watched it a while and then could not see it.

The light went out of the broken end. Then more birds came back to it, a harsh gargling racket over the water that went round and round the roof, and then a grumble as they shoved along the gallery and settled. Every dusk since the forest had come with a whine at his ears and the biters at his neck and wrists. He waited for them. Only the wind came to his skin.

Of the two suits there was only a faint glow left, in patches, where the film was still alive. He could see the shape of Teva's arm by it and not her face.

Nobody said what they had found today.

To stop his teeth he started talking.

"Topo used to watch me drill Noli," he said. "Every morning, from the edge of the fern gardens. One morning she wouldn't pin. She went straight past the line and into the gardens and lay down in the middle of them, and I had to crawl in after her on my hands and knees."

"Into the gardens," Benal said.

"Everybody heard. And after that, every morning, Topo would sit up when I came out and say, *Who's pinning who today?*" His teeth knocked on it, and the roof gave back a little clatter after the words.

Beside him Teva let out a breath through her nose. It was nearly a laugh.

"Every morning," she said.

"For a month. More. He never got tired of it."

He could see Topo sitting up, pleased, with his bad foot out in front of him. He kept his eyes on it until it went.

"Teva," Benal said, "who do you sit with? At home. I was trying to think, and I couldn't."

"Two girls. You know them. One's Sila's daughter."

"Sila's—"

"They're closer to each other than they are to me."

Keo had never heard her say anything like that. He had seen her with them at the pits, and thought she was the one who held back.

"That's the trouble with three," Benal said. "Possibly."

Teva was quiet. Then she said, "In the evenings I walk Omya down to the Vitarium. She doesn't need to go any more. She goes anyway." The wind came along the gallery and she waited for it to pass. "All the way down she asks me if the figs are in. Every evening, whatever the month. I tell her not yet, and she says you can't hurry a fig."

"Are they?" Keo said. "In?"

"I don't know. I didn't look."

Somewhere in it his teeth had stopped, and Teva's shudders had come further apart.

Then he was standing at the open edge of the gallery, with nothing in front of him but black and the slap of water a long way down. He did not know how he had got there. Benal had him by the arm.

"Keo. Keo. Where are you going?"

"I'm not."

"You went straight to the edge."

The floor tilted under him, slowly, toward the water, though nothing had moved. Then it came back.

"It's the cold," he said.

"Come on," Benal said. "This way. Small steps."

He let Benal turn him. They went back along the gallery, Keo's hand on the wall and Benal's on his arm, toward the faint patches of Teva's suit.

"Keo?" she said.

"Here."

He sat down between them again, and Benal did not let go of his arm. Out at the seaward end the house drew its long breath, and let it go.

It was not even properly night yet.
