As they emerged from the Vitarium into the main council chamber, Keo saw people sitting shoulder to shoulder on the floor and standing packed behind them. They drew in their knees and feet as they walked past. Talk dropped.

"Look at them. There's nothing left of them."

The shaft of light from the roof hole had faded as evening arrived. Lightmold grown into the bare walls now gave every face a faint yellow-green tint.

Out of the corner of his eye, Keo saw Joram and Riel standing against the side wall, a watcher at each shoulder. They started.

The watchers walked them to the dais. Sila sat at its center with her hands open on her knees, with Hesh and other council members nearby. Keo noticed Elana standing with her arms folded at the front of the crowd.

"The keepers have what you brought?" Hesh began.

"Rano has it," Teva said. "He's working."

"Good. That is the keepers' business, and they are about it," Sila said. "This is ours. You went to Genesis, against the word of this Council. Tell us what you found there."

Benal stepped forward with the founders' tablet in both hands and the cylinder under his arm. Keo took a step to stay at his shoulder.

Benal held the tablet up. It glinted. Marks were cut into it, bold and angular. Every eye in the room went to it.

"This is from the room the founders worked in at Genesis. That's their stamp in the corner. Thirty-three months after the jump. <!-- @WF-plates.stamp: +33mo --> Year three."

He didn't stammer. His voice carried.

Keo found himself thinking about how strong Benal was. It was a fact about him, like his height.

On the dais Hesh leaned forward.

"Whose hand is it?"

"Theirs. The same hand as the plates Rano is reading now."

"Read it."

"*Impact, narrowed down to sometime in the next two hundred and twenty years.*" <!-- @WF-dating.fix.margin: 220y -->

Benal stopped and waited while they processed.

"But the founders said centuries more than that!" A man's voice, from the crowd behind Keo.

"The founders brought an estimate with them," Benal said. "They kept working at it, and three years in <!-- @WF-dating.fix.year: 3 --> they had a new one. Nobody who came to Enclave ever knew it."

"And we are to take that from a plate three children carried out of Genesis," Sila said.

Benal shook his head. "Don't take it from me. Take it from them. What we brought of their records is tied down behind Teva's seat, and the seat is in the wig, and the wig is in the river." Heads turned toward the cleft, and back. "Everyone in this room just went down there and looked at it. We didn't make that up. We came home in it."

He lowered the tablet but raised a finger.

"They cut what I just read in year three. That's the stamp. Two hundred twenty years after year three is two hundred twenty-three. This is year two hundred six." <!-- @WF-transit.elapsed: 206y --> <!-- @WF-dating.fix.margin: 220y -->

He waited again, while the room thought about his numbers some more.

"Seventeen years,"<!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y --> someone murmured.

"At the most," another added.

Keo had heard it at Genesis. Now the whole chamber took it in at once and went still. Somewhere behind him a woman began to cry, and stopped herself.

Hesh sat back.

"So probably not seventeen. Anything short of it. Ten. Two. This one." <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y -->

His eyes went to the children in their parents' laps, and to the older ones standing at the back.

"Every child in this room will be here for it. Even most of us older ones."

Sila stood up.

Keo had watched her do that once before, from the back of this room, and had felt something rise in him with her. She got up the same way now, like someone just woken, with the whole room sunk around her.

"Then we have seventeen more years to leave no trace." <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y --> She turned to take in all of them. "Enclave has always had two traces we couldn't avoid, and we did all we could not to add a third. This afternoon a third came over the barrier, and it is lying in our river, and every one of you has seen it. Whatever comes, the future must find nothing of us."

Benal wasn't finished.

"There's something else. What's in every child. The upgrades. I think they're the cure. It's what the founders came here..."

But the room was stuck on the number, and voices piled in from all over the floor, one atop another.

"Seventeen from when? From tonight?"

"It's a guess, not a schedule."

"Seventeen is the *max*!"

"My girl is four."

Teva pushed past Keo onto the open floor below the dais before anyone had given it to her. She stamped, hard, and allowed the harsh echo to reverberate until everyone was shocked into silence. Then she raised a fist at Sila, and her voice filled the chamber.

"You're talking over him. You *always* talk over him!"

Keo's face had gone hot, too. He didn't have the right words, but he was glad she did.

"When Alira died, this room sat here and said wait." She swung round toward the side wall. "And the two best explorers in Enclave sat on that floor and said nothing —"

Keo stirred uncomfortably and opened his mouth.

"— with ten suits in the Repository that they kept for nobody. We went because you wouldn't. Because they wouldn't."

It was his own argument. He had made it to them in the nest on the morning of the debate, and he had never heard it from outside. He didn't like the sound of it.

"The custodians will answer for what they kept, child, and for what they lost," Sila said. "Two suits went out of the Repository, against every rule we have, on the most foolish plan this Council has ever heard, past every taboo the founders left us."

Heads nodded around the floor. A woman near the front had tears running down her face, and she was nodding with them.

"Teva's no child," Keo growled.

"Three children went out." His mother's voice rang out from the side wall. She somehow sounded calm, but there was an iron in her voice that Keo rarely heard. "Three came home. That is what the suits are for."

Keo heard the missing word. She had said *children* went out. *Not children* came home.

Riel looked at Keo, then sideways at Joram.

On the night of the debate, from the back of this room, Keo's father's face had held nothing but fear. Now Keo saw something open and tender there. Joram's eyes swept down over the burns on Keo's arms, to the leg and the torn hands, and came back up to his son's face, wet.

Standing there, Keo felt nineteen days <!-- @WF-story.span: 19d --> arrive in him at once: the cuts and salt tight on his skin, the rib catching at every breath, the wound in his thigh, and his knees wanting to give.

Across the floor, Marek sat in the crowd with Lunet beside him. Both were watching Benal intently.

Benal stepped out toward the dais again. Teva stepped back to give him space.

"The upgrades are the cure. What's in every child in this village is what the founders came here to make. It's on their wall at Genesis, in their own words."

Sila's face lit.

"Then it is true. All of it." She leaned in. "The founders put their work into our children's blood, and we have kept it there, clean, through every seeding for two hundred years. Every rule we kept, we kept for this. The mission was never lost. We were keeping it." She looked round the room. "Have I not said so?"

The room murmured, and some of it was yes. Keo was already moving.

"That isn't what he said," Lunet said sharply.

"No," said Marek. He was on his feet. "It isn't."

"Marek —"

He didn't stop for her. He walked out across the floor, talking in the voice Keo had heard go up and up at the debate.

"I told my son his work was useless. Dead symbols, I called it, at my own table, more times than I can count. And I was so wrong. He is the best mind in this room, and he did what none of us would."

He stopped at Benal's side. Keo came up a step behind him and took the other.

Benal stood between them with the tablet against his chest.

"It isn't," Benal said. "The cure is real. But only the founders could ever have carried it home." He looked up at Sila. "The jump back has to hold on to a time you've already lived in. They'd lived there. Nobody born here has. Nobody in this room was ever going home."

Nobody spoke over him this time.

"Nobody doubts him, Marek," Hesh said. "It's just more than one night can hold."

"What do we tell the yazhi?" someone called.

Sila opened her mouth, but nothing came out.

Hesh cleared his throat. "The children are home," he said, "and the keepers are fixing the cultures. That is a mercy. Let's adjourn. We can decide what to do about the suits and the custodians tomorrow."
