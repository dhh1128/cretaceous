Elana lifted her daughter off the stone.

Nobody helped her, and nobody tried. She carried Alira out of the Vitarium and along the narrow passage with the child's head against her shoulder, the way you carry a child who has fallen asleep somewhere inconvenient, and Teva went after her because she could not think of anything else to do. She left her grandmother behind her in the Vitarium, and did not go back.

They crossed the Council chamber under the yellow-green of the lightmold and went up the throat, and Elana put her back to the ceratops hide and pushed through it into the night.

It was dark outside, with no moon and no stars, and the air was close and did not move. Beyond the open ground the nests went up into the conifers and were lost there. Low fires still marked the cook pits past the last nests, as if nothing had happened.

Elana knelt on the open ground a few steps from the hide and laid Alira down there.

That was all it took. People came. They came out of the kiva behind Teva and down from the nests, and they came carrying mats, whatever their households had, rolled under their arms, and they set them down at the edge of the ground and stood. Someone brought embers from the pits in a clay pot and fed them until they gave enough light to see faces by.

A woman Teva knew only by sight brought a water skin and a cloth and knelt beside Elana, and Elana took the cloth and began to wash her daughter's face. She washed the forehead, and the eyelids, and the small hard chin, and a man at the front said, "She used to stand at the bottom of our ladder and shout up that she was coming, and then not come."

Somebody made a sound that was half a laugh, and someone else was crying already.

"She counted the rungs on our ladder every time," said a girl of about eight, loudly. "She always got to nine. There are twelve."

"She took my sandal," said someone else. "I never got it back."

They went on, in no order, with nobody leading it. More people spoke while Elana washed her daughter's hands, one and then the other, finger by finger. She wept the whole time without making any sound, and nobody looked away from her. The man who had spoken first had his face in his hands.

Teva had one thing. She had seen Alira at the yazhi pool on Elana's hip, too young to be drilled, watching the older ones with her thumb in her mouth. She did not say it.

When the remembering was done, there was a silence, and then the names began. There was already a sharpness in the air.

Each of them said it once. The man at the front, and the girl, and the woman with the water skin, one at a time and in no order, *Alira*, *Alira*, quiet, the way you say the name of someone in the next room. It went round the crowd and back. Teva said it when it came to her, and her own voice sounded strange in her ears, and then it went on past her.

Elana said it last.

Then she covered her. She took the mats people handed her and laid them one over another until there was only a shape, and somebody gave her the balm in its pot, and she opened the pot and spread the balm over the mats with her own hands, all of it, the way it was done.

Teva's arms had folded tight across her ribs, and her eyes had gone to the fire and away from the mats, before she knew it. Her body had done this before. It remembered where to put her hands and where not to look before the rest of her did.

Somewhere in the village, someone laughed.

At the edge of the firelight, one of the Council elders lifted her head. "The Council will sit tomorrow," she said, "at dusk."

Nobody answered her. The Council had sat once already, when the flood took the two Masters, and decided to wait. Teva's arms tightened across her ribs until it hurt.

Then they drew back. People picked up their own children and went home in ones and twos, and someone carried the pot of embers round the kiva's shoulder to the far side, where they could no longer see the mats, and Elana went with it and sat down. A few stayed with her a while. Teva stayed.

By the time the last of the others had gone, the smell had reached the fire. It came round the shoulder of rock, faint at first and then not faint, sharp and clean and chemical, the kind that gets in behind the eyes and makes them run, and nothing at all like rot. By the small hours it was so strong that Teva breathed through her mouth and her eyes streamed and she let them.

Elana did not seem to notice it. She sat with her knees drawn up and looked at the fire.

Some time after the last of the others had gone, Teva got up and found the water skin where the woman had left it and brought it to her. Elana drank, and gave it back, and Teva set it down where she could reach it. Teva sat down again on the other side of the fire and fed it a stick when it needed one.

They did not speak.

The dark thinned slowly, a long time before it was light. When it did, Elana stood up and walked a few steps out, past the shoulder of the rock, to where she could see, and stood there. After a while Teva got up and stood a little behind her.

Across the open ground, the mats lay flat.

From where they stood there was nothing to see but that: a few woven mats lying on bare earth, the way mats lie when somebody has put them down and gone away.

Elana looked at them for a long time. Then she turned and walked toward the nests, and did not look back.

It was morning. Teva did not go home.
