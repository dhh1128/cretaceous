Elana lifted her daughter off the stone.

Nobody helped her, and nobody tried. She carried Alira out of the Vitarium and along the narrow passage with the child's head against her shoulder, the way you carry a child who has fallen asleep somewhere inconvenient, and Teva went after her because she could not think of anything else to do. She left her grandmother behind her in the Vitarium, and did not go back.

They crossed the Council chamber under the yellow-green of the lightmold and went up the throat, and Elana put her back to the ceratops hide and pushed through it into the night.

It was dark outside, with no moon and no stars, and the air was close and did not move. Beyond the open ground the nests went up into the conifers and were lost there. Low fires still marked the cook pits past the last of them, as if nothing had happened.

Elana did not stop. She walked straight across the open ground and in among the nest trees, with the child against her shoulder, and Teva understood where she was going and went with her.

People came. Nobody called them. A ladder creaked overhead, then another. They came down out of the dark and fell in behind, carrying mats, whatever their households had, rolled under their arms. Some of them had been asleep. At the cook pits a man scooped embers into a clay pot without being asked and carried it after them, and its small red light went along at the back of the crowd, bobbing, so that Teva could see the feet ahead of her and not much more.

They went out past the last nests and into the groves, along the path between the fig rows, and on, a long way, until the trees stopped. Halfway there Elana's father came up beside Elana and held out his arms. She shook her head without looking at him, and he dropped back. She carried her daughter the whole way.

The returning ground was bare earth at the far edge of the ring, downwind of everything, where nobody walked unless they had a reason. Nothing on it was marked. Nothing ever was. Elana went out onto it a little way, to a place nobody had used that Teva could see. She knelt and laid Alira down.

The people with mats set them down at the edge of the ground and stood. The man with the pot of embers fed it until it gave enough light to see faces by.

A woman Teva knew only by sight brought a water skin and a cloth and knelt beside Elana, and Elana took the cloth and began to wash her daughter's face. She washed the forehead, the eyelids, the small hard chin. A man at the front said, "She used to stand at the bottom of our ladder and shout up that she was coming, and then not come."

Somebody made a sound that was half a laugh, and someone else was crying already.

"She counted the rungs from her mother's back," said a girl of about eight, loudly. "All the way up. She always got to twenty and then started again at twelve."

"She took my sandal," said someone else. "I never got it back."

They went on, in no order, with nobody leading it. More people spoke while Elana washed her daughter's hands, one and then the other, finger by finger. She wept the whole time without making any sound, and nobody looked away from her. The man who had spoken first had his face in his hands.

Teva had one thing. She had seen Alira at the yazhi pool on Elana's hip, too young to be drilled, watching the older ones with her thumb in her mouth. She did not say it.

When the remembering was done, there was a silence, and then the names began. There was already a sharpness in the air.

Each of them said it once. The man at the front, and the girl, and the woman with the water skin, one at a time and in no order, *Alira*, *Alira*, quiet, the way you say the name of someone in the next room. It went round the crowd and back. Teva said it when it came to her. Her own voice sounded strange in her ears, and then the name went on past her.

Elana said it last.

Somebody gave her the balm in its pot. She opened it and spread the balm on her daughter with her own hands, the face she had just washed, the arms, the small feet, all of it, the way it was done. Then she took the mats people handed her and laid them over Alira one after another until there was only a shape.

Teva's arms had folded tight across her ribs, and her eyes had gone to the fire and away from the mats, before she knew it. Her body had done this before. It remembered where to put her hands and where not to look before the rest of her did.

Somewhere back in the center of the village, a child laughed.

One of the Council elders lifted her head. "The Council will sit tomorrow," she said, "at dusk."

Nobody answered her. The Council had sat once already, when the flood took the two Masters, and decided to wait. Teva's arms tightened across her ribs until it hurt.

Then they drew back. People picked up their own children and went home in ones and twos. The man with the pot of embers carried it back to the first trees of the grove, where the mats could no longer be seen, and Elana went with it and sat down. A few stayed with her a while. Teva stayed.

By the time the last of the others had gone, the smell had reached the fire. It came back through the trees, faint at first and then not faint, sharp and clean and chemical, the kind that gets in behind the eyes and makes them run, and nothing at all like rot. By the small hours it was so strong that Teva breathed through her mouth and her eyes streamed and she let them.

Elana did not seem to notice it. She sat with her knees drawn up and looked at the fire.

Some time after the last of the others had gone, Teva got up and found the water skin where the woman had left it and brought it to her. Elana drank and gave it back, and Teva set it down where she could reach it. Teva sat down again on the other side of the fire and fed it a stick when it needed one.

They did not speak.

The dark thinned slowly, a long time before it was light. When it did, Elana stood up and walked out to the edge of the trees, to where she could see, and stood there. After a while Teva got up and stood a little behind her.

Across the open ground, the mats lay flat.

From where they stood there was nothing to see but that: a few woven mats lying on bare earth, the way mats lie when somebody has put them down and gone away.

Elana looked at them for a long time. Then she turned and walked toward the nests, and did not look back.

It was morning. Teva did not go home.
