Elana lifted her daughter off the stone.

She carried Alira out along the narrow passage with the child's head against her shoulder, the way you carry a child who has fallen asleep somewhere inconvenient. Teva went after her because she could not think of anything else to do, and left her grandmother behind in the Vitarium.

They crossed the Council chamber under the yellow-green of the lightmold and went up the throat, and Elana put her back to the ceratops hide and pushed through it into the night.

Outside there was no moon and no stars, and the air was close and still. Beyond the open ground the nests went up into the conifers and were lost there. Fires burned low at the cook pits past the last of them.

Elana walked straight across the open ground and in among the nest trees. People came. Nobody called them. A ladder creaked overhead, then another. They came down out of the dark and fell in behind with mats rolled under their arms, whatever their households had. Some of them had been asleep. At the cook pits a man scooped embers into a clay pot and carried it after them, its small red light bobbing at the back of the crowd, so that Teva could see the feet ahead of her and not much more.

They went out past the last nests, between the fig rows of the groves, and on a long way, until the trees stopped. Halfway there Elana's father came up beside Elana and held out his arms. She shook her head without looking at him, and he dropped back. She carried her daughter the whole way.

The returning ground was bare earth at the far edge of the ring, downwind of everything. Nothing on it was marked. Nothing ever was. Elana went out onto it a little way, to a place nobody had used that Teva could see, and knelt and laid Alira down.

The people with mats set them down at the edge of the ground and stood, and the man with the pot of embers fed it until it gave light enough to see faces by.

A woman Teva knew only by sight knelt beside Elana with a water skin and a cloth, and Elana took the cloth and washed her daughter's face: the forehead, the eyelids, the small hard chin. A man at the front said, "She used to stand at the bottom of our ladder and shout up that she was coming, and then not come."

Somebody made a sound that was half a laugh, and someone else was crying already.

"She counted the rungs from her mother's back," said a girl of about eight, loudly. "All the way up. She always got to twenty and then started again at twelve."

"She took my sandal," said someone else. "I never got it back."

They went on, in no order, while Elana washed her daughter's hands, one and then the other, finger by finger, weeping without a sound. The man who had spoken first had his face in his hands.

Teva had one thing. She held on to it while the others spoke, because it was hardly anything, and because once she said it, it would not be only hers. Then the man at the front took his hands away from his face, and she heard her own voice.

"She used to watch the older ones at the pool," Teva said. "From your hip. With her thumb in her mouth."

Elana looked up at her for the first time. She nodded, and went back to the small hands.

When the remembering was done, there was a silence, and then the names began. There was already a sharpness in the air.

Each of them said it once: the man at the front, the girl, the woman with the water skin, *Alira*, *Alira*, quiet, not loud enough to call anyone. It went around the crowd and back. Keo said it from between his parents, hard, like a challenge, and his mother's face beside him was wet and perfectly still. At the back, a head above everyone, Benal said it so low that Teva saw it more than heard it. When it came to Teva, her own voice sounded strange in her ears, and then the name went on past her.

Elana said it last.

Somebody gave her the balm in its pot, and she spread it on her daughter with her own hands: the face she had just washed, the arms, the small feet, all of it. Then she laid the mats people handed her over Alira, one after another, until there was only a shape.

Teva's arms had folded tight across her ribs, and her eyes had gone to the fire and away from the mats, before she knew it. Her body had done this before.

Somewhere back in the center of the village, a child laughed.

One of the Council elders lifted her head. "The Council will sit tomorrow," she said, "at dusk."

"To wait," a man near the fire muttered. The Council had sat once already, when the flood took the two Masters, and decided to wait. Teva's arms tightened across her ribs until it hurt.

Then they drew back, picking up their own children, going home in ones and twos. Riel, passing with Joram and Keo, put a hand on Teva's arm and was gone into the trees. She had been kind to Teva once or twice, years ago; Teva's mother had admired her, and Omya still did. The man with the embers carried the pot back to the first trees of the grove, where the mats could no longer be seen, and Elana went with it and sat down. A few stayed with her a while. Teva stayed.

By the time the last of the others had gone, the smell had reached the fire. It came back through the trees, faint at first and then not faint, sharp and clean and chemical, the kind that gets in behind the eyes and makes them run, and nothing like rot. By the small hours Teva was breathing through her mouth, her eyes streaming, and she let them.

Elana did not seem to notice it. She sat with her knees drawn up and looked at the fire.

Teva found the water skin where the woman had left it and brought it to her. Elana drank and gave it back. Teva set it where she could reach it, sat down again across the fire, and fed it a stick when it needed one.

A long while later Elana said, without looking up, "You're Omya's girl."

"Yes."

"Go home to her."

"Not yet," Teva said.

Elana nodded at the fire, and they sat on.

The dark thinned. When it was light, Elana walked out to the edge of the trees, where she could see, and stood there. After a while Teva got up and stood a little behind her.

Across the open ground, the mats lay flat.

From where they stood there was nothing to see but that: a few woven mats on bare earth, the way mats lie when somebody has put them down and gone away.

Elana looked at them for a long time. Then she turned and walked toward the nests, and did not look back.

It was morning. Teva did not go home.
