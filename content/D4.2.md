Keo kicked the fire apart in the rain, and the wet ash came up off the coals in a gray breath that went straight into Benal's face. Under it was the smell that had been under everything since the morning: leaves going soft and black on the ground, wood going to mush inside its bark, the whole floor of the world quietly rotting. The others did not seem to notice it. Benal got up last and followed.

The understory rose as they walked. At first it was at his chest, and he could see the sky in pieces over it. Then it was at his shoulders. Then the crowns of the tree ferns were at his face, because he was taller than either of the others, and he walked with his arm up to keep the fronds out of his eyes. When he looked up there was no sky. There were fronds over fronds, green light coming through them, and water dripping off every tip.

The suit kept the far noise off him. It had done that since the tangle, and he still could not quite believe it: the whole buzzing wall of the forest, the thing that had pressed on him every day of his life at home, held off at a distance like a crowd outside a door. What it did not hold off was what was close. Every drip off every frond, near enough to touch, landed in his ear. The ferns hissed on the suits as they pushed through. Keo's boots sucked at the mud ahead of him. Teva breathed. He could hear his own heart.

Then the light changed ahead, and they came out into the clearing.

A big tree had come down there a long time ago, and the sky had come down after it. Light reached all the way to the ground. And the air over the fallen trunk was full of moths.

They were the size of his hand, and there were hundreds of them, more, turning over each other in the sun. Their wings were green, then blue, then a color that was neither, depending on how they turned. Benal stopped. So did the others. He had never seen anything like them. At the edge of hearing there was a sound, so faint he was not sure of it at first: a dry, soft rushing, like breath through reeds, made of all those wings at once. Nobody else gave any sign of hearing it. For once, something reaching him that the others could not hear was beautiful, and he stood in it with his mouth open.

Noli went for them. She leaped at the cloud and snapped and came down with nothing. She leaped again with her bad shoulder held clear, and got nothing again. Then she stopped, crouched in the grass, and turned her head toward the rushing at the same moment he did. She held it there, listening. He looked at her, and she looked at the moths.

Keo said, "Come on," and they went on.

The understory closed over them again, thicker than before. Something had changed, and it took Benal a few steps to know what it was. No air moved. In the clearing there had been a little stir of it; in here there was none. The last shower was coming up off the ground as steam, and his suit kept his body cool and could do nothing at all for his face. Sweat ran into his eyes. He breathed water.

The flies found them there.

They came out of nowhere, small and dark, and went for faces: the corners of the eyes, the lips, the wet of the nostrils. They were on all three of them. Keo had them worst, crawling in the bites on his neck and his torn knees, and he slapped at them and muttered and kept walking. Teva wiped her face with her sleeve every few steps, and kept walking.

For Benal each fly was a separate sound. The whine of one at the rim of his ear was as loud as a voice. When it landed he heard its feet. When it crawled toward his eye he could feel it and hear it at once, and when he brushed it away there were three more, and each one had its own note, and they did not stop. And with every breath the heat brought him everything: the rot underfoot, Keo's sweat and blood, the flies themselves. He could not turn any of it down. It all came in.

He watched the other two walk on through it. They swatted and walked on.

Teva looked back once. "Ben. All right?"

"Wonderful," he said. "I'm thinking of staying." She turned away, and he heard her not quite laugh, and wished he had said something true instead, and knew he would not.

He could tell them. He thought about it for a long time, walking. He could say, *it's worse for me*. They would stop and look at him. Keo would want to fix it, and there would be nothing to fix. He had stopped them twice already today. The first time there had been nothing there at all, and the second time it had been a bird. He had been the one to say the number at the lay-up, too, and he had watched their faces when he said it. And Keo was being eaten alive in front of him without a suit and had not said one word about it.

A boy who was going to get them all killed did not complain about flies.

At his returning, he thought, somebody would have to say what had taken him. *Flies. He was bothered by flies.* It was quite funny. He said it to Keo's back, under his breath, and Keo said, "What?" and he said, "Nothing," and that was funnier.

The rain came through the fronds a little later, a hiss far off and then everywhere, and he reached up and raised his helmet.

It came up over his skull and down around his neck, and the rain struck it. It was a roar. It was a steady drumming on a shell an inch from his ears, so close and so loud that there was nothing else, and for a moment he did not understand what had happened to him.

Then he did. Nothing was reaching him. The flies were still there, he could feel them, but he could not hear them. The drips, the ferns on the suits, Keo's boots, his own heart: gone, all of it, under the roar. He had not heard so little since the day he was born. He walked inside it, and his shoulders came down from around his ears. He thought he might cry. He did not.

It was a little while before he noticed what else he could not hear. He could not hear Keo ahead of him. He could see him, a shape in the ferns, but there was no sound to it. He could not hear Noli at all. He could not hear his own feet on the ground.

There was a log across the way, old and dark with wet, and Keo stepped over it. Benal stepped over it after him, and his back foot came down on the wood.

It went. There must have been a crack, the kind he would have heard on any other afternoon of his life and stepped back from. He had not heard it. His leg went down through the rotten wood to the shin, and termites boiled out of the break, pale, over his foot. He pitched forward into the ferns with one leg trapped behind him. The suit took the knock. It hurt less than it should have, and he lay there with his face in the wet and the roar on his helmet and could not hear himself breathe.

A hand closed on his arm. Keo hauled him out of the log in one pull and set him on his feet. He looked him over, and his mouth made a shape under the roar that was probably *all right?*, and Benal nodded, and Keo went on.

Benal thought he knew what Keo had seen. The tall one, the clumsy one, falling through a log that Keo had stepped over without thinking. It was what everybody saw. He brushed the termites off his leg and followed.

He did not say anything about it. Not the flies, not the log, not that the rain had taken his ears. He could have lowered the helmet. He kept it up, because the roar on it was the only quiet he had had since the clearing, and he could not make himself give it back.
