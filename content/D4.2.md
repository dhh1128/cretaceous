The fire had burned low through the middle of the day. Benal was still sitting by it when Keo kicked it apart in the rain, and the wet ash came up off the coals in a gray breath, straight into his face. Under it was the smell that had been under everything since the morning: leaves going soft and black on the ground, wood going to mush inside its bark, the whole floor of the world rotting. The others did not seem to notice it. Benal got up last and followed.

The understory rose as they walked. First it was at his chest, with the sky in pieces over it. Then at his shoulders. Then the tree-fern crowns were at his face, because he was taller than the others, and he walked with an arm up to keep the fronds out of his eyes. Overhead there was no sky, only fronds over fronds, green light coming through them, and water dripping off every tip.

The suit kept the far noise off him, the whole buzzing wall of the forest held off like a crowd outside a door. It did not hold off what was close. Every drip off every frond landed in his ear. The ferns hissed on the suits. Keo's boots sucked at the mud ahead of him. Teva breathed. He could hear his own heart.

Then the light changed ahead, and they came out into a clearing.

A big tree had come down there long ago, and the sky had come down after it. Light reached all the way to the ground. The air over the fallen trunk was full of moths.

They were the size of his hand, hundreds of them, more, turning over each other in the sun: green, then blue, then a color that was neither as they turned. Benal stopped. So did the others. At the edge of hearing there was something, so faint he was not sure of it at first.

Teva was watching him and not the moths. "What do you hear?"

"Them. The wings." He listened. "All of them at once. It's kind of a rushing, like breath through reeds. Louder when they turn together. There. Like that."

Keo tipped his head, listened, and shook it. Teva stared at the cloud as if she might hear it if she looked hard enough.

For once, something reaching him that the others could not hear was beautiful, and somebody had asked him about it. He stood in it with his mouth open.

Noli went for them. She leaped at the cloud and snapped and came down with nothing. She leaped again with her bad shoulder held clear, and got nothing again. Then she crouched in the ferns and turned her head toward the rushing at the same moment he did, and held it there, listening.

Keo said, "Come on," and they went on.

The understory closed over them again, thicker. No air moved. The last shower was coming up off the ground as steam, and his suit kept his body cool and could do nothing for his face. Sweat ran into his eyes. He breathed water.

The flies found them there.

They were small, and went for faces: the corners of the eyes, the lips, the wet of the nostrils. They were on all three of them. Keo had them worst, crawling in the bites on his neck and his torn knees. He slapped at them and grinned back over his shoulder. "They like me best. Look at that. I'm the only one of us worth eating."

Teva wiped her face with her sleeve every few steps, and kept walking.

For Benal each fly was a separate sound. The whine of one at the rim of his ear was as loud as a voice. When it landed he heard its feet. When he brushed it away there were three more, each with its own note. And every breath of the heat brought him everything: the rot underfoot, Keo's sweat and blood, the flies themselves. He could not turn any of it down.

Teva looked back once. "Ben. All right?"

"Wonderful," he said. "I'm thinking of staying." She turned away, and he heard her not quite laugh. He wished he had said something true.

He could tell them. He could say, *it's worse for me*. They would stop and look at him. Keo would want to fix it, and there was nothing to fix. He had stopped them twice already today, once for nothing and once for a bird. He had said the number at the lay-up and watched their faces. And Keo was being eaten alive in front of him without a suit, and joking about it.

A boy who was going to get them all killed did not complain about flies.

At his returning, he thought, somebody would have to say what had taken him. *Flies. He was bothered by flies.* It was pretty funny. He said it to Keo's back, under his breath, and Keo said, "What?" and he said, "Nothing," and that was funnier.

The rain came through the fronds a little later, a hiss far off and then everywhere, and he reached up and raised his helmet.

It came up over his skull and down around his neck, and the rain struck it: a roar, a drumming on a shell a finger's width from his ears. For a moment he did not understand what had happened to him.

Then he did. Nothing was reaching him. He could feel the flies but not hear them. The drips, the ferns on the suits, Keo's boots, his own heart: gone under the roar. He had not heard so little since the day he was born. His shoulders came down from around his ears. He thought he might cry. He did not.

It was a little while before he noticed what else he could not hear. Keo ahead of him was a shape in the ferns with no sound to it. He could not hear Noli at all, or his own feet on the ground.

There was a log across the way, old and sodden, and Keo stepped over it. Benal stepped over it after him, and his back foot came down on the wood.

It went. There must have been a crack, the kind he would have heard on any other afternoon of his life and stepped back from. His leg plunged through the rotten wood to the shin, and termites boiled out of the break, pale, over his foot. He pitched forward into the ferns with one leg trapped behind him. The suit took the knock. He lay with his face in the rot and the roar on his helmet and could not hear himself breathe.

A hand closed on his arm. Keo hauled him out of the log in one pull, set him on his feet, and looked him over. His mouth made a shape under the roar that was probably *all right?* Benal nodded, and Keo went on.

Benal thought he knew what Keo had seen: the tall one, the clumsy one, falling through a log Keo had stepped over without thinking. It was what everybody saw. He brushed the termites off his leg and followed.

He did not say anything about it. Not the flies, not the log, not that the rain had taken his ears. He kept the helmet up, because the roar on it was the only quiet he had had since the clearing, and he could not make himself give it back.
