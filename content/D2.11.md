Noli went to ground in the ferns between two roots of the big conifer and flattened there, and her unease came to him down the bond. Everything here smelled of people.

The Repository had no wall and no roof that anyone would notice. It was dug in under the conifer's roots where they ran into a pile of limestone boulders, and its door was one of the boulders, a slab nobody had ever shaped. Keo put both hands flat on it, the way his mother did, and felt along its edge to the crack where the cord came down, stiff and slick with grease. His parents had shown him where it was because they trusted him with it. He had never once opened it without them.

He pulled, hand over hand. Somewhere above him in the boulders the net of stones went down and the cord creaked over its saddle, and the slab lifted until there was room under it. He went through sideways, then let the cord out slowly on the inside so the slab would not knock as it came down. The night was gone.

Lightmold glowed yellow-green across the ceiling, steady and dim. The air was cool and damp with the limestone, and under the damp was the smell that belonged to this room and nowhere else, sharp and dry like the air after lightning. Racks of lashed branches stood in rows back into the dimness, built to hold far more than they held. Nearly all were bare. His parents could tell the story of every suit that was no longer there.

The weapons were along the near wall, with the Explorers' kit. He took two croc-spikes and ran his thumb over each tip, a croc's tooth bound onto hardwood, and laid them by the door. He took a razor-club, its head slotted with razortail teeth, and a sling and its pouch of round pebbles. Every edge in the room had come out of a mouth, except the ones on the shelf behind him.

When he turned to that shelf, it was only to look.

The things on it were wrapped, every one, in bindings the color of old bark, and the shelf smelled faintly of figs gone sour. Something lived in those bindings, asleep. If the custodians missed one week's balm, or the chamber flooded, it would wake and eat whatever it was wrapped around, metal to rust and the rest to sludge, inside a few hours. Keeping it asleep was his parents' charge. They had been down here this morning doing it.

The long, flat bundle at the end of the shelf was the machete. A blade like that would go through a hanging vine at a stroke. It would also be the worst thing anyone could carry out past the tangle, worse than any suit, because a blade lost out there could survive to be found. He did not touch the shelf.

The suits hung at the back, on one pole.

There were twelve of them, the last in the world, <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 --> and in the dim room they glowed faintly, because the living film was never quite dark while it was healthy. His parents' two hung near the middle. He knew the patches on his mother's, and the place on his father's sleeve where the film had grown back paler than the rest. He left them alone.

Teva's had to be the small one, because nothing in a suit could be let out or taken in, and there was only one small one. He lifted it off the pole. It weighed almost nothing, as if it were made of the light it gave off. Its film was scarred, though, and dark in patches where the cells had died and not grown back. He turned the wrist toward him. The panel woke under his thumb and showed him the thermal mark. It was green.

It would hide her worse than the others would. It would keep her cool, and that mattered more. He held it a moment longer than he needed to. She would be in it by morning. Then he folded it and laid it on the floor.

Benal's size was easy to find. When Keo woke its panel, a red line crossed the mark for one shoulder, and the mark for the shock grid below it was dim. Keo had seen his mother frown at warnings like those and never asked what they meant. Ben would work it out, and tell him a great deal more about it than he wanted to hear. It would have to do.

He folded it on top of Teva's.

The third suit on the pole was his size.

It hung there whole and healthy, faintly lit. Ben would have had a number for leaving it, and put *possibly* in front of it. Teva would have asked him why. He knew what he would have told them both. He had his ears and his nose, he healed faster than anyone he knew, and he could hold an animal with nothing but his head. He left it hanging.

He was bending for the suits when Noli's fear came down the bond.

It hit all at once: not the long dull dread of the Barrier but something sharp and close. Then, from above him and to one side, a cough came down the air shaft, small and dry. A foot scuffed stone.

Keo went still between two of the empty racks.

He reached up through the rock with his sense: a person, moving slowly along over his head. A Watcher walking the round, and whoever it was might have ears as good as his, or a nose. He stood with his hands open at his sides and breathed through his mouth, slowly. Down the bond Noli was a hard knot of fear in the ferns. He pushed stillness at her, the picture of lying flat, and could not tell whether it reached her.

The footsteps went on along the round, unhurried, and faded.

He waited a while longer. Then he knelt and fed the folded suits through the straps of his fieldpack, one on each side, and shrugged it on to try the weight. The suits made it bulky and no heavier. He put the sling and its pebbles in the pack and settled the spikes and the club on his shoulder.

The pot of septic gel stood by the door, small and heavy and stoppered tight. He checked the stopper twice before he packed it. Noli would be walking beside the pack.

At the door he found the cord and pulled, and the slab came up creaking. He turned the spikes end-on and ducked under it, keeping the cord in his fist.

From outside he looked once more down the room, past the empty racks to the pole at the back. Then he let the cord slide through his hand, slowly, and the door came down on the third suit.
