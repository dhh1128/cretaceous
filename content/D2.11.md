Noli went to ground in the ferns between two roots of the big conifer and flattened there, and her unease came to him down the bond. Everything here smelled of people.

The Repository had no wall and no roof that anyone would notice. It was dug in under the conifer's roots where they ran into a pile of limestone boulders, and its door was one of the boulders, a slab nobody had ever shaped. Keo put both hands flat on it, the way his mother did, and felt down along the stone until his fingers found the grip. His parents had shown him where it was because they trusted him with it. He had never once opened it without them.

He pulled the grip, and the slab floated back on a long hiss of escaping air. He went through sideways. Behind him it hissed shut. The night was gone.

Inside it was the same yellow-green as the kiva, from the lightmold across the ceiling, steady and dim and doing nothing. The air was cool and damp with the limestone, and under the damp there was the other smell, the one that belonged to this room and nowhere else, sharp and dry like the air after lightning. Racks of lashed branches stood in rows back into the dimness, built to hold far more than they held. Nearly all were bare. His parents could tell the story of every suit that was no longer there.

The weapons were along the near wall, with the Explorers' kit. He took two croc-spikes and ran his thumb over each tip, a croc's tooth bound onto hardwood, and laid them by the door. He took a razor-club, its head slotted with razortail teeth, and a sling, and the sling's pouch of round pebbles. Every edge in the room had come out of a mouth, except the ones on the shelf behind him.

He did not turn around for that shelf straight away. When he did, it was only to look.

The things on it were wrapped, every one, in bindings the color of old bark, and the shelf smelled faintly of figs gone sour. He knew what was in the wrappings without unwrapping them. Something lived in those bindings, asleep. If the custodians missed one week's balm, or the chamber flooded, it would wake and eat whatever it was wrapped around, metal to rust and the rest to sludge, inside a few hours. Keeping it asleep was his parents' charge. They had been down here this morning doing it.

The long, flat bundle at the end of the shelf was the machete. He looked at it a moment longer than at the others. A blade like that would go through a hanging vine at a stroke. It would also be the worst thing anyone could carry out past the tangle, worse than any suit, because a blade lost out there could survive to be found. He left it where it was. He did not touch the shelf at all.

The suits hung at the back, on one pole.

There were twelve of them, the last in the world, <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 --> and in the dim room they gave off a faint light of their own, because the living film over them was never quite dark while it was healthy. His parents' two hung near the middle. He knew every patch on his mother's. He knew the place on his father's sleeve where the film had grown back paler than the rest. He did not touch either one.

Teva's had to be the small one, because sizes were fixed and nothing in a suit could be let out or taken in. There was only one small one. He lifted it off the pole. It weighed almost nothing, the way suits always did, as if it had been made of the light it gave off. Its film was scarred, though, and dark in patches where the cells had died and not grown back. He turned the wrist toward him. The panel there woke under his thumb and showed him the thermal mark. It was green.

It would hide her worse than the others would. It would keep her cool. He folded it and laid it on the floor.

Benal's size was easy to find. When Keo woke its panel there was a red line across the mark for one shoulder, and the mark for the shock grid below it was dark. He had seen his mother frown at warnings like those. He had never asked what they meant. It would have to do.

He took it down and folded it on top of Teva's.

The third suit on the pole was his size.

It hung there whole and healthy, faintly lit, and his hand did not go to it. He did not need it. He had his ears and his nose, he healed faster than anyone he knew, and he could hold an animal with nothing but his head. He left it hanging.

He was bending for the suits when Noli's fear came down the bond.

It came all at once, a weight dropped on him from outside: not the long dull dread of the Barrier but something sharp and close. Something had frightened her. Then, from above him and to one side, he heard a cough. It came down the air shaft, small and dry. Then a foot scuffed stone.

Keo went still between two of the empty racks.

He reached up through the rock with his sense, and it was there: a person, moving slowly along over his head. That was all the sense would give him. It could not say who. A Watcher was walking the round, and whoever it was might have ears as good as his own, or a nose as good. He stood with his hands open at his sides and breathed through his mouth, slowly. Down the bond Noli was a hard knot of fear in the ferns. He pushed stillness at her, the picture of lying flat, and could not tell whether it reached her. He could only hope.

The footsteps went on along the round, unhurried, and faded.

He waited a while longer. Then he knelt and fed the folded suits through the straps of his fieldpack, one on each side, and shrugged the pack back on to try the weight. It was awkward, and lighter than it looked. He put the sling and its pebbles in the pack, then settled the spears and the club on his shoulder.

The pot of septic gel stood by the door, small and heavy and stoppered tight. He checked the stopper twice before he packed the pot. Noli would be walking beside the pack.

At the door he found the grip again and pulled, and the slab floated back. He turned the spears end-on.

He looked once more down the room, past the empty racks to the pole at the back. Then he went out, and the door floated shut on the third suit.
