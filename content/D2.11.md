Noli went to ground in the ferns between two roots of the big conifer, and her unease came to him down the bond. Everything here smelled of people.

The Repository had no wall and no roof that anyone would notice. It was dug in under the conifer's roots where they ran into a pile of limestone boulders, and its door was one of the boulders, a slab nobody had ever shaped. Keo put both hands flat on it, the way his mother did, and felt along its edge to the crack where the cord came down, stiff and slick with grease. His parents had shown him where it was because they trusted him with it. He had never opened it without them.

He pulled, hand over hand. Up in the boulders the net of stones went down and the cord creaked over its saddle, and the slab lifted until there was room under it. He went through sideways and let the cord out slowly on the inside so the slab would not knock as it came down. The night was gone.

Lightmold glowed yellow-green across the ceiling. The air was cool and damp with limestone, and under the damp was the smell that belonged to this room and nowhere else, sharp and dry like the air after lightning. Racks of lashed branches stood in rows back into the dimness, nearly all of them bare. His parents could tell the story of every suit that was gone.

The weapons hung along the near wall with the Explorers' kit. He took two croc-spikes, thumbed each tip, a croc's tooth bound onto hardwood, and laid them by the door. He took a razor-club, its head slotted with razortail teeth, and a sling with its pouch of round pebbles. Every edge in the room had come out of a mouth, except the ones on the shelf behind him.

He turned to that shelf only to look.

Everything on it was wrapped in bark-brown bindings, and the shelf smelled of figs gone sour. Something lived in those bindings, asleep. If the custodians missed one week's balm, or the chamber flooded, it would wake and eat whatever it was wrapped around, metal to rust and the rest to sludge, inside a few hours. Keeping it asleep was his parents' charge.

The long, flat bundle at the end was the machete. It would go through a hanging vine at a stroke. It would also be the worst thing anyone could carry past the tangle, worse than any suit, because a blade lost out there could survive to be found. He did not touch the shelf.

The suits hung at the back, on one pole.

There were twelve, the last in the world, <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 --> glowing faintly in the dim, because the living film was never quite dark while it was healthy. He knew the patches on his mother's, and the place on his father's sleeve where the film had grown back paler.

Teva's had to be the small one, the only one that would fit her. He lifted it off the pole. It weighed almost nothing, as if it were made of the light it gave off. Its film was scarred, though, dark in patches where the cells had died. He turned the wrist toward him, and the panel woke under his thumb: the thermal mark was green.

It would hide her worse than the others would. It would keep her cool, and that mattered more. He held it a moment longer than he needed to. She would be in it by morning. Then he folded it and laid it on the floor.

Benal's size was easy to find. Its panel showed a red line across the mark for one shoulder, and the shock-grid mark below it was dim. Keo had seen his mother frown at warnings like those and never asked what they meant. Ben would work it out, and tell him a great deal more than he wanted to hear. It would have to do.

The third suit on the pole was his size.

It hung there whole and healthy, faintly lit. Ben would have had a number for leaving it, with *possibly* in front of it. Teva would have asked him why. He knew what he'd have told them both: he had his ears and his nose, he healed faster than anyone he knew, and he could hold an animal with nothing but his head. He left it hanging.

He was bending for the suits when Noli's fear came down the bond, all at once: not the long dull dread of the Barrier but something sharp and close. Then, from above and to one side, a cough came down the air shaft, small and dry. A foot scuffed stone.

Keo went still between two empty racks.

He reached up through the rock with his sense: a person, moving slowly along over his head. A Watcher walking the round, and whoever it was might have ears as good as his, or a nose. He stood with his hands open and breathed through his mouth. Down the bond Noli was a hard knot of fear in the ferns. He pushed stillness at her, the picture of lying flat, and could not tell whether it reached her.

The footsteps went on along the round, unhurried, and faded.

He waited. Then he knelt and fed the folded suits through the straps of his fieldpack, one on each side, and shrugged it on. The suits made it bulky and no heavier. He put the sling and pebbles in the pack and settled the spikes and the club on his shoulder.

The pot of septic gel stood by the door, small and heavy and stoppered tight. He checked the stopper twice before he packed it. Noli would be walking beside the pack.

At the door he pulled the cord, and the slab came up creaking. He turned the spikes end-on and ducked under it, keeping the cord in his fist.

From outside he looked once more down the room, past the empty racks to the pole at the back. Then he let the cord slide slowly through his hand, and the door came down on the third suit.
