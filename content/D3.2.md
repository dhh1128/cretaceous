Benal could separate their clicks.

The suit had taken the insects away, and in the space where they had been, each click stood by itself, dry and exact. There was one ahead and a little to the left. Another answered it from the right, further off. A third came from somewhere behind the first, and more answered further out, and then the first one again, closer. The faint air coming down the slope brought the rest: wet feathers, and something rank under them, like a mouth that had eaten and not been cleaned. Keo's hand was still tight on his arm. Keo had told them not to move, and Benal, who had never wanted to do anything less in his life than move, was not moving.

Then Keo's hand opened.

Benal felt it go before he understood it. Keo's head had turned, sharp, toward the place in the fog where Noli had gone, the way a man's head turns when somebody calls his name. Whatever had come down the bond from her, it was bad. Keo took one step, then he was walking. Then the fog had him: a shape, a smear, gone, out ahead with his spear and his pack and no suit.

Benal opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

Beside him Teva had not moved at all. She stood with the spike held low and pointed away from her, and she was listening too. She could not hear what he could hear.

Out in the fog, something small clawed its way up out of the ground. A grounder, Benal thought, out of its burrow. He heard it go, a scrabble in leaf litter and then a frantic crashing away through the ferns to the right, and two of the clicks, then a third, turned and went after it at speed. *Keo*, he thought. Keo had done that, from out there, with his head. Benal did not know how. He only knew that three were going one way, after whatever it was, and the rest were not.

The rest were moving, and he could follow them as they shifted.

"They've gone round," he whispered. It came out whole, without catching once, because it was not fear now, it was a sum. "The ones that are left. They've gone round between us and him. Listen. That one, and that one, and the one that keeps going quiet. They're closing on him, not on us. He's in the middle of them."

Teva turned her head toward him. He could see her face over the suit and nothing else of her.

Then, ahead, it went wrong all at once.

Noli shrieked. It was a sound he had never heard an animal make, high and ragged and full of something like outrage, and under it there was a heavy rush in the ferns and a thud of a body going down. Keo was shouting with no words in it. Then he shouted her name, and then there was only his breath. Something struck Keo's spear in the fog. Keo grunted.

Teva ran.

She ran straight at it, into the fog, with the spike low at her side, and Benal found that he was running after her with the club in both hands, because the only thing worse than going toward that noise was standing in the fern by himself and listening to it.

He saw it in pieces. Its tail was held stiff behind it, longer than his arm, and its feathers were the color of mud. The low body was on top of Noli with its feet clamped into her shoulder, snapping at her neck while she thrashed and screamed under it. A second one was backing off from Keo's spear, which lay in the ferns now where he had dropped it. Keo was on his knees beside Noli with a long rake of claw marks down his forearm, both hands out, his face clenched shut. Benal had seen that look on him at the tangle, when he held Noli, but it was doing nothing now. The animal on top of her did not so much as turn its head.

Teva did not stab it. She brought the gelled tooth down beside its head, close enough to touch its nose, and held it there.

The razortail let go as if it had been burned. It came off Noli in one convulsive leap, shrieking, twisting in the air, snapping at nothing, and hit the ground already running away. It struck another razortail, which bolted too. Then the clicking scattered across the fog on every side, fast, and getting further.

Noli came up off the ground in a panic of her own, straight away from the reek. Keo threw himself across her, took his weight on his knees, and held on. There were two small bloody holes in the feathers of her shoulder.

"Run," said Teva.

None of them knew whether it was over. They ran. Keo had his spear back in one hand and a fist in Noli's feathers, and his forearm was running red. Benal had the club, which was heavy and useless and which he did not think to drop. Teva ran last, glancing back, the spike held low toward the fog. The ground went up under them into a low ridge of limestone, and they went over it on hands and knees. Benal felt the rock drag at the knees of the suit and saw the living film go dark in patches where it scraped. Keo's leggings split at the knees, and the stone opened his skin.

They went on past the ridge and down the other side, and on, until the fog was thinner and the light was coming.

Benal stopped first. The clicking had thinned, and drawn off, and stopped. He stood with his chest heaving and listened until he trusted the silence, and then a while longer.

Then his knees folded. He was on all fours in the ferns, bringing up everything he had and then nothing, his whole body heaving at a stomach with nothing left in it. He could not stop. He could feel them both watching, and that was worse than the retching.

When it let him go he stayed where he was, on his knees, and looked up at Keo.

"You —" His voice caught. He made it go on. "You r-reckless, unsuited idiot." And then it did not catch at all. "You told us not to move, and then you walked out there with nothing on, on your own, into the middle of them, and if Teva hadn't come you'd be lying out there now. You'd both be lying out there."

He had never said anything like it to Keo in his life. He did not know what would happen.

"He's right," said Teva. She had not raised her voice. "He worked out where they'd gone. That's why I knew where to run."

Keo did not argue. He did not agree either. He knelt by Noli with one hand still in her feathers, and he pressed the heel of his other hand hard into his eyes and held it there. Benal had seen him do that at the tangle. This was worse. When he took the hand away he moved slowly, like a man carrying something on his head. There were biters all over the blood on his arm, and on his neck, and on his knees. He slapped at them and they came straight back.

None landed on Benal's sleeves. He looked down at them and saw nothing there at all.

"Well," said Benal. He wiped his mouth. "On the bright side, they'll find most of me in one place."

"Only if they left any," said Teva.

Then she shut her eyes and stood very still, and after a moment she turned a little to the right and opened them again. "That way," she said.

"How far are we from the tangle?" said Keo.

Benal looked at the little square of light on his forearm. It knew which way north was; there was a mark for it, steady, that swung as he turned. And it had counted every step he had run, so it could tell him exactly how far that was. What it had not kept was which way the steps had gone. They had run one way and then another, round thickets and over the rise, and none of them looking at an arm, and every one of those turns was gone. He could point in any direction from here. He could not say where here was.

And it mattered. The First Walk had crossed the river in one place, where it ran between the stones, and everybody knew that much of the story. A good heading from the wrong place would bring them to the river somewhere else, on a stretch of bank the story never mentioned. Benal turned the sum over and did not like any of its answers.

Nobody answered Keo.
