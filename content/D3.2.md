Benal could hear every one of them.

The suit had taken the insects away, and in the space where they had been, each click stood by itself, small and dry and exact. There was one ahead and a little to the left. Another answered it from the right, further off. A third came from somewhere behind the first, and then the first one again, closer. The faint air coming down the slope brought the rest: wet feathers, and something rank under them, like a mouth that had eaten and not been cleaned. Keo's hand was still tight on his arm. Keo had told them not to move, and Benal, who had never wanted to do anything less in his life than move, was not moving.

Then Keo's hand opened.

Benal felt it go before he understood it. Keo's head had turned, sharp, toward the place in the fog where Noli had gone, the way a man's head turns when somebody calls his name. Whatever had come down the bond from her, it was bad. Keo took one step, then he was walking. Then the fog had him: a shape, a smear, gone, out into the open with his spear and his tunic and nothing else.

Benal opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

Beside him Teva had not moved at all. She stood with the spike held low and pointed away from her, the way she had carried it since the tangle, and she was listening too. She could not hear what he could hear. He knew that, and for a moment it made him feel very alone.

Out in the fog, something small broke from cover. He heard it go, a scrabble of claws in leaf litter and then a frantic crashing away through the ferns to the right, and two of the clicks, then a third, turned and went after it at speed. *Keo*, he thought. Keo had done that, from out there, with his head. Benal did not know how. He only knew that three were going one way, after whatever it was, and the rest were not.

The rest were moving, and he could hear exactly where.

"They've gone round," he whispered. It came out whole, without catching once, because it was not fear now, it was a sum. "The ones that are left. They've gone round between us and him. Listen. That one, and that one, and the one that keeps going quiet. They're closing on him, not on us. He's in the middle of them."

Teva turned her head toward him. He could see her face over the suit and nothing else of her.

Then, ahead, it went wrong all at once.

Noli shrieked. It was a sound he had never heard an animal make, high and ragged and full of something like outrage, and under it there was a heavy rush in the ferns and a thud of a body going down. Keo was shouting, no words in it, and then a word, her name, and then something that was only breath. A second set of claws went at him. Benal heard the spear shaft knock against something that was not wood. Keo grunted, and something ripped.

Teva ran.

She ran straight at it, into the fog, with the spike out in front of her, and Benal found that he was running after her with the club in both hands, because the only thing worse than going toward that noise was standing in the fern by himself and listening to it.

He saw it in pieces. A tail held out stiff behind, longer than his arm. Its feathers were the color of mud. A long low body on top of Noli, its back feet clamped into her, its jaws working at her neck while she thrashed and screamed under it. Keo was on his knees beside them with blood on his arm, both hands out, his face clenched shut. Benal had seen that look on him at the tangle, when he held Noli, but it was doing nothing now. The animal on top of her did not so much as turn its head.

Teva did not try to stab it. She did not have the time or the skill and she seemed to know it. She drove the tip of the spike in beside its head, close enough to touch, and held it there.

The razortail let go as if it had been burned. It came off Noli in one convulsive leap, shrieking, twisting in the air, snapping at nothing, and hit the ground already running away. It ran into another of its kind and that one broke too, and then the clicks were going everywhere at once, scattering away into the fog on every side, fast, and getting further.

Noli came up off the ground in a panic of her own, straight away from the reek, and Keo threw himself on top of her and held on.

"Run," said Teva.

None of them knew whether it was over. They ran. Keo had a fist in Noli's feathers and his forearm was running red. Benal had the club, which was heavy and useless and which he did not think to drop. Teva came last, walking backward half the time, the spike held out behind her toward the fog. The ground went up under them into a low ridge of limestone, and they went over it on hands and knees. Benal felt the rock drag at the knees of the suit and saw the living film go gray where it scraped. Keo had nothing on his knees but cloth, and he left skin on the stone.

They went on past the ridge and down the other side, and on, until the fog was thinner and the light was coming.

Benal stopped first. The clicking had gone thin, and then far, and then there was nothing. He stood with his chest heaving and listened to the nothing until he was sure of it. Then he listened a while longer. It took a long time before he was really sure.

Then his knees went, and his stomach turned over, and he was on all fours in the ferns bringing up everything he had and then nothing, over and over, his whole body heaving at a stomach with nothing left in it. He could not stop. He could feel them both watching, and that was worse than the retching.

When it let him go he stayed where he was, on his knees, and looked up at Keo.

"You —" His voice caught. He made it go on. "You r-reckless, unsuited idiot." And then it did not catch at all. "You told us not to move, and then you walked out there with nothing on, on your own, into the middle of them, and if she hadn't come you'd be lying out there now. You'd both be lying out there."

He had never said anything like it to Keo in his life. He did not know what would happen.

"He's right," said Teva. She had not raised her voice. "He worked out where they'd gone. That's why I knew where to run."

Keo did not argue. He did not agree either. He knelt by Noli with one hand still in her feathers, and he pressed the heel of his other hand hard into his eyes and held it there. Benal had seen him do that at the tangle. This was worse. When he took the hand away he moved slowly, like a man carrying something on his head. There were small dark insects all over the blood on his arm, and on his neck, and on his knees. He slapped at them and they came straight back.

There were none on Benal. He looked down at his own sleeves and saw nothing landing there at all.

"Well," said Benal. He wiped his mouth. "On the bright side, they'll find most of me in one place."

"They'd find you by the noise," said Teva.

Then she shut her eyes and stood very still, and after a moment she turned a little to the right and opened them again. "North," she said. "That way."

"How far did we come?" said Keo.

Benal looked at the little square of light on his forearm, which had kept counting the whole time and could tell him exactly how far he had run. It could not tell him which way any of it had gone. Nobody answered Keo.
