Benal lay on his stomach in the ferns at the top of the bank and could not breathe. He stared at the place where Keo had been, at the water sliding over it.

Then, on the next stone down, a head came up.

Keo hung on the rock with both arms, the skins tangled under him, and coughed the river out of himself. His hands were empty. Then he let go and came through the last shallows, half wading, half falling, and up the mud on his knees.

Teva caught his arm from higher on the bank, braced against a root. Benal took the other, and they dragged him up into the ferns, well back from the water.

Keo rolled onto his side and was sick.

He lay afterward with his hands over his eyes, his face the gray of old ash. Benal knew that what an animal felt could come back through a push while it lasted, and he thought he could see in Keo's face what this one had cost.

"Are you hurt?" Teva said.

"My head."

True, Benal thought. Not all of it.

Benal took Teva's skin, the only one with water in it, and held it out. Keo had pressed water on all of them since the tangle; now he wouldn't take it. Benal kept holding it there until Keo took it to make him stop, and drank, and drank again.

"What was it like?" Benal said.

"Shut up, Ben."

Benal shut up. After a while Keo said, from under his hands, "I let go of the cord."

Benal thought he knew why.

"Ben. You didn't kick."

"No."

"Good."

Benal had to look away at the trees for a while.

His chest hurt where the snake had been, and his arms from holding on. Everything else in him had gone strange and light. *Benal crossed the river*, people would say, if anyone ever said anything about it. What he had done was hold a cord and keep still, while Teva hauled him from stone to stone like a sack and Keo sent something out into the water to die.

Every time the water closed over his face he had been sure this was drowning. Every part of him had wanted to thrash. He hadn't. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, and from outside it looked like nothing.

"I'm a sack," he said. "A very well-traveled sack."

"A sack doesn't hold on that well," Teva said, coiling the cord.

"I had a choice between holding on and drowning." His face went hot. "I'm not a complete idiot."

"Possibly," Teva said.

Noli came in then, to Keo, whom the river had washed clean. She shoved her head against his chest and left it there. Keo put a hand on her without opening his eyes.

An hour went by before any of them could stand. A short way into the trees they found a thicket of young trunks so close that Benal could not have turned sideways between them, open on one side. They wrung out the lashings and laid them over a log in the last of the sun, and fenced the open side with stakes and thorn.

Only when the fence was up did it arrive. Benal was sitting against a stake when he started laughing. He didn't know at what. It came up out of his stomach and wouldn't stop, and it hurt his chest, which made it worse. Teva stared at him like he'd lost his mind. Then her mouth went, and she put her face in her hands and laughed too, without a sound. Keo lay on his back with his hands over his eyes and laughed until he coughed.

When it ran out, Teva went to dress the stakes. She opened the pot, looked in, and turned it so they could see. Scraped clean.

"It's empty," she said.

"We used it on the crossing," Keo said.

"That was possibly what it was for," Benal said.

Keo lit a small fire of driftwood from the old flood line.

Noli folded her legs under her close outside the fence, her back against the thorns, nearer than she had ever slept to any of them.

Benal looked back through the trees. He could hear the river, low, over its stones. It was between them and home now. Tonight all that stood between them and the dark was wood.

He wondered whether the others had thought it yet. He did not say so.
