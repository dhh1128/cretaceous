The cord lay slack in Teva's hands, running down the bank into the water, and the second chute was empty.

Benal could not breathe. He lay on his stomach in the ferns at the top of the bank and looked at the place where Keo had been, at the brown water going over it, smooth and empty.

Then, on the next stone down, a head came up.

Keo came up coughing, with both arms around the rock and the skins still tangled under him. He had nothing in his hands. He hung there and coughed the river out of himself. Then he let go and came down through the last shallows, half wading and half falling, and up the mud on his knees.

Teva was already going down the bank to him, and Benal went after her without deciding to. Between them they got Keo by the arms and dragged him up the steep bank, and nobody stopped until they were all three at the top, well back from the water, in the ferns.

Keo rolled onto his side and was sick.

He lay there afterward with his hands over his eyes. His face had gone the gray of old ash. Benal knew what came back to Keo through a push, while the push lasted: whatever the animal felt. He had pushed that cow out into the pool until the river took her, and for as long as he was pushing he had been inside her fear. Benal did not know how long that had been. He could see what it had cost.

"Are you hurt?" Teva said.

"My head."

That was true, Benal thought, and it was not all of it.

He unstoppered his water skin and held it out. Keo did not take it. Benal went on holding it out, the way Keo had held a skin out to each of them every morning since the tangle, until Keo took it to make him stop, and drank, and then drank again.

"What was it like?" Benal said.

"Shut up, Ben."

Benal shut up. After a while, with his hands back over his eyes, Keo said, "I let go of the cord."

Why was the part he left out. Benal thought he knew it anyway.

He sat back on his heels in the ferns. His chest hurt where the snake had been and his arms hurt from holding on. Everything else in him had gone strange and light. He had crossed the river. That was what everyone would say about it, if anyone ever said anything about it. *Benal crossed the river.* What he had actually done was hold a cord and keep still. Teva had pulled him from stone to stone like a sack. Keo had sent something out into the water to die so that the river would be busy.

He had not kicked. That was all. Every time the water closed over his face he had been sure, completely sure, that this was drowning. Every part of him had wanted to thrash. He had not. It was the hardest thing he had ever done and it had looked from the outside like nothing.

"I'm luggage," he said. "Very well-traveled luggage."

"Luggage doesn't hold on that well," Teva said, coiling the cord.

He had no answer to that. He felt his face get hot.

Noli came in then. She had stopped short of them at the top of the bank while the gel was still on their skin. Now the river had taken most of it off, and she came all the way in, low, and pushed her head hard against Keo's chest and left it there. Keo put one hand on her without opening his eyes.

That was why she had called, Benal thought. Not to bring them over. To bring him.

They got up because it was going to be dark. They walked a short way into the trees and found a place, and before they did anything else they wrung out the bindings and the spear lashings and laid them over a log in the last of the sun. Then the fence went up, stakes and thorn, as it went up every night.

It was inside the fence that it arrived.

Benal was sitting with his back to a stake, doing nothing, when he started laughing. He did not know what at. It came up out of his stomach and would not stop, and it hurt his chest, which made it worse. Teva looked at him like he had lost his mind. Then her mouth went, and she put her face in her hands and laughed too, without a sound, her shoulders shaking. Keo lay on his back with his hands over his eyes and laughed until he coughed.

The words for it never came. The laughing came instead, until it ran out.

When it had, Teva went to dress the stakes. She opened the pot out of habit and looked into it, then turned it so they could see. It had been scraped clean.

"It's empty," she said.

"We used it on the crossing," Keo said.

"That was possibly what it was for," Benal said.

What it meant for the nights, they left alone. Noli turned around three times in the litter close outside the fence and lay down there, with her back against the thorns, closer than she had ever slept to any of them.

Benal looked back through the trees the way they had come. He could hear the river from here, low, talking over its stones. It was between them and home now. All that stood between them and the dark tonight was wood.

He was the first of them to think it. He did not say so.
