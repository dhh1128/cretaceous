Benal lay on his stomach in the ferns at the top of the bank and could not breathe. He stared at the place where Keo had been, at the brown water going over it, smooth and empty.

Then, on the next stone down, a head came up.

Keo came up coughing, with both arms around the rock and the skins still tangled under him. He had nothing in his hands. He hung there and coughed the river out of himself. Then he let go and came down through the last shallows, half wading and half falling, and up the mud on his knees.

Keo got himself up above the mud. Teva caught his arm from higher on the bank, braced against a root, and Benal took the other, and between them they dragged him the rest of the way up. Nobody stopped until they were all three at the top, well back from the water, in the ferns.

Keo rolled onto his side and was sick.

He lay there afterward with his hands over his eyes. His face had gone the gray of old ash. Keo had pushed the cow out past where it would turn back. Benal knew that what an animal felt could come back through a push, while the push lasted, and he thought he could see in Keo's face what this one had cost.

"Are you hurt?" Teva said.

"My head."

That was true, Benal thought, and it was not all of it.

Teva's skin was the only one with water in it. Benal took it from her and held it out. Keo did not take it. Keo had pressed water on all of them since the tangle; Benal held the skin out and kept holding it until Keo took it to make him stop, and drank, and then drank again.

"What was it like?" Benal said.

"Shut up, Ben."

Benal shut up. After a while, with his hands back over his eyes, Keo said, "I let go of the cord."

Why was the part he left out. Benal thought he knew it anyway.

"Ben," Keo said, without taking his hands off his eyes. "You didn't kick."

"No."

"Good."

That was all, and Benal found he had to look away at the trees for a while.

He sat back on his heels in the ferns. His chest hurt where the snake had been and his arms hurt from holding on. Everything else in him had gone strange and light. He had crossed the river. That was what everyone would say about it, if anyone ever said anything about it. *Benal crossed the river.* What he had actually done was hold a cord and keep still. Teva had pulled him from stone to stone like a sack. Keo had sent something out into the water to die so that the river would be busy.

He had not kicked. That was all. Every time the water closed over his face he had been sure, completely sure, that this was drowning. Every part of him had wanted to thrash. He had not. It was the hardest thing he had ever done and it had looked from the outside like nothing.

"I'm luggage," he said. "Very well-traveled luggage."

"Luggage doesn't hold on that well," Teva said, coiling the cord.

"I had a choice between holding on and drowning," Benal said, and felt his face get hot. "I'm not a complete idiot."

"Possibly," Teva said.

Noli came in then. She had stopped short of them at the top of the bank while the gel was still on their skin. The river had taken most of it off. Noli came in low and pushed her head hard against Keo's chest and left it there. Keo put one hand on her without opening his eyes.

Perhaps she had been calling for Keo all along, Benal thought, and not for any of them.

An hour went by before any of them could stand. They got up then because it was going to be dark, and there had to be time to build a fence. They walked a short way into the trees and found a thicket of young trunks standing so close that Benal could not have turned sideways between them, open on one side only. Before they did anything else they wrung out the bindings and the spike lashings and laid them over a log in the last of the sun. Then the fence went up across the open side, stakes and thorn, as it went up every night.

Only when the fence was up did it arrive. Benal was sitting with his back to a stake, doing nothing, when he started laughing. He did not know what at. It came up out of his stomach and would not stop, and it hurt his chest, which made it worse. Teva looked at him like he had lost his mind. Then her mouth went, and she put her face in her hands and laughed too, without a sound, her shoulders shaking. Keo lay on his back with his hands over his eyes and laughed until he coughed.

When the laughing ran out, Teva went to dress the stakes. She opened the pot out of habit and looked into it, then turned it so they could see. It had been scraped clean.

"It's empty," she said.

"We used it on the crossing," Keo said.

"That was possibly what it was for," Benal said.

Noli turned around three times in the litter close outside the fence and lay down there, with her back against the thorns, closer than she had ever slept to any of them.

Benal looked back through the trees the way they had come. He could hear the river from here, low, over its stones. It was between them and home now. All that stood between them and the dark tonight was wood.

He wondered whether either of the others had thought it yet. He did not say so.
