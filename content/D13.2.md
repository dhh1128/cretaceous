They had lain up through the heat under the palms. When the sun was off the top of the sky, Keo went to lift Teva again. He got her as far as his shoulders, and his ankle gave. The pain went up into his knee and left him cold.

He knelt, and she slid off his back onto the fronds. She tried to stand, and her knees folded. Then she turned on her side and brought up the water she had kept since noon.

"You can't, Keo," Benal said. "Not on that ankle."

"Give me a minute."

"A minute won't mend it." Benal looked along the slow water, where saplings stood up out of the scrub. "We'll have to drag her. Two poles, and something across them she can lie on."

Keo was limping for the nearest sapling with the tooth-knife before Benal had finished.

Benal cut the second pole with Teva's knife. Keo lashed crosspieces between the poles with cord from the fieldpack, and the coil ran out with the last crosspiece still loose in his hand.

He looked at the spikes. The binding on Teva's had gone soft in the rain. It came away in a few turns, and the croc tooth came with it into his palm. He finished the frame with the binding and put the tooth in the fieldpack. Benal took the bare shaft.

They laid her on a bed of fronds across the poles, and Benal covered her with more against the sun. Keo took a pole end in each hand and dragged her out of the shade into the afternoon.

The poles hissed through the scrub. Teva lay tilted with her head at his back, her eyes open on the way they had come.

The sun was on his neck, and under it was another heat that came from inside him. Sweat ran under the strips on his thigh and stung the cut, though it was closed. The rot between his toes burned at every step.

He counted steps to a palm ahead. Somewhere past forty he was thinking about the binding on his own spike, whether it would hold if it had to, and then he was not thinking about anything. When he looked up, the palm was behind him. He started again from one.

At a rain pool too shallow to hide anything, Benal laid the weapons down beside the frame and went to the edge. Keo stayed standing between the poles. If he set them down, he did not know that he could lift them again.

Benal came back with three frogs, a fish no longer than his hand, and a stonefruit off a bush, scaled and green and whole. He split it with Teva's knife and held a piece to her mouth. She ate it. A little while later she turned her head off the side of the frame and it came back up.

"Drink," Keo said.

Benal held the skin for her. She took one swallow, and kept it.

Benal skinned the frogs before anyone touched them. The two of them ate the rest of the fruit, and then the frogs and the fish, raw. Whatever the mash would do to them at nightfall, it would do whether they ate or not.

Later, far off to the left where the plain rose, Keo saw a shape standing upright that was not a tree. It was a T. rex, and it was looking at them.

"Ben," he said. "Left, on the rise. Keep walking."

Benal looked. His step went short, and then he kept on.

It watched them as long as Keo could hold it in the corner of his eye. It did not come. Then the palms closed across it.

"Liaso," Benal said.

The name took Keo back to the night they left: fog on his face in the ferns, under the watch platform. Up there Liaso stamped twice and walked away along the planks, humming the long song, and they walked the hurdle out a body's width while the song went through. His parents were asleep somewhere behind the fog.

Behind him Teva turned her head toward the rise. Benal walked on with his mouth pressed shut.

Liaso had shaken until the next day. Keo had been shaking since noon, and the rex had nothing to do with it.

Some time after that, between one step and the next, everything went quiet, even his own feet, and his skin prickled. The air smelled of lightning. His next step came down somewhere he had not put it. Then he was walking, and he did not know for how long.

The healing, he thought. It had been taking the thread from him all afternoon.

They smelled the hadrosaur long before they reached it: sweet, then thick, then lodged in the back of Keo's throat. It lay on its side across their line, swollen tight. The flies lifted off it in a sheet and settled again. Beetles worked along the split in its belly, black and shining, in hundreds.

He took the frame wide around it, upwind.

"Ben," Teva said. Her voice was hardly anything. "Behind it."

A lizard was coming round the carcass, low, its tongue flicking. It was half as long again as Benal was tall, and it walked with its belly clear of the ground.

Benal stopped. His forearm lit, and he jerked it in against his chest.

The lizard looked at him. Then its head swung past him, to the frame, where Teva lay still under the fronds.

"Keo," Teva said. "It's coming."

He dropped the poles. The frame hit the ground, and she gasped.

"Ben, the spike!"

Benal was already backing toward them. He held out Keo's spike, and Keo got it in both hands and limped along the frame to stand between the lizard and her feet. Benal came up beside him with the bare shaft at arm's length. It shook.

"Get back!" Keo shouted. It was not much of a shout. "Go on!"

The lizard puffed its throat and came another step. Keo jabbed, and the point went into the dirt in front of it. His ankle went, and he caught himself on the spike. Benal struck the bare wood on the scrub and yelled something with no words in it.

The lizard looked at the two of them, and at the carcass behind it. Then it turned and went back to the hadrosaur, put its head into the split belly and stayed there.

Keo got the poles up on the second try. From then on Benal walked beside the frame with the shaft across his body, watching behind them. When the breeze carried anything dead, Keo took them wide of it.

Each palm was more steps than Keo had in him. He lost the count and started again.

He thought of the bag hanging in the runnel, where he had said to put it. He thought of her at the spring, eating the first share a knifeful at a time while the two of them watched, so that neither of them would have to.

Then he counted again.

Late in the afternoon the breeze freshened and cooled. It brought the sandflies, a cloud of them at his eyes and in the sweat on his neck, and he could not lift a hand from the poles.

Under the flies, faint and then not faint, the wind smelled of salt.
