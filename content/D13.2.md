They had lain up through the heat under the palms, and when the sun was off the top of the sky Keo went to lift Teva again. He got her as far as his shoulders, and his ankle would not take the rest of her. It gave, and the pain went up into his knee and left him cold.

He knelt, and she slid off his back onto the fronds where she had lain through the heat. She tried to get her feet under her. Her knees would not hold, and she sat down again. Then she turned on her side and brought up the water she had kept since noon.

"You can't, Keo," Benal said. "Not on that ankle."

"Give me a minute."

"A minute won't mend it." Keo watched him look along the edge of the slow water, where saplings stood up out of the scrub. "We'll have to drag her. Two poles, and something across them she can lie on. You lift one end and the other end drags."

Keo had the tooth-knife out and was limping for the nearest sapling before Benal had finished.

The wood was green, and the knife went through it slowly. Benal took Teva's knife from her belt and cut the second. Keo laid the two poles a body's width apart, cut shorter lengths to go across them, and lashed each one at both ends with cord from the coil in the fieldpack. Benal laid fronds over the crosspieces until there was a bed.

The coil ran out with the last crosspiece still loose in Keo's hand.

He looked at the spikes. The binding on Teva's had gone soft in the rain. It came away from the head in a few turns, and the croc tooth came with it into his palm. He finished the last crosspiece with the binding and put the tooth in the fieldpack. Benal took the bare shaft.

They lifted her onto it, Keo at her shoulders and Benal at her knees. Benal cut more fronds and laid them over her against the sun.

Keo stepped in between the pole ends, bent, and took one in each hand. When he straightened, her end of the frame came up off the ground and the far ends stayed in the mud.

Then he dragged the frame she lay on out of the shade and into the afternoon.

The poles hissed through the scrub behind him. Teva lay tilted with her head at his back, looking back the way they had come with her eyes open.

The sun was on his neck, and under it there was another heat that came from inside him. Sweat ran under the strips on his thigh and into the cut, which was closed and stung anyway. The rot between his toes burned at every step.

He counted steps to a palm ahead, because counting was something to do with his head. Somewhere past forty he was thinking about the binding on his own spike, whether it would hold if it had to, and then he was not thinking about anything. When he looked up, the palm was behind him. He did not know how far. He started again from one.

Where the ground dipped to a rain pool too shallow for anything to lie in, Benal laid the weapons down beside the frame and went to the edge. Keo stayed standing between the poles. If he set them down he did not know that he could lift them again.

Benal crouched at the pool for a long time. He came back with three frogs and a fish no longer than his hand. On the way he pulled a stonefruit off a bush, scaled and green and whole. Nothing had found it.

He split it with Teva's knife and held a piece to her mouth. She ate it. A little while later she turned her head off the side of the frame and it came back up.

"Drink," Keo said.

Benal held the skin for her. She took one swallow, and kept it.

Keo skinned the frogs before anyone touched them. The two of them ate the rest of the fruit, and then the frogs and the fish, raw. Whatever the mash was going to do to them at nightfall, it would do whether they ate or not.

Later, far off to the left past the palms, where the plain rose a little, Keo saw a shape standing upright that was not a tree. It was a T. rex, and it was looking at them.

"Ben," he said. "Left, on the rise. Keep walking."

Benal looked. His step went short, and then he kept on.

It watched them for as long as Keo could hold it in the corner of his eye. It did not come. Then the palms closed across it.

"Liaso," Benal said.

The name took Keo back to the night they left: the ferns with the fog on his face, under the watch platform over the rebuild. Up there Liaso stamped twice and turned and walked away along the planks, humming the long song, and they walked the hurdle out a body's width while the song went through. His parents were asleep somewhere behind the fog.

Behind him Teva turned her head toward the rise. Benal walked on with his mouth pressed shut.

Liaso had shaken until the next day. Keo had been shaking since noon, and the rex had nothing to do with it.

Some time after that the light flickered between one step and the next, and there was the smell of the air after lightning, sharp in his nose. His next step came down somewhere he had not put it. Then he was walking, and he did not know how long he had been walking without knowing it.

The healing, he thought. It had been taking the thread from him all afternoon.

They smelled the hadrosaur long before they reached it. The breeze brought it to them sweet, then thick, and then it was in the back of Keo's throat and would not go down or come up. It lay on its side across their line, swollen tight. When they came near, the flies lifted off it in a sheet, buzzing, and settled again. Beetles were working along the split in its belly, black and shining, in hundreds.

He took the frame wide around it, upwind, and kept going.

"Ben," Teva said. Her voice was hardly anything. "Behind it."

A lizard was coming round the hindquarters of the carcass, low, its tongue going in and out. It was half as long again as Benal was tall, and it walked with its belly clear of the ground.

Benal stopped. A moment later his forearm lit, and he jerked it in against his chest.

The lizard stopped and looked at him. Then its head swung past him, to the frame, where Teva lay under the fronds and did not move.

"Keo," Teva said. "It's coming."

He dropped the poles. The head of the frame hit the ground, and she gasped.

"Ben, the spike!"

Benal was already backing toward them. He held out Keo's spike, and Keo got it in both hands and limped along the frame to stand between the lizard and her feet. Benal came up beside him with the bare shaft held out at arm's length. It shook.

"Get back!" Keo shouted. It was not much of a shout. "Go on!"

The lizard puffed its throat at them and came another step. Keo jabbed, and the point went into the dirt in front of it. His ankle went, and he caught himself on the spike. Benal struck the bare wood on the scrub and yelled something with no words in it.

The lizard looked at the two of them, and at the carcass behind it. Then it backed off, turned, and went back to the hadrosaur. It put its head into the split in the belly and stayed there.

Keo got the poles up again on the second try.

From then on Benal walked beside the frame, with the shaft held across his body and his eyes on everything behind them. When the breeze carried anything dead, Keo took them wide of it.

He counted to a palm, and then to the next one. Each palm was more steps than he had in him. When he lost the count he started again, and the frame came after him through the scrub.

He thought of the bag hanging in the runnel, where he had said to put it. He thought of her at the spring, eating the first share a knifeful at a time while the two of them watched, so that neither of them would have to.

Then he counted again.

Late in the afternoon the breeze freshened. It was cooler. It brought the sandflies with it, a cloud of them at his eyes and in the sweat on his neck, and he could not lift a hand from the poles to them.

Under the flies, faint and then not faint, the wind smelled of salt.
