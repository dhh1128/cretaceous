Keo lay on his mat with his clothes on and listened to his parents breathe.

They were on the other side of the fern screen, an arm's length past his feet, and he could have picked either of them out of a crowd. His father breathed slow and deep, with a small catch at the top of each breath, as though he were lifting something and setting it down. His mother's breath was lighter and quicker, and she turned more. Keo had gone to sleep to those two sounds nearly every night of his life. He had never before lain awake waiting for them to settle.

The rest of the night went on around them. Storm water was still coming off the leaves a drop at a time onto the thatch, and somewhere down among the nest trees a yazhi woke and complained and was hushed. Whenever his father turned over, the floor gave a little and the ropes took the weight with a creak Keo had stopped hearing years ago. He heard it now.

When the two rhythms had been steady for a long time, he reached out the other way.

It was nothing like hearing. It was more like laying a hand flat in the dark and moving it slowly through the room to find what was alive in it, and it came with a faint tingling pressure. His parents were where they should be, a little apart. They were two warm banked coals, steady and low. Further down, past the floor and the long drop of the tree and in under its roots, something small and cold was working its way through the earth. The sense would tell him that much and no more: something was there, and what kind of thing it was. Whether his mother was dreaming, and of what, it could not tell him.

Beyond anything the sense could reach, at the far end of the bond, Noli was asleep or near it. What came back from her was warm and heavy and wanted nothing. Somewhere at the inner edge of the buffer, she was bedded down. She would not thank him for waking her. He let her sleep.

He got up.

He carried his boots across to the gear pile instead of wearing them, because a bare foot on the woven floor made almost no sound, and sat down beside it to lace them by feel. The hide was stiff from the wet. He drew each lace through slowly so that it would not squeak. His father's breath caught and went on while he tied the second.

The pile was the family's, and he knew it by touch the way he knew his own teeth. His fieldpack was at the bottom, where it had been since his last trip outside with them. On top of it lay the coil of fiber-cord, then the spare water skin, then the kindler, a gourd wrapped in leaf with the resin paste in it that would catch when tinder would not. He packed them one at a time, the cord flat at the bottom so it would not shift. Nothing rattled.

His hand found a knife next, in its sheath. He knew before he had it out that it was the wrong one. The binding on the haft was tight and new. His father had wrapped it this morning, a turn at a time, while Keo talked at him. It was the better knife. Keo put it back exactly where it had been. His own was underneath, the binding older and gone soft from his hand, and he hung that one on his belt.

Then he took the pack to the larder.

The basket was where it always was, by the opening. He felt through it until he had three strips of journey-pan, the dense kind that kept for days and had to be broken rather than torn. The greasy pouch of roasted crawlers held two handfuls. He took the pouch too. His knuckles knocked the gourds lined up behind the basket, and he steadied them: three light, one still full.

It was their food. That was the easy part. The hard part would come in the morning, when his mother found his mat empty and his pack gone, and went down to the Repository with his father and counted the racks. Their standing in the Enclave rested on one thing, which was that the suits were safe with them. By tomorrow night people would be saying their names in another voice.

There would be water standing in the low groves after a storm like this afternoon's. Every yazhi in the Enclave was drilled on it now, that after heavy rain the groves were the river's, and Keo knew exactly why the drilling had started. Yara had been six. <!-- @WF-yara.age.at.death: 6 --> The whole family had gone out gathering snails after the rain, and the croc had been lying half in the mud of a flooded grove where nobody thought to look. It had come in through the gratings small, years before, and grown up inside. Their parents were close by. They saw all of it. Keo had been beside her, and he had pushed at the croc harder than he had ever pushed at anything, and it had not so much as turned its head.

Beyond the screen, his mother's breathing stopped being sleep.

Keo froze. His hand was still on the gourds. It was not a sound that had stopped him; it was the absence of the sound he had been listening to all night. Her breath came shallow and uneven now, and the mat rustled under her. She was coming up.

"Keo?"

Her voice was thick, half of it still somewhere else.

"S'nothing," his father said, without ever quite waking. "G'back to sleep, Ri."

Keo did not move.

The answer was already in his mouth. *It's me. Go back to sleep.* He had said it into the dark a hundred times when he was small, after a bad dream or a drink of water in the night, and she had always gone back to sleep on it. He kept his teeth shut on it. He held his breath until it hurt and then let it out a little at a time through his open mouth, which was quieter. Through his sense she was the same warm coal she had been all night, in exactly the same place, and it told him nothing at all about whether her eyes were open. Hers would be telling her what his told him: he was in the room, up and moving. It could not tell her he had his boots on.

So he listened instead. Her breathing hung shallow for a long time. Then it slowed, caught once, and slowed again, and at last went back down into the light, quick rhythm she slept in. His father's went on under it the whole while as if nothing had happened.

He did not trust her breathing for a long time after that. When he did move at last, he moved lower and slower than before. He took his hand from the gourds and stowed the food in the top of the pack.

He lifted the pack onto his shoulders. It was heavier than it should have been.

At the opening in the floor he sat on the edge and found the top rung with his foot. Below him the ladder went down into the dark, and the night air came up through the hole. He swung his weight onto the ladder and started down, rung by rung, and he did not look back at the screen, or at the two of them behind it.

He knew he hadn't.
