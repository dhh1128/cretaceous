Keo lay on his mat with his clothes on and listened to his parents breathe.

They were on the other side of the fern screen, an arm's length past his feet. His father breathed slow and deep, with a small catch at the top of each breath, as though he were lifting something and setting it down. His mother's breath was lighter and quicker, and she turned more. Keo had gone to sleep to those two sounds nearly every night of his life. He had never lain awake waiting for them to settle.

Storm water still dripped off the leaves onto the thatch, and somewhere down among the nest trees a yazhi woke and whimpered and was hushed. Whenever his father turned over, the floor gave and the ropes creaked. Keo had stopped hearing that creak years ago. He heard it now.

When the two rhythms had been steady for a long time, he reached out the other way.

It was like laying a hand flat in the dark and moving it slowly through the room to find what was alive in it, with a faint tingling pressure. His parents were where they should be, a little apart, two warm banked coals. Far down, past the floor and the long drop of the tree and in under its roots, something small and cold was working through the earth. Whether his mother was dreaming, the sense could not tell him.

At the far end of the bond, Noli was bedded down at the inner edge of the buffer, warm and heavy and wanting nothing. She would not thank him for waking her. He let her sleep.

He got up.

He carried his boots to the gear pile, because a bare foot on the woven floor made almost no sound, and sat down to lace them by feel. The hide was stiff from the wet. He drew each lace through slowly so it would not squeak. His father's breath caught and went on while he tied the second.

He knew the pile by touch the way he knew his own teeth. His fieldpack was at the bottom. On top of it lay the coil of fiber-cord, the spare water skin, and the kindler, a gourd wrapped in leaf with the resin paste in it that would catch when tinder would not. He packed them one at a time, the cord flat at the bottom so it would not shift.

His hand found a knife next, in its sheath, and knew it was the wrong one before he had it out. The binding on the haft was tight and new. His father had wrapped it this morning, a turn at a time, while Keo talked at him. It was the better knife. Keo put it back exactly where it had been. His own was underneath, the binding gone soft from his hand, and he hung that one on his belt.

Then he took the pack to the larder.

From the basket by the opening he took three strips of journey-pan, the dense kind that had to be broken rather than torn, and the greasy pouch of roasted crawlers. His knuckles knocked the gourds lined up behind the basket, and he steadied them: three light, one still full.

Teva's shoulders letting go. Ben's hands, trembling the whole time he talked about the anchor. Two people were waiting on what was in this pack.

It was his parents' food. That was the easy part. The hard part would come in the morning, when his mother found his mat empty and went down to the Repository with his father and counted the racks. Their standing in the Enclave rested on one thing: the suits were safe with them. By tomorrow night people would be saying their names in another voice.

There would be water standing in the low groves after a storm like this afternoon's. Every yazhi in the Enclave was drilled on it now, that after heavy rain the groves were the river's, and Keo knew exactly why the drilling had started. Yara had been six. <!-- @WF-yara.age.at.death: 6 --> The whole family had gone out gathering snails after the rain, and the croc had been lying half in the mud of a flooded grove where nobody thought to look. It had come in through the gratings small, years before, and grown up inside. Their parents were close by. They saw all of it. Keo had been beside her, and he had pushed at the croc harder than he had ever pushed at anything, and it had not so much as turned its head.

Beyond the screen, his mother's breathing stopped being sleep.

Keo froze, his hand still on the gourds. Her breath came shallow and uneven now, and the mat rustled under her.

"Keo?"

Her voice was thick, half of it still somewhere else.

"S'nothing," his father said, without quite waking. "G'back to sleep, Ri."

The answer was already in Keo's mouth. *It's me. Go back to sleep.* He had said it into the dark more times than any of them could count when he was small, after a bad dream or a drink of water, and she had always gone back to sleep on it. He kept his teeth shut on it. He held his breath until it hurt and let it out a little at a time through his open mouth. Through his sense she was the same warm coal, in exactly the same place. Hers would be telling her what his told him: he was in the room, up and moving. It could not tell her he had his boots on.

Her breathing hung shallow for a long time. Then it slowed, caught once, slowed again, and at last went back down into the light, quick rhythm she slept in. His father's went on under it, unbroken.

When Keo moved at last, he moved lower and slower than before. He took his hand from the gourds and stowed the food in the top of the pack.

He lifted the pack onto his shoulders. It was heavier than it should have been.

At the opening in the floor he sat on the edge and found the top rung with his foot. The night air came up through the hole. He swung onto the ladder and started down, rung by rung, and he did not look back at the screen, or at the two of them behind it.

He knew he hadn't.
