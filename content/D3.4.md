They built the fence against a shoulder of rock that did one side of it for them, and Keo told them how.

He had watched his parents do it on every trip he had been allowed on, and he found that he knew it the way he knew the ladder at home. The spined cycad fronds went down first, points outward, overlapping like roof thatch.

"Green stakes next," he said. "Sharpen them. Points out, and lean them so anything coming in comes onto the point."

They cut the stakes with their tooth-knives and drove them in at a slant. Benal cut his slowly and too well. Teva cut hers fast and did not look up. Two sides were up while there was still good light, and Keo could already see what the camp would be when they had finished it.

Noli lay inside the half-built ring with her shoulder held stiffly off the ground. The two holes the razortail had left were small and dark and crusted. Keo knelt and laid a hand on her neck and kept her easy through the bond. Benal had the salves, so it was Benal who had to do it. He came across the ring with the pot held out in front of him like an offering to something that might take his hand with it. Noli watched him come. Benal knelt as far off as his arm would let him and dabbed the salve on with one finger. His finger shook the whole time. She let him.

When he sat back, she got up and followed him.

It was not the salve she wanted. It was his sleeve. As Benal lowered the pot, his arm came out of the rock's shadow into the last of the open light, and the film on his suit changed with it, quick and silent, the colors sliding across his forearm. Noli saw them move. She went flat and crept toward his arm with every feather down. When she pounced, Benal flinched. The sleeve turned, and its colors changed again under her claws.

She tried again, and he flinched again.

Benal had gone completely rigid. He had been afraid of her all day, Keo knew, probably all his life. Now she was crouched a hand's width from his elbow with her tail lashing, glaring at his sleeve as if it had insulted her.

Then Benal laughed. It came out of him like a cough, surprised. He moved his arm a little to one side, into shadow and out again. The colors ran, and Noli went after them.

Keo pushed *good-pack-good-food* at her, warm, the full belly and the others close. What came back up the bond was contentment, broad and simple, and it had nothing whatsoever to do with him. She did not even look round.

"She's mine, you know," Keo said.

"I know," said Benal. He moved his arm again. "I'm only the toy."

They closed the third side and plugged the gap with fronds before the light went. Then Teva went round the ring with the pot of gel, a dab on every point and on the cut fronds, and the reek of it came up off the fence and settled over the camp. Noli stood up and looked at the fence, at the three of them, and at the fence again. Every line of her said what she thought of people who would do that to a perfectly good camp. Then she went over the thorn at the low place and away into the dark. Keo watched her vanish past the rocks.

He kept the argument to himself. He had made it this morning, and lost.

They made the fire on a patch of ground scraped bare, small, with standing deadwood the long hot day had dried. Keo worked a pinch of resin paste from the kindler gourd into the tinder and had a flame on the first try. The frogs went on sticks. They were mostly bone and they were the best thing Keo had ever eaten. Keo put out his pan and crawlers. Teva added hers, and Benal put down the pan he had carried. Keo handed it round. Nobody held back, and he did not ask them to. Later he found one strip still in his pouch and began to count what was left of the rest, and stopped. It could wait, like everything else tonight.

They told the day back to each other, and with every telling it got a little less frightening and a little more like something they had done on purpose. Teva told the part with the spike in four words. Benal told how they had found the heading again in four hundred, and Keo made him start over at the ridge. Keo spread his hands to show how long the croc in the horsetail had been, and Benal moved his hands further apart, and Keo moved them further still. For the first time he could tell it without seeing the water.

After a while Teva lay back with her hands under her head and looked up. There was no moon yet, and through the gap in the crowns overhead the stars were thicker than Keo had ever seen them from the nests. She lay very still. He watched her for a while, and did not ask what she was looking at.

They set the watches: Teva first, then Benal, then Keo, into the dawn.

Benal lay down by the fire, and then sat up again.

"I'd like it on record," he said, "that the first thing in the world to want me was a sicklefoot. And it only wanted my arm."

Keo laughed. It came up from somewhere under his ribs and went on and on, and he lay on his back by the fire with the stars over him and his bitten arms and his torn knees and laughed until his eyes ran, and could not have said why, and did not care.
