They built the fence against a shoulder of rock that did one side of it for them, and Keo told them how.

He had watched his parents do it on every trip he had been allowed on, and he found that he knew it the way he knew the ladder at home. The spined cycad fronds went down first, points outward, overlapping like roof thatch. Then the stakes, cut green with the tooth-knives and sharpened and driven in at a slant so that anything coming at them came onto a point. Benal cut stakes the way he did everything, slowly and too well. Teva cut them fast and did not look up. It took the last of the daylight. When it was done there was a ring of thorn and points with a gap in it the width of a person, and Keo looked at it and felt as though he had built a house.

Noli lay in the middle of it with her shoulder held stiffly off the ground. The two holes the razortail had left were small and dark and crusted. Keo knelt and laid a hand on her neck and kept her easy through the bond. Benal had the salves, so it was Benal who had to do it. He came across the ring with the pot held out in front of him like an offering to something that might take his hand with it. Noli watched him come. Benal knelt as far off as his arm would let him and dabbed the salve on with one finger. His finger shook the whole time. She let him.

When he sat back, she got up and followed him.

It was not the salve she wanted. It was his sleeve. In the failing light the living film on Benal's suit had gone slow and dim, sliding toward the color of the dusk, and the pattern on his forearm crept as it changed. Noli had seen it move. She went flat. She crept up on his arm, every feather down, and froze, and pounced, and the pattern slid out from under her claws and went on creeping as if nothing had happened.

She pounced again, and it did it again.

Benal had gone completely rigid. He had been afraid of her all day, Keo knew, probably all his life. Now she was crouched a hand's width from his elbow with her tail lashing, glaring at his sleeve as if it had insulted her.

Then Benal laughed. It came out of him like a cough, surprised. He moved his arm a little to one side. The pattern ran, and Noli went after it.

Keo pushed *good-pack-good-food* at her, warm, the full belly and the others close. What came back up the bond was contentment, broad and simple, and it had nothing whatsoever to do with him. She did not even look round.

"She's mine, you know," Keo said.

"I know," said Benal. He moved his arm again. "I'm only the toy."

Teva gelled the stakes when the last light went. She went round the ring with the pot, a dab on every point, and the reek of it came up off the fence and settled over the camp. Noli stood up. She looked at the fence, at the three of them, and at the fence again. Every line of her said what she thought of people who would do that to a perfectly good camp. Then she went over the thorn at the low place and away into the dark. Keo felt her settle somewhere off past the rocks, sulking, near enough.

He did not say anything, because he had said it this morning and lost.

They made the fire on a patch of ground scraped bare, small, with dead wood that the long hot day had dried, and Keo got it going on the first try with the kindler. The frogs went on sticks. They were mostly bone and they were the best thing Keo had ever eaten. He broke out the journey-pan and the crawlers and handed them round. Nobody held back, and he did not ask them to. Somewhere in the middle of it he started to count what was left in the pouch. He got as far as the first handful and stopped. It could wait. Everything could wait tonight.

They talked about the day. They told it back to each other, the razortails and the spike and Benal's sum and the croc in the horsetail, and with every telling it got a little less frightening and a little more like something they had done on purpose. Teva told the part with the spike in four words. Benal told his part in four hundred. Keo told the part about the croc and made the line in the stems longer each time.

None of them said anything about home.

After a while Teva lay back with her hands under her head and looked up. There was no moon yet, and through the gap in the crowns overhead the stars were thicker than Keo had ever seen them from the nests. She lay very still. He watched her for a while, and did not ask what she was looking at.

They set the watches: Teva first, then Benal, then Keo, into the dawn.

Benal lay down by the fire, and then sat up again.

"I'd like it on record," he said, "that the first thing in the world to want me was a sicklefoot. And it only wanted my arm."

Keo laughed. It came up from somewhere under his ribs and went on and on, and he lay on his back by the fire with the stars over him and his bitten arms and his torn knees and laughed until his eyes ran, and could not have said why, and did not care.
