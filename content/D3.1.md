Keo's sense found them before his eyes did. There were two people at the drill place, both of them still, and when he came in under the palms the fog gave them back to him one at a time. Teva stood with her back to him and her arms folded. Benal sat against a trunk with his knees drawn up and his arms around them, as if he were trying to take up less of the world.

Noli came in at his heel and did not like any of it. They were too near the Barrier, and she knew exactly where she was. Every feather on her had lifted a little away from the skin.

The fog was pale rather than dark. Somewhere above it the moon had come up high, and the light came down through the wet air from everywhere at once, so that nothing had a shadow. Faces showed at arm's length and not much past it.

Teva turned around. "You have them."

It was not a question. Keo let the pack slide off his shoulders and laid the spears and the club down beside it, and she was on her knees at the straps before he had straightened up.

Benal had stood up too fast and was holding the trunk.

"Drink," Keo said. "Both of you. As much as you can hold. Then go into the ferns and empty out, now, before the suits."

Benal looked at him as if he had said something in another language. Then the meaning reached him, and it was such an ordinary thing to be told that it broke whatever had been holding him against the tree. Keo passed them the spare skin from his pack, and Benal drank, and then Teva. They went off into the ferns in different directions without a word. Keo stood guard over the pack and over Noli, and he found that he was smiling.

Teva came back first. Keo drew the small suit out of the straps and shook it loose.

"I've never touched one," she said.

"You step in. Feet first, then the arms. The tab's at the collar. Pull it across and it does the rest."

She stood on one foot and then the other, holding the suit like something that might bite, and it went on her more easily than she expected. Keo saw her surprise at that. She found the tab and drew it across, and the suit hissed shut along its whole length.

Then the film woke.

It went over her in a slow ripple from the collar down, gray and green and the white of the fog, and in a breath or two her face hung over a patch of moving fern and mist. If he looked away for a heartbeat, he lost the rest of her. He had seen suits his whole life, folded and hanging. He had never seen one put on somebody he knew and make her disappear, and something in his chest went light and loose at it. The old-tech was real. It worked.

"Ben," he said.

Benal came out of the ferns wiping his mouth with the back of his hand. Keo held out the large suit. Benal took it in both hands and turned it over, then over again, and could not find the opening. His hands were shaking too hard to hold the edges apart. He bent suddenly, turned his head away and retched into the ferns, and nothing came up but water.

"Sorry," he said. "I'm — sorry."

"I've got you."

He knelt and held the suit open at the bottom. He took Benal's ankle and guided the foot in, and then the other one. "Now the arms. That's it. Now the tab."

The suit closed over Benal with a hiss. The film went across him the way it had gone over Teva, and there was a tall smear of fog with Benal's face at the top of it. Then a small square of light woke on his forearm, soft and green, the way a dark panel woke when you touched it. Benal lifted his arm and held the red line in view for a long moment. Then he let his arm fall and said nothing about it.

Then he tilted his head.

"The thrum," he said. "It's gone."

"What is?"

"The insects. All of them. The noise." His voice had gone strange and quiet. "It's still out there, I know it's there, but it's not *on* me. I can hear the drip off the fronds. I can hear you breathe." He turned his head slowly, one way and then the other, as if he were trying it out. "I can think."

He had heard that wall of sound from his nest every night of his life, Keo realized. He had never once been out in it without it pressing on him. Now the suit had taken it off him, and he was standing there in the fog like a man who had put down a load he had been carrying so long he had forgotten it was there.

Keo felt a flush of something he knew was foolish and enjoyed anyway. The other two were inside the old-tech now, and he was not. He had his tunic and his leggings and nothing else, and he was the only one of the three the fog could touch anywhere below the chin.

"Weapons," he said. "And food. Say what you've got."

He slung his pack and took up a spear. "Spike. Knife. Journey-pan, and the crawlers."

Teva took the other spike. Keo could see her face over it and not much else. "Spike," she said. "Knife. The same."

Benal had picked up the razor-club and was holding it out from his body. "This. And the sling, and the stones for it. Salves, for stingburn and for cuts. Two chemical pods." He hesitated. "And journey-pan. Only journey-pan. I think I may have forgotten the protein."

"Possibly," said Teva.

Benal opened his mouth, and shut it.

"We'll manage," said Keo. "Come on."

The tangle went up in front of them out of the fog, close and black, trunks laid on trunks and woven through with thorn, and along its foot the nozzles were weeping. Keo could smell them from the palms. Noli stopped ten meters short of them, where she always stopped, and set her feet.

He did not argue with her. He pinned her.

The pressure began behind his eyes at once. Her terror came back up the bond, and he carried it, and walked her past the reeking nozzles one stiff step at a time.

The pot of gel stayed stoppered in his pack. Nobody needed it to walk past a wall.

Off to the left, the tangle stopped being a wall.

This was the rebuild, where a stretch of the old barrier had come down rotten and the crew had been building it back up for a week. In the daytime the crew worked in the gap. At night it was shut with the thorn hurdle, dragged across on its poles. Above it, on a platform of lashed trunks high enough to stand clear of the fog, stood the Watcher, a dark upright shape against the paler sky.

They went to ground in the ferns, twenty paces off.

Benal put his face close to Keo's ear. "Liaso," he whispered. "He stamps. Every so often. Like that." Above them the shape stamped twice and shifted his spear. "Then he walks to the far end and looks out. He stays turned while he counts thirty. He always counts."

Keo looked at him. He did not have to ask how Benal knew. Liaso told his cousin stories, and Benal listened. Keo said nothing about it. If Benal wanted it said, he could say it himself.

The shape on the platform stamped, and turned, and walked away from them along the planks.

"Now," Keo breathed.

The hurdle was too heavy to drag clear and too thorny to climb, but its near end sat against the new work, and Keo knew that end. He and Teva got their hands under the pole and walked it out from the tangle, slow, a body's width and no more. Teva went through the slot sideways and was gone. Benal followed her. His pack caught in the thorn and he froze where he was, and Keo could hear him not breathing. He reached in, worked the pack free with two fingers, and pushed Benal on through by the small of the back.

Then he looked back, once.

There was nothing to see. The nests were up there somewhere in the mist, his parents asleep in one of them, and the fog had all of it. He turned his back on it.

He went through the slot with his hand in Noli's feathers. She did not want to go. Her claws scored the pole as she went past it. The pin held, and he felt her come out the far side under his hand.

Teva and Benal were already at the pole. Nobody had to say it. Every child in the Enclave knew what a gap in the tangle was for, and whose nests stood behind it. They walked the hurdle back the way it had come, slow, until its end sat snug against the new work again and the slot was gone. Above them the platform was quiet.

Then they were through, and the world was louder.

He had heard the outside from the tangle every dawn for a year. The noise was the same noise, only nearer. It was all around him, and there was nothing between him and any of it. The ground under his boots was mud and roots, and the fog closed behind them as if the tangle had never been there.

He let Noli go.

What the pin left behind settled in behind his eyes, leaden. Noli did not wait to be told anything. She went away from the Barrier smell in three long bounds, and the fog took her. Through the bond he felt her fear ease into something smaller and more nervous, and further off.

Teva had stopped. She stood with her eyes closed, very still, and Keo knew what she was doing and waited for it. Then she opened her eyes and pointed into the fog, a little to the left of where Noli had gone.

"There," she said.

"Right." He started to go.

"Wait." She held out her hand. "The gel. Give it to me."

"We're through. We don't need it now."

"We need it on the spikes." Her voice was perfectly flat. "Anything that comes at us, it gets that in the face first."

"Not on mine," Keo said. "She won't come near me if my spike stinks of it. And I don't need it."

Teva looked at him for a moment. Then she stepped around him, unbuckled the top of his pack herself and took the pot out. She worked the stopper loose, dipped two fingers, and painted the tooth at the end of her spike with them, all the way round. The reek came up off it into the wet air. She stoppered the pot, put it back in his pack, buckled it, and wiped her fingers on the ferns.

"Now we go," she said.

Keo took point. His head was pounding and he did not care. Ten meters out, then fifty, into fog he could not see the end of, with mud to the ankles and roots that caught at his boots. He was outside the tangle at night, in no suit at all, and he was the one leading. He was pleased with himself, and he knew it.

Behind him Benal stopped dead.

"Listen."

Keo stopped. He heard the drip off the fronds and the insects, and his own blood thudding in his head.

Then he heard it too: a click, dry and hard, like bone on stone. It came from ahead of them. A moment later it came again from a little to one side.

He reached out with his sense. There were several of them, small, low to the ground and moving, spread across the fog ahead, between the three of them and the place where Noli had gone. He knew what they were before he had finished counting them. Razortails hunted in packs, and they hunted sicklefeet.

Down the bond, Noli's nerves turned to fear.

Keo put his hand out behind him, flat, without looking, and found Benal's suited arm and held it.

"Don't," he said. "Move."

The clicking came again, closer than it had been.
