There was a ringing in his ears, high and steady, and under it there was nothing at all.

He was sitting down. He did not remember that either. He was sitting where he had sat all through the watch, with the spike across his knees again, and the sun was a hand's width above the fern and warm already on the side of his face.

He reached for her without meaning to. He had done it every morning for years, before he was properly awake. He did it now, and there was nothing at the end of it. He did it again. It was like pressing on a bruise to see if it still hurt, except that there was nothing there to hurt. There was only the place where she had been.

Somebody was talking behind him, low. It was Benal, and then Teva, sharper. He heard the words go past and did not take any of them in.

After a while he got up and went out through the gap in the fence. Teva came and stood in the gap behind him, watching the sky.

The fern where she had slept was pressed flat in a hollow the shape of her, and on one side of it the fronds were broken and dragged. In the middle of the hollow there was one feather.

He picked it up. It was one of the long ones from her forearm, and it was purple-black in the low sun, almost blue at the edge, with a dark spatter dried across it near the quill. He turned it, and it went mud-brown. She had always gone that color when she walked out of the shade. He turned it back, and it was purple again, nearly black. He did not know what to do with it, so he held it.

When he came back in, Teva had already kicked the ashes of the fire apart and scattered them into the fern. The spikes were lying together by the packs, and her suit was rolled tight beside them. She had done all of it while he sat there. He had not seen her do any of it.

She shook her belt skin, listened, and opened it. There was a little left in it.

"Benal," she said. "Here." She held it out, and Benal drank one swallow and gave it back. She held it out to Keo.

He shook his head.

"Drink it," she said. She did not look at him. She was looking west, past him, at the edge of the sky. "There's a line out there. See it? Low down. That's cloud."

He looked. Along the bottom of the western sky there was a dark line, thin as a thread, under the clear blue.

"It could be rain by the middle of the morning," Teva said. "If it comes this way. Benal, could it come this way?"

"It could," Benal said. "There's nothing to stop it. Possibly it's already raining under it, and we can't see because it's so far off."

"Then we don't wait for it. We go north." She pointed, without looking where she pointed. "There's broken ground that way, rock, under the fern. There'll be shade in it, and somewhere the water runs off, if it rains."

"How far?" Benal said.

"Far enough that if the rain finds us, it finds us in the rock and not out here." She was still holding the skin out. "Keo. Drink."

He took it because she would not put it down, and drank. It was warm and tasted of the skin. There were two swallows in it, and he only understood when it was empty that the second had been hers. She took the skin back and hung it on her belt and went to look at the packs.

Benal had started on the fence.

He did not drag the fronds away into the fern. He took it apart. He worked a little way off from Keo, not beside him. He pulled each frond loose by itself and laid it down, and pulled the stake it had been tied to, and wiped the point on the fern, and laid the stake in a row with the others. The row got longer. He did not hurry.

"If the cloud is moving at the speed it was moving at when I first looked," Benal said, to the stake in his hands, "and it is moving this way, which I'm not sure of, then it reaches us about when the heat would have. Which would be a good trade. Possibly the best trade we've had." He laid the stake down. "Possibly it misses us altogether and goes north. In which case we'll be walking into it. Which is also a good trade." He pulled the next stake. "The rock's closer than the cloud, anyway. I think. Distances out here are very hard to trust."

Keo turned the feather in his fingers.

"Keo." Benal had stopped with another stake half out of the ground. "These are good stakes still. Are they worth carrying? You'd know better than me."

"Yes," Keo said.

"Then I'll carry them." Benal pulled the stake free and wiped it and laid it with the others.

He went on taking the fence apart, frond by frond, a stake at a time.

Keo looked up.

The sky was very clear, all the way to the top, and empty. Somewhere up there she had gone. Nothing had come back down. Nothing was going to.

There would be nothing to lay out. At home, when someone died, they were laid on fresh earth at the returning ground and given the balm and covered, and everyone drew back, and in the morning the covering lay flat and there was nothing to carry, and everyone knew where they had gone. Here there was a hollow in the fern and one feather, and the sky.

It had been like that with Yara.

The croc had taken her in the flooded grove with him beside her, and there had been nothing to lay out then either. His parents had never said so. There had been no balm and no covering lying flat in the morning, only the place she had been and the place she was not, and his hands with nothing in them.

His push had done nothing then. He had reached for the thing in the water with everything he had, and it had not known he was there. And this morning he had reached for the thing in the sky, and it had not known he was there either. He had not even reached in time.

It was not the same. He knew it was not the same. The rite was for people, and it had never been meant for her; nobody would have given the balm to a sicklefoot. But his hands were the same. They were the same empty hands.

He stood with the feather, and the thought went round once in his head and stopped.

"Keo."

Teva was standing in front of him. She had the fieldpack up off the ground and open, holding it by the straps, and she was holding it out to him the way you would hold out a tunic for a small child to put his arms into.

He looked at it.

He turned and put his arms through the straps. She lowered the weight onto his shoulders, drew the straps snug, and let go.

The feather was still in his hand. He opened the neck of his tunic and put it inside, against his skin, where it would not be bent.

Benal had the stakes bundled under one arm and his own pack on. Teva had her suit rolled under her arm and both spikes in her hand. She held his out to him, and he took it.

"North," Teva said.

They walked. The fence was gone behind them. The fern closed over the hollow where she had slept, and over the place where the fire had been, until the place could not be told from any other. Ahead of them, low and dark along the edge of the world, the broken ground began to come up out of the fern. The sun climbed, and Keo did not look up again.
