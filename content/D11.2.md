He sat with the spike across his knees again, and the sun was a hand's width above the fern. His ears rang.

He reached for her without meaning to, the way he had every morning for years, and there was nothing at the end of it. He did it again, like pressing on a bruise.

Behind him Benal was talking, low, and then Teva, sharper.

After a while he got up and went out through the gap in the fence.

The fern where Noli had slept was pressed flat in a hollow the shape of her, the fronds on one side broken and dragged. In the middle of the hollow lay one feather.

He picked it up. It was one of the long ones from her forearm, purple-black in the low sun, with a dark spatter dried across it near the quill. He turned it, and it went mud-brown, the color she always went when she walked out of the shade. He turned it back.

When he came back in, Teva had kicked the fire apart and scattered the ashes into the fern, laid the spikes by the packs and rolled her suit tight.

She shook her belt skin and listened.

"Ben. Here." Benal drank one swallow and gave it back. She held it out to Keo.

He shook his head.

"Drink it." She was looking past him, west, at the edge of the sky. "See that line, low down? Cloud. What do you think, Ben?"

Along the bottom of the western sky lay a thin dark thread. Benal said nothing.

"If it's rain, we don't wait for it here on soft ground." She pointed. "Yesterday on a rise I saw broken ground that way. Rock. We want a place the water runs off." She was still holding out the skin. "Keo. Drink."

He took it because she would not put it down, and drank. There were two swallows in it, and only when it was empty did he understand that the second had been hers. She hung the skin on her belt and went to the packs.

Benal had started taking down the fence.

"If it keeps coming," he said, as if she had only just asked, "I'd guess it gets here in a couple hours, about when it's getting hot. Wet for hot. Good trade." He laid a stake down and pulled the next. "Possibly it goes north instead, and we walk into it. Also a good trade."

Keo turned the feather in his fingers.

"Keo." Benal had stopped with a stake half out of the ground. "These are still good stakes. Worth carrying? You'd know better than me."

"Yes," Keo said.

"Then I'll carry them."

Keo looked up. The sky was clear all the way to the top. Somewhere up there she had gone.

There would be nothing to lay out. At home the dead were laid on fresh earth at the returning ground and given the balm and covered, and in the morning the covering lay flat and there was nothing to carry, and everyone knew where they had gone. Here there was a hollow in the fern, one feather, and the sky.

It had been like that with Yara.

The croc had taken her in the flooded grove with him beside her, and there had been nothing to lay out then either. His parents had never said so. There had been only the place she had been, and his hands with nothing in them.

He had pushed at the thing in the water with everything he had, and it had not known he was there. This morning the thing in the sky had not known either. He had not even reached in time.

It was not the same. Nobody would have given the balm to a sicklefoot. But his empty hands were the same.

"Keo."

Teva stood in front of him, holding the fieldpack open by the straps the way you would hold out a tunic for a small child.

He turned and put his arms through. She lowered the weight onto his shoulders, drew the straps snug, and let go.

The feather was still in his hand. He opened the neck of his tunic and put it inside, against his skin, where it would not bend.

Benal had the stakes bundled under one arm. Teva held out his spike, and he took it.

"That way," she said.

They walked. Behind them the fern closed over the hollow where she had slept.
