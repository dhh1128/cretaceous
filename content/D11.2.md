He was sitting with the spike across his knees again, and the sun was a hand's width above the fern. His ears rang.

He reached for her without meaning to, the way he had every morning for years, and there was nothing at the end of it. He did it again, like pressing on a bruise.

Behind him Benal was talking, low, and then Teva, sharper. He did not take any of it in.

After a while he got up and went out through the gap in the fence.

The fern where Noli had slept was pressed flat in a hollow the shape of her, and on one side of it the fronds were broken and dragged. In the middle of the hollow there was one feather.

He picked it up. It was one of the long ones from her forearm, purple-black in the low sun, with a dark spatter dried across it near the quill. He turned it, and it went mud-brown, the color she had always gone when she walked out of the shade. He turned it back.

When he came back in, Teva had kicked the fire apart and scattered the ashes into the fern, laid the spikes by the packs and rolled her suit tight.

She shook her belt skin, listened.

"Ben," she said. "Here." She held it out, and Benal drank one swallow and gave it back. She held it out to Keo.

He shook his head.

"Drink it," she said. She did not look at him. She was looking west, past him, at the edge of the sky. "See that line, low down? Cloud."

Along the bottom of the western sky there was a dark thin thread.

"Could bring rain if it comes this way," Teva said. "What do you think, Ben?"

Benal shrugged.

"Then we don't wait for it here on soft ground." She pointed. "Yesterday on a rise I saw broken ground that way. Rock. We want a place the water runs off."

"How far?" Benal said.

Teva shrugged. "Still a ways. Hour or two." She was still holding the skin out. "Keo. Drink."

He took it because she would not put it down, and drank. There were two swallows in it, and he only understood when it was empty that the second had been hers. She took the skin back and hung it on her belt and went to look at the packs.

Benal had started taking down the fence.

"If the cloud moves at a normal speed," he said, "and keeps coming this way, then I'd guess it gets here in a couple hours, when it will be getting hot. Wet for hot. Good trade." He laid a stake down. "Possibly it misses us altogether and goes north. In which case we'll be walking into it. Which is also a good trade." He pulled the next stake. "The rock might be closer than the cloud, anyway."

Keo turned the feather in his fingers.

"Keo." Benal had stopped with another stake half out of the ground. "These are good stakes still. Are they worth carrying? You'd know better than me."

"Yes," Keo said.

"Then I'll carry them."

Keo looked up. The sky was clear all the way to the top. Somewhere up there she had gone.

There would be nothing to lay out. At home, when someone died, they were laid on fresh earth at the returning ground and given the balm and covered, and everyone drew back, and in the morning the covering lay flat and there was nothing to carry, and everyone knew where they had gone. Here there was a hollow in the fern and one feather, and the sky.

It had been like that with Yara.

The croc had taken her in the flooded grove with him beside her, and there had been nothing to lay out then either. His parents had never said so. There had been only the place she had been, and his hands with nothing in them.

His push had done nothing then. He had reached for the thing in the water with everything he had, and it had not known he was there. And this morning he had reached for the thing in the sky, and it had not known he was there either. He had not even reached in time.

It was not the same. The rite was for people; nobody would have given the balm to a sicklefoot. But his empty hands were the same.

"Keo."

Teva was standing in front of him, holding the fieldpack open by the straps the way you would hold out a tunic for a small child.

He turned and put his arms through the straps. She lowered the weight onto his shoulders, drew the straps snug, and let go.

The feather was still in his hand. He opened the neck of his tunic and put it inside, against his skin, where it would not be bent.

Benal had the stakes bundled under one arm. Teva held his spike out to him, and he took it.

"That way," Teva said.

They walked. Behind them the fern closed over the hollow where she had slept, until it could not be told from any other.
