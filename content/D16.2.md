Keo's tunic had half dried on him crossing the crown in the sun. What was left of the damp clung at his back and under his arms, salt stiff in every seam. Now, he felt dry inside air take the rest of it. His skin tightened over his forearms. Then every cut the shore had given him woke as it dried, hundreds of them, small and sharp, all at once.

He had had no shell over him since the night they left the Enclave, and he had not been dry since the storm. He stood still and let it go out of him. His eyes stung. He put a hand on the nearest shelf and kept it there.

The lights were set flat into the ceiling, and they lit every corner the same. There was no flame in it, no smoke, no flicker, and no heat on his face. Nothing in the room had a shadow worth the name. Keo read a forest by its shadows, where they lay and what moved in them, and here there was nothing to read. Under it all ran the hum, louder than it had been outside.

The room was full. Shelves ran along the walls and benches down the middle, and there were words everywhere: along the edges of the shelves, on the walls, on the fronts of things. On the benches and the floor lay soft sheets, stained and gone to rot, stuck together in blocks with writing on them.

The other two started at once. Benal went straight on through, one room into the next, to a door at the far end with a lit panel beside it, and read it with his face close.

"It wants the same as the other one," he said.

Teva went along the shelves reading the words aloud as she came to them. "Media. Buffers. Cold store. Sterile." Some were the Vitarium's words; most Keo had never heard. Halfway along a shelf she stopped in the middle of one, and her hand went to the back of her neck.

"I know," she said, to the wall beside her.

There was nobody there. Benal was at the door with his back to her. She went on reading. "Sequencing. Reagents."

Keo went to the shelf his hand was on. A row of words ran along its edge, and he set himself to the first of them, slowly. The first letter faced the wrong way. He turned it round in his head, which made it a different letter, and the word came out as nothing. By then he had lost where it began. It was lessons again, the chalk on the board and everyone else already finished. He stopped.

When he looked up, Benal was watching him from the door. Benal looked a moment longer and turned back to the panel.

Keo looked at the shelves instead of their words. There was old-tech on every one of them, and on the benches, more than the Repository held: things of metal, faded and scuffed, some of them with a small light still lit in their faces. Every piece was whole.

"None of it's wrapped," he said.

Teva stopped reading. Benal turned from the door.

"No," Benal said. "If it had been, and nobody balmed it, these shelves would be empty."

"There's no fig smell," Keo said. "Not anywhere."

His parents went down to the Repository every week of his life to keep a few wrapped things asleep. He put out a hand toward the nearest piece on the shelf, and took it back without touching it.

Teva looked round at all of it. "The boat was the same. Under the stones. There wasn't a binding on it anywhere."

"We never even thought about it," Keo said.

"We were busy," Benal said. "Drowning, mostly."

"We came for the archive because they put it on metal, so it would last." Teva's voice was flat. "Everything else we thought would be gone. Wrapped and gone. It's their law." She looked at the shelves. "Why didn't they wrap their own things?"

Keo waited for Benal. Benal always had something.

"I don't know," Benal said.

Teva stood a while longer with her hand on the shelf. Keo heard her stomach rumble. Then she went to the doorway and looked out at the cut, at the light on the rock.

"The water's off the reef," she said. "I saw it from the top. It won't stay off, and we've had nothing since the eggs."

"The door—" Benal said.

"The door can wait an hour. The low water can't." She looked at Keo. "What's on a reef when the water's off it? Anything we can eat?"

"Clams. Fish, if the pools have caught any."

"Then you'll have to show me."

"I'll stay with it," Benal said.

"Come on, then," Keo said, and went.

They went down the track and round the foot of the crown to the reef flat, together, Keo slow on the ankle. The sea had drawn back off the flat inside the reef and left the sand ribbed in lines. Pools lay in the rock all along the foot of the crown. Out past them the water inside the ring lay still and green to the reef, where the surf stood up white. Keo let his sense go out over it, and it came back thick: fish, everywhere, a reef full of small minds that blurred into one another until he could not see into it at all. Anything big that lay out there would be lost in it.

They kept to the pools under the rocks. Every few breaths Keo looked up from the work at the water inside the ring, which showed him nothing.

Clams lay half buried at the pools' edges. He knocked each one with the back of his knife. Teva crouched beside him and watched.

"Shut ones?" she said.

"Shut ones. If it gapes and won't close when you knock it, it's dead, and you don't eat dead."

She started knocking them too. In a pool against the rock two fish were going round and round, caught where the water had gone out and left them.

"Can you come round that side?" Keo said.

She came round. They put their hands in together and closed them. He had his fish against the rock; hers squirmed half out of her fingers, and she held it to her chest with both hands until it stopped.

"You've done this," she said.

"Not in salt."

"What's different in salt?"

"The fish. I don't know any of them."

She nearly smiled.

In the shade of the rock something edged sideways with its claws up. It was the size of his hand, low and wide, its eyes up on stalks: a crayfish that walked sideways. He looked at it a long time.

"That one?" Teva said.

"You don't eat crayfish raw."

"There's no fire."

"Then it can stay where it is."

Out toward the reef, where the storm's drift had come in over the rock and caught, lay a green clump the size of his two hands spread. He went out to it, slow on the ankle. It was a head of fruit, nuts packed tight together like the scales of a cone, still green, and there were three more along the rocks beyond it. He had seen palms hung with heads like these on the plain, the day before the sea.

Teva turned one over. "The groves at home grow something like these. Not these. Something like." She cracked a nut against the rock with the back of her knife. Inside it was a skin of clear jelly, and she touched her tongue to it. "Sweet. Just."

He pulled off his tunic, folded the clams and the fish and the four heads into it and knotted it. For an hour his hands had known exactly what to do.

Back in the first room he shook the catch out of his tunic and pulled it back on.

Teva took a head of the fruit before anything else. She split the nuts one after another, scraped out the jelly with her knife, and opened the feeding well at her belly and worked it in with her finger until the well was full. Benal did the same with the next, though his arm made a slow job of it. Keo had no well to feed. He watched the clear stuff go into the gray. He had seen Teva do this once before, with the last of the honey, before she ate any herself.

"Will it bring them back?" he said.

"Probably not," Benal said. "But it might stop them going further."

They gave him what was left of the last head. He ate it, and it was sweet, just. Then they sat on the floor and ate the catch raw, all three of them. Keo opened the clams with his knife and passed them round. They were salt first and then sweet, and slick going down. He had never eaten anything uncooked that tasted like them.

Benal held his first one a long time before he tipped it into his mouth. "It moved," he said.

"That's how you know it's good."

"I'd rather it hadn't told me." He ate it anyway, and then the next.

Keo opened the bigger fish down its belly, cleaned it, and put it on the floor in front of Benal. Benal looked at it, and at him, and ate. It was the first time since the honey that there had been enough.

A long creak went through the walls. It started somewhere overhead and ran the length of the room and died away slow, a big tree's groan when it works in the wind. There was no wind. Dust sifted down out of a seam in the ceiling in a thin stream and lay in the light.

"What was that?" Teva said.

"The sun's on the roof," Benal said. "It's all shifting a little as it warms. I think."

"Not under that, then," Keo said.

They took the food and moved through to the room by the inner door, and sat down again with their backs to the wall.

"What's the first thing you'll do?" Keo said. "When we're home."

Benal licked his fingers. "Find something flat and write it all out again. The whole equation, since my board's at the bottom of the sea. And then make my father sit down and look at it."

"Make him?"

"I'll stand in the doorway until he does. Possibly for days."

"Teva?"

"Do Omya's hair. Whoever's had it while I've been gone won't have done the loops."

"You?" Benal said.

"Tell my father something," Keo said.

"What?" Teva said.

"Something."

Teva looked at him a while and let it be. "Then I hope he listens."

Afterward the other two went back to it, Benal to the panel and Teva to the shelves, reading under her breath. Keo sat on with his back to the wall and his hands empty in his lap. Benal's nose was nearly on the panel. Teva had found something on a lower shelf and gone down on her heels to read it. They knew what they were doing, both of them. He liked watching it.

"You'd hate it in here," he said, low, to the place by his knee where Noli would have been. "Nothing to chase."

Nothing answered.

Then his skin prickled, and the hairs lifted along his arms. The hum was gone, and Teva's voice with it. The room smelled of lightning. The light lay on the floor wrong, from somewhere else, at an hour that was not this one.

Then it did not. The hum came back, and Teva's voice. He looked down at his hands.

Teva straightened from the shelf and turned round.

"What else is in here?" she said.
