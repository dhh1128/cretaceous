Keo's tunic had half dried on him crossing the crown. The damp left in it clung at his back and under his arms, salt stiff in every seam. Now the dry air inside took the rest. His skin tightened over his forearms. Then every cut the shore had given him woke as it dried, hundreds of them, small and sharp, all at once.

He had had no shell over him since the night they left the Enclave, and he had not been dry since the storm. He stood still and let it go out of him. His eyes stung. He put a hand on the nearest shelf and kept it there.

The lights were set flat into the ceiling and lit every corner the same, with no flame and no flicker and no heat on his face. Nothing had a shadow worth the name. Keo read a forest by its shadows, where they lay and what moved in them, and here there was nothing to read. Under it all ran the hum.

Shelves ran along the walls and benches down the middle, and there were words everywhere: on the edges of the shelves, on the walls, on the fronts of things. On the benches and the floor lay soft sheets, stained and gone to rot, stuck together in blocks with writing on them.

The other two started at once. Benal went straight through to a door at the far end of the rooms, with a lit panel beside it, and read it with his face close.

"It wants the same as the other one," he said.

Teva went along the shelves reading aloud. "Media. Buffers. Cold store. Sterile." Some were the Vitarium's words; most Keo had never heard. Halfway along she stopped in the middle of one, and her hand went to the back of her neck.

"I know," she said, to the wall beside her.

There was nobody there. Benal was at the door with his back to her. She went on reading. "Sequencing. Reagents."

Keo tried the first word along the shelf under his hand. The first letter faced the wrong way. He turned it round in his head, which made it a different letter, and the word came out as nothing. By then he had lost where it began. It was lessons again, the chalk on the board and everyone else already finished. He stopped.

When he looked up, Benal was watching him from the door. Benal looked a moment longer and turned back to the panel.

Keo looked at the shelves instead of their words. There was old-tech on every one of them, more than the Repository held: metal things, faded and scuffed, some with a small light still lit in their faces. Every piece was whole.

"None of it's wrapped," he said.

"No," Benal said. "If it had been, and nobody balmed it, these shelves would be empty."

"There's no fig smell," Keo said. "Not anywhere."

He reached toward the nearest piece, and took his hand back without touching it.

"The boat was the same," Teva said. "Under the stones. Not a binding on it anywhere."

"We were busy," Benal said. "Drowning, mostly."

"We came for the archive because they put it on metal, so it would last." Teva's voice was flat. "Everything else we thought would be wrapped and gone. It's their law." She looked at the shelves. "So why didn't they wrap their own things?"

Keo waited for Benal. Benal always had something.

"I don't know," Benal said.

Teva's stomach growled. She went to the doorway and looked out at the light on the rock.

"The water's off the reef," she said. "I saw it from the top. It won't stay off, and we've had nothing since the eggs."

"The door—" Benal said.

"The door can wait an hour. The low water can't." She looked at Keo. "What's on a reef when the water's off it?"

"Clams. Fish, if the pools have caught any."

"Then show me."

"I'll stay with it," Benal said.

They went round the foot of the crown to the reef flat, Keo slow on the ankle. The sea had drawn back and left the sand ribbed, and pools in the rock all along the foot of the crown. Past them the water inside the reef lay still and green out to where the surf stood up white. Keo let his sense go out over it, and it came back thick with fish, small minds blurring into one another until he could not see into it at all. Anything big out there would be lost in it.

They kept to the pools under the rocks. Every few breaths Keo looked up from the work at the water, which showed him nothing.

Clams lay half buried at the pools' edges. He knocked each one with the back of his knife.

"Shut ones?" Teva said.

"Shut ones. If it gapes and won't close when you knock it, it's dead, and you don't eat dead."

She started knocking them too. Two fish circled in a pool against the rock, trapped where the water had gone out. He sent her round the far side, and they put their hands in together and closed them. He pinned his fish against the rock; hers squirmed half out of her fingers, and she clutched it to her chest until it stopped.

"You've done this," she said.

"Not in salt."

"What's different in salt?"

"The fish. I don't know any of them."

She nearly smiled.

In the shade of the rock something edged sideways with its claws up, low and wide, its eyes on stalks: a crayfish that walked sideways. He looked at it a long time.

"That one?" Teva said.

"You don't eat crayfish raw."

"There's no fire."

"Then it stays where it is."

Out toward the reef, where the storm's drift had caught on the rock, lay a green clump the size of his two hands spread: a head of fruit, nuts packed tight like the scales of a cone. There were three more along the rocks. He had seen palms hung with heads like these on the plain, the day before the sea.

Teva turned one over. "The groves at home grow something like these. Not these. Something like." She cracked a nut on the rock with the back of her knife and touched her tongue to the clear jelly inside. "Sweet. Just."

He pulled off his tunic, knotted the clams and the fish and the four heads into it, and carried it back. For an hour his hands had known exactly what to do.

Inside, Teva took a head of the fruit before anything else. She split the nuts, scraped out the jelly, and worked it into the feeding well at her belly with her finger until the well was full. Benal did the same, slowly, because of his arm. Keo had no well to feed. He watched the clear stuff go into the gray.

"Will it bring them back?" he said.

"Probably not," Benal said. "But it might stop them going further."

They gave him what was left of the last head. Then they sat on the floor and ate the catch raw. Keo opened the clams with his knife and passed them round. They were salt first and then sweet, and slick going down. He had never eaten anything uncooked that tasted like them.

Benal held his first one a long time before he tipped it into his mouth. "It moved," he said.

"That's how you know it's good."

"I'd rather it hadn't told me." He ate it anyway, and then the next.

Keo gutted the bigger fish and put it on the floor in front of Benal. Benal looked at it, and at him, and ate. It was the first time since the honey day that there had been enough.

A long creak went through the walls. It started overhead and ran the length of the room and died away slow, a big tree groaning in the wind. There was no wind. Dust sifted out of a seam in the ceiling in a thin stream and lay in the light.

"What was that?" Teva said.

"The sun's on the roof," Benal said. "It's all shifting a little as it warms. I think."

"Not under that, then," Keo said.

They took the food through to the room by the inner door and sat down again with their backs to the wall.

"What's the first thing you'll do when we're home?" Keo said.

Benal licked his fingers. "Find something flat and write the whole equation out again, since my board's at the bottom of the sea. And then make my father sit down and look at it."

"Make him?"

"I'll stand in the doorway until he does. Possibly for days."

"Teva?"

"Do Omya's hair. Whoever's had it while I've been gone won't have done the loops."

"You?" Benal said.

"Tell my father something," Keo said.

"What?" Teva said.

"Something."

Teva looked at him a while and let it be. "Then I hope he listens."

Afterward the other two went back to it, Benal to the panel with his nose nearly on it, Teva down on her heels at a lower shelf, reading under her breath. Keo sat on with his back to the wall and his hands empty in his lap. They knew what they were doing, both of them. He liked watching it.

"You'd hate it in here," he whispered, to the place by his knee where Noli would have been. "Nothing to chase."

Nothing answered.

Then his skin prickled, and the hairs lifted along his arms. The hum was gone, and Teva's voice with it. The room smelled of lightning. The light lay on the floor wrong, from somewhere else, at an hour that was not this one.

Then it did not. The hum came back, and Teva's voice. He looked down at his hands.

Teva straightened from the shelf and turned round.

"What else is in here?" she said.
