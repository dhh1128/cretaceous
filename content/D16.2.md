Keo's tunic had half dried on him crossing the crown in the sun. What was left of the damp clung at his back and under his arms, and the salt had gone stiff in every seam. Standing on the first dry floor he had touched since the sea, he felt the air in here take the rest of it. His skin tightened over his forearms. Then every cut the bank had given him woke as it dried, hundreds of them, small and sharp, all at once.

He had been wet with no shell over him since the night they left the Enclave, soaked by rain and river and swamp and then by the sea. He stood still and let it go out of him. His eyes stung, and he put a hand on the nearest shelf and kept it there.

The light came from panels set flat into the ceiling, white and even and the same in every corner. There was no flame in it, no smoke, no flicker, and no heat on his face. Nothing in the room had a shadow worth the name. Keo read a forest by its shadows, where they lay and what moved in them, and here there was nothing to read. Of everything in the room, that was what he could least get used to. Under it all ran the hum Benal had heard through the rubble, louder in here, one note that did not change.

The room was full. Shelves ran along the walls and benches down the middle, and there were words everywhere: along the edges of the shelves, on the walls, on the fronts of things. On the benches and the floor lay soft sheets, stained brown and stuck together in blocks, with writing on them.

The other two started at once. Benal went straight through to the far end, where another door stood with a lit panel beside it, and read it with his face close.

"It wants the same as the other one," he said.

Teva went along the shelves reading the words aloud as she came to them. "Media. Buffers. Cold store. Sterile." Some were the Vitarium's words; most Keo had never heard. Halfway along a shelf she stopped in the middle of one, and her hand went to the back of her neck.

"I know," she said, to the wall beside her.

There was nobody there. Benal was at the door with his back to her. She went on reading. "Sequencing. Reagents."

Keo went to the shelf his hand was on. A row of words ran along its edge, and he set himself to the first of them, slowly. The first letter faced the wrong way. He turned it round in his head, which made it a different letter, and the word came out as nothing. By then he had lost where it began. It was lessons again, the chalk on the board and everyone else already finished. He stopped.

When he looked up, Benal was watching him from the door. Benal looked a moment longer and turned back to the panel.

Keo looked at the shelves instead of their words. There was old-tech on every one of them, and on the benches, more than the Repository had ever held: things of metal, faded and scuffed, some of them with a small light still lit in their faces. He looked along all of it twice. Every piece was whole, and not one was wrapped.

"None of it's wrapped," he said.

Teva stopped reading. Benal turned from the door.

"No," Benal said. "If it had been, and nobody balmed it, there'd be nothing on those shelves but rust."

"There's no fig smell," Keo said. "Not anywhere."

His parents went down to the Repository every week of his life to keep a few wrapped things asleep. He put out a hand toward the nearest piece on the shelf, and took it back without touching it.

Teva looked round at all of it. "The boat was the same. Under the stones. There wasn't a binding on it anywhere."

"We never even thought about it," Keo said.

"We were busy," Benal said. "Drowning, mostly."

"We came for the archive because they put it on metal, so it would last." Teva's voice was flat. "Everything else we thought would be gone. Wrapped and gone. It's their law." She looked at the shelves. "Why didn't they wrap their own things?"

Keo waited for Benal. Benal always had something.

"I don't know," Benal said.

Teva stood a while longer with her hand on the shelf. Then she went to the doorway and looked out at the cut, at the light on the rock.

"The water's off the reef," she said. "I saw it from the top. It won't stay off, and we've had nothing since the eggs."

"The door—" Benal said.

"The door can wait an hour. The low water can't."

"I'll stay with it."

"Come on, then," Keo said, and went.

They went down the track and round the foot of the crown to the reef flat, together, Keo slow on the ankle. The sea had drawn back off the flat inside the reef and left wet sand ribbed in lines, and pools in the rock all along the foot of the crown. Out past them the water inside the ring lay flat and green to the reef, where the surf stood up white. Keo let his sense go out over it, and it came back thick: fish, everywhere, a reef full of small minds that blurred into one another until he could not see into it at all. Anything big that lay out there would be lost in it.

They kept to the pools under the rocks. Keo did the work, and every few breaths he looked up from it at the water inside the ring, which showed him nothing.

Clams lay half buried at the pools' edges. He knocked each one with the back of his knife. Teva crouched beside him and watched.

"Shut ones?" she said.

"Shut ones. If it gapes and won't close when you knock it, it's dead, and you don't eat dead."

She started knocking them too. In a pool against the rock two fish were going round and round, caught where the water had gone out and left them.

"Can you come round that side?" Keo said.

She came round. They put their hands in together and closed them. He had his fish against the rock; hers squirmed half out of her fingers, and she held it to her chest with both hands until it stopped.

"You've done this," she said.

"Not in salt."

In the shade of the rock something held still with its claws up. It was the size of his hand, flat and wide, its eyes up on stalks. It was a crayfish going sideways, and he looked at it a long time.

"That one?" Teva said.

"You don't eat crayfish raw."

"There's no fire."

"Then it can stay where it is."

He pulled off his tunic, folded the clams and the fish into it and knotted it. For an hour his hands had known exactly what to do.

Back in the dry room they sat on the floor and ate it raw, all three of them. Keo opened the clams with his knife and passed them round. They were salt first and then sweet, and slick going down. He had never eaten anything uncooked that tasted like them. The nearest was the river's crayfish, and that was always cooked.

Benal held his first one a long time before he tipped it into his mouth. "It moved," he said.

"That's how you know it's good."

"I'd rather it hadn't told me." He ate it anyway, and then the next.

Keo opened the bigger fish down its belly, cleaned it, and put it on the floor in front of Benal. Benal looked at it, and at him, and ate. It was the first time since the honey that there had been enough.

A long creak went through the walls. It started somewhere overhead and ran the length of the room and died away, slow, the sound a big tree makes working in the wind, and there was no wind. Dust sifted down out of a seam in the ceiling in a thin stream and lay in the light.

"What was that?" Teva said.

"The sun's on the roof," Benal said. "It's all shifting a little as it warms. I think."

"Not under that, then," Keo said.

They took the food and moved through to the room by the inner door, and sat down again with their backs to the wall.

"What's the first thing you'll do?" Keo said. "When we're home."

Benal licked his fingers. "Find something flat and write it all out again. The whole equation, since the board's at the bottom of the sea. And then make my father sit down and look at it."

"Make him?"

"I'll stand in the doorway until he does. Possibly for some days."

"Teva?"

"Do Omya's hair."

"You?" Benal said.

"Tell my father something," Keo said.

"What?" Teva said.

"Something."

Afterward the other two went back to it, Benal to the panel and Teva to the shelves, reading under her breath. Keo sat on with his back to the wall and his hands empty in his lap. Benal's nose was nearly on the panel. Teva had found something on a lower shelf and gone down on her heels to read it. They knew what they were doing, both of them, and he liked watching it.

"You'd hate it in here," he said, low, to the place by his knee where Noli would have been. "Nothing to chase."

Nothing answered.

Then his skin prickled, and the hairs lifted along his arms. The hum was gone, and Teva's voice with it, and the room smelled of lightning. The light lay on the floor wrong, from somewhere else, at an hour that was not this one.

Then it did not. The hum came back, and Teva's voice. He looked down at his hands.

Teva straightened from the shelf and turned round.

"What else is in here?" she said.
