The light started to come but never finished. Teva lay inside the fence and watched the sky go from black to gray, and then it stayed there, a flat gray hide pulled across the world from one edge of the crowns to the other. The showers had stopped in the night. Nothing dripped or moved.

There was no breakfast, and nobody said so. Keo pulled the stakes, and Benal helped him, slowly. Teva shouldered her pack, and when the straps settled she saw what she had seen yesterday and decided not to see: along both straps, where they rubbed, her suit had gone gray. Everywhere else it copied the morning; this was a dead patch that copied nothing. She shifted the straps an inch outward, onto living film, and said nothing about that either.

The snails were out again on the wet stems, and they picked them as they went, into Keo's empty food pouch, without stopping. It was the only thing they did all morning that felt like doing something.

Then it was walking.

She had thought yesterday was only walking. Today had the suit's heading on her arm and Keo's back in front of her, and that was all. The understory was over her head in long stretches, tree fern and cycad, and under it the dead leaves lay deep and still. In this light she could not tell them from the live ones. Everything was one gray-brown, near and far, and her eyes, which were only ordinary eyes, kept trying to find an edge to something and failing.

Keo was limping, not much. He put one foot down a little short and a little flat. Under her eyes he straightened his step for a few paces, and then forgot. She did not say anything. If he wanted it known he would have said it.

Before the middle of the morning Benal said, "Wait."

Keo turned with the spear as they stopped and stood with his head down, reaching. Teva waited while the insect wall went on, soft through her suit, the same as it had been since the tangle.

"Something," Benal said. "Off that way, a long way. I think. A sound, possibly. It's stopped now."

They waited a little, not as long as yesterday. Then Keo said, "Nothing," and they went on.

He stopped them again an hour later, for less: a sound he could not even point at. This time Keo did not turn around. He stood with his back to them while Benal listened, then walked on. Teva walked on after him, with Benal last. She did not look back at him. She had trusted his thinking in the fog. This was only his ears, and his ears had stopped them four times since yesterday, once for a bird. She was hungry besides.

The mushrooms grew out of a fallen trunk in a shelf. Most of them were small and brown, and Benal crouched and named them, one and another and that one. Then there was the big one, low on the trunk, pale as fat and as wide as her two hands.

Keo looked at it the way Noli looked at things.

"I don't know it," Benal said.

"It's the same shape."

"It's not the same color underneath. And it's bigger, which—" He stopped. "I don't know it. Possibly it's fine."

"Possibly," Keo said. "Look at the size of it. That's a meal."

"I know it's a meal."

"So how hungry do we have to be?"

Benal looked at the mushroom and then at Keo without answering, and his mouth worked. Teva waited for one of them to settle it. Neither did. Keo stood a while longer with his hand half out, and then he dropped it and picked the small ones, because Benal would not say yes. They left the big one on the trunk. She looked back at it once and did not know whether they had just been careful or stupid. She did not expect to find out.

At the next stop she saw the wasp.

It was on a frond at the height of her eyes, on a caterpillar that was green and fat. The wasp was riding it with its body curled under, the tip driven into the caterpillar's back, and it held there, very still. The caterpillar jerked and stayed on the frond.

"What is it doing?" she said.

Benal leaned in, and his face changed: he had stopped being afraid of something and started being interested in it. "Laying eggs," he said. "In it. Not on it, in it. When they hatch they'll feed inside it while it stays alive. It'll go on eating and walking about as if nothing were wrong, for days, possibly, until they're ready to come out, and then—"

He stopped. He looked at the caterpillar a moment longer, and then he looked away into the ferns.

The wasp lifted off and was gone. The caterpillar began eating again.

They lay up when the heat came down, in a hollow under a laurel, and ate the snails off a fire Keo made. There were not many. Teva counted hers without meaning to, and then could not stop knowing the number.

Benal held one up. "Now we'll have to do this every day," he said.

Nobody laughed.

He ate it.

Teva lay back on the leaves. Keo was asleep almost at once. Benal sat with his knees up and his eyes shut. She was cool. Under the laurel, in the heat that had come down on everything, she was cool, and she lay there feeling it like a hand on her back.

A week on snails had been a number yesterday. Today she was hungry. She had said *I know*, and she had known. She had not known what it would feel like on the second morning with no breakfast.

She thought of Omya. Omya would have gotten up this morning and not found her. Somebody would have come by, because somebody always did. They would sit with her and talk about the weather. Omya would answer, and some of the answers would be right. But she would be lonely. That was the thing Teva had not let herself think about when she left. Omya would not be helpless. She would be lonely, and some of the time she would know exactly why.

Teva shut her eyes, and it was all still there behind them.

They got up when the worst of the heat had gone off, in the middle of the afternoon, and Keo kicked the fire apart and scattered the ashes. Noli stayed at Keo's heel when they set out again, so close she nearly tripped him. She had run ahead every day before. Today she would not leave him. Teva watched her favor the shoulder and thought the shoulder was worse.

The light had gone down another step since the lay-up, as if the day meant to end early. They had been walking a little while when Benal said, "Wait."

She stopped. Keo did not.

"Something's stopped," Benal said behind her. "Something— I don't know what. Something's gone."

Teva listened. The insect wall came through her suit soft and even and everywhere, the same as it had all day. She could not hear anything gone. She could hear Keo's feet in the leaves ahead, going away.

Keo had his head down a little. He was reaching, she thought, or meant her to think he was. Then he kept walking anyway, without turning around.

She stood a moment longer with Benal behind her. Then she walked on too.
