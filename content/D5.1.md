The light started to come but never finished. Teva lay among the fallen trunks they had camped in, the stakes across the one open side, and watched the sky go from black to gray and stay there, a hide pulled across the world from one edge of the crowns to the other. Nothing dripped or moved.

There was no breakfast. "Later," Keo said, when Benal looked at the empty pouch.

"When they return me," Benal said, "tell them I was very brave about breakfast."

"You'll get breakfast."

"I'm only saying. In case." He helped Keo pull the stakes, slowly.

Teva shouldered her pack and saw what she had seen yesterday and decided not to see: where the straps rubbed, her suit had gone gray. Everywhere else it copied the morning; this copied nothing. She shifted the straps a finger's width outward, onto living film.

Then it was walking. They picked snails off the stems as they went. The understory closed over her head in long stretches, and in this light she could not tell the dead leaves from the live ones. Once there was something small and white at the foot of a cycad, and she had it in her fingers before she knew she had stooped. She had half turned to put it somewhere before she remembered there was nobody to give it to. She let it drop and walked on.

Before the middle of the morning Benal said, "Wait."

Keo turned with the spike and stood with his head down, reaching.

"Something," Benal said. "Off that way, a long way. I think. A sound, possibly. It's stopped now."

They waited a little, not as long as yesterday.

"Same nothing as yesterday?" Keo said. "Or a new one?"

"A new one," Benal said. "Possibly."

"Tell it we're busy." Keo waited, long enough to be fair, and walked on. Teva followed, Benal last. She did not look back at him. She had trusted his thinking in the fog. This was only his ears, and his ears had stopped them three times since yesterday, once for a bird. And she was hungry.

They lay up when the heat came down, in a hollow under a big conifer, and ate the snails. Teva counted out her share and made it last.

Benal held one up. "Now we'll have to do this every day," he said.

Nobody laughed.

He ate it.

Noli came into the hollow and leaned her whole weight against Keo's leg, as she had since she was small. He held a snail down to her. "Go on," he said, in the level voice he kept for her. "It's good."

She put her nose to it, took her head away, and looked at something else.

"She's off her food," Keo said.

"She's had your cooking before," Teva said.

"They're good snails," he said.

"They're snails."

Keo stared at her, working out whether that had been a joke. Then he grinned and ate the snail himself. He was asleep almost at once, Noli against his leg. Benal sat with his knees up and his eyes shut. Teva lay back on the leaves. In the heat that had come down on everything she was cool, and she lay feeling it like a hand on her back.

A week on snails had been a number yesterday. She had said *I know*, and she had known. She hadn't known how the second morning with no breakfast would feel.

She thought of Omya. Omya would have gotten up this morning and not found her. Somebody would have come by, because somebody always did, and sat with her and talked about the weather. Omya would answer, and some of the answers would be right. But she would be lonely. Teva had not let herself think that when she left. Omya would not be helpless. She would be lonely, and some of the time she would know exactly why.

Teva shut her eyes, and it was all still there behind them.

They got up in the middle of the afternoon. Noli stayed at Keo's heel, so close she nearly tripped him, when every day before she had run ahead. Teva watched her favor the shoulder and thought the shoulder was worse.

The light had gone down another step since the lay-up. They had been walking a little while when Benal said, "Wait."

She stopped. Keo did not.

"Something's stopped," Benal said behind her. "Something— I don't know what. Something's gone."

Teva listened. The insect wall came through her suit soft and even and everywhere, the same as it had all day. She could not hear anything gone. She could hear Keo's feet in the leaves ahead, going away.

Keo had his head down a little. He was reaching, she thought, or meant her to think he was. He kept walking.

She stood a moment longer with Benal behind her. Then she walked on too.
