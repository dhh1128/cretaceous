The light started to come but never finished. Teva lay among the fallen trunks they had camped in, with the stakes across the one open side. She watched the sky go from black to gray, and then it stayed there, a hide pulled across the world from one edge of the crowns to the other. The showers had stopped in the night. Nothing dripped or moved.

There was no breakfast. "Later," Keo said, when Benal looked at the empty pouch.

"When they return me," Benal said, "tell them I was very brave about breakfast."

"You'll get breakfast."

"I'm only saying. In case." He helped Keo pull the stakes, slowly.

Teva shouldered her pack, and when the straps settled she saw what she had seen yesterday and decided not to see: along both straps, where they rubbed, her suit had gone gray. Everywhere else it copied the morning; this was a dead patch that copied nothing. She shifted the straps a finger's width outward, onto living film, and left them there.

Then it was walking. They picked snails off the stems as they went, without stopping. The understory closed over her head in long stretches, and in this light she could not tell the dead leaves from the live ones. Once there was something small and white at the foot of a cycad, and she had it in her fingers before she knew she had stooped. She had half turned to put it somewhere before she remembered there was nobody to give it to. She let it drop and walked on.

Before the middle of the morning Benal said, "Wait."

Keo turned with the spike as they stopped and stood with his head down, reaching. Teva waited while the insect wall went on, soft through her suit.

"Something," Benal said. "Off that way, a long way. I think. A sound, possibly. It's stopped now."

They waited a little, not as long as yesterday.

Keo looked back at him. "The same nothing as yesterday?" he said. "Or a new one?"

"A new one," Benal said. "Possibly."

"Tell it we're busy." Keo waited while Benal listened, long enough to be fair, and then walked on. Teva walked on after him, with Benal last. She did not look back at him. She had trusted his thinking in the fog. This was only his ears, and his ears had stopped them three times since yesterday, once for a bird. She was hungry besides.

They lay up when the heat came down, in a hollow under a big conifer, and ate the snails. There were not many. Teva counted out her share and made it last.

Benal held one up. "Now we'll have to do this every day," he said.

Nobody laughed.

He ate it.

Noli came into the hollow and leaned her whole weight against Keo's leg, as she had since she was small. He held a snail down to her. "Here," he said, in the low, level voice he kept for her. "Go on. It's good."

She put her nose to it, took her head away, and looked at something else.

"She's off her food," Keo said.

"She's had your cooking before," Teva said.

He turned his head and looked at her. "They're good snails."

"They're snails."

Keo looked at her a moment longer, working out whether that had been a joke. Then he grinned and ate the snail himself. He was asleep almost at once, with Noli against his leg. Benal sat with his knees up and his eyes shut. Teva lay back on the leaves. In the hollow, in the heat that had come down on everything, she was cool, and she lay there feeling it like a hand on her back.

A week on snails had been a number yesterday. Today she was hungry. She had said *I know*, and she had known. She had not known what it would feel like on the second morning with no breakfast.

She thought of Omya. Omya would have gotten up this morning and not found her. Somebody would have come by, because somebody always did. They would sit with her and talk about the weather. Omya would answer, and some of the answers would be right. But she would be lonely. That was the thing Teva had not let herself think about when she left. Omya would not be helpless. She would be lonely, and some of the time she would know exactly why.

Teva shut her eyes, and it was all still there behind them.

They got up when the worst of the heat had gone off, in the middle of the afternoon. Noli stayed at Keo's heel when they set out again, so close she nearly tripped him. She had run ahead every day before. Teva watched her favor the shoulder and thought the shoulder was worse.

The light had gone down another step since the lay-up, as if the day meant to end early. They had been walking a little while when Benal said, "Wait."

She stopped. Keo did not.

"Something's stopped," Benal said behind her. "Something— I don't know what. Something's gone."

Teva listened. The insect wall came through her suit soft and even and everywhere, the same as it had all day. She could not hear anything gone. She could hear Keo's feet in the leaves ahead, going away.

Keo had his head down a little. He was reaching, she thought, or meant her to think he was. Then he kept walking anyway, without turning around.

She stood a moment longer with Benal behind her. Then she walked on too.
