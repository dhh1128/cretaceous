The Vitarium smelled wrong before Teva could see anything in it.

She had walked her grandmother down here more evenings than she could count, and the air at the bottom had always been cool and damp, mineral, faintly yeasty: living things kept carefully. Tonight something rank lay under it, sour, a gourd gone over.

When her eyes adjusted, she found the cultures in their lopsided clay vessels in the niches of the rock, each shaped like a lump the cave had made by accident, all of them meant to glow. The rust-red lines still did, and the blue, but dimly, like coals at the end of a night; the dimmest had gone gray. The rust-red and the blue had been alight since the jump, two hundred and six years <!-- @WF-transit.elapsed: 206y --> ago. Stone sapped the heat from her heels. She stayed at the mouth of the chamber.

She had brought Omya down the narrow passage herself, one hand on the wall and the other under her grandmother's elbow, a step at a time in the dark. Halfway down Omya stopped, and for a moment Teva thought she was lost again. Then Omya reached up, found the end of Teva's plait by feel, and retied it.

"Crooked," she said. "You'll want it straight in there. People look."

"I did yours first," Teva said.

"So you did." Omya felt the loops at the back of her own head, found them tight, and went on down.

At the bottom she had let go of Teva's arm and walked into the chamber on her own, upright, the way a Master walked into the Vitarium. That was the last thing Teva had been sure of.

Now Omya sat at the protocol station, her silver hair plaited in a Master's loops, her hands resting either side of the sequencing tray, where the brightest of the rust-red lines still lit it. Rano stood across from her, tired. When he spoke his voice was very patient. He had laid out the keys himself, in the order she had taught him at Teva's age, every one turned the same way, and his hands were stained to the wrist. For three weeks he had run the attempts under her direction, where anyone could watch, and come out each evening to tell people he was close.

"Let's start with the blue," he said. "From the beginning."

He was giving her something easy. Teva was grateful to him for it, and hated that he had to.

"Position one," Omya said. "Position two. Vector into culture, culture into sequencing —" and on she went, her voice never catching, as clean as Teva had heard it a hundred times at home while Teva did her plaits. Rano let her run to the end, nodding, his lips moving with hers. Something in Teva's chest unclenched a little. Maybe the last few weeks had been the bad weeks, and this would be a good evening after all.

Behind her, people were watching. She could hear them shifting: a handful of adults at the opening, yellow-green in the lightmold, who had not been asked and had come anyway.

"Good," said Rano. "That's good. Now the rust-red." He swallowed. "Position one-oh-seven, Omya. The final fold. Please." Her name came out of him raw.

Teva watched her grandmother's hands. They had pointed out the stars to her one at a time when she was small, and drilled her on the protocol until she could say it in her sleep. *Precision, Teva,* Omya used to say, tapping the table between them. *We are scientists, not shamans.*

Omya picked up a key from the tray. She turned it over, frowning, and tried it in the sequence. It did not lock. Neither did the next, and the tray stayed dark.

Teva took a step into the chamber before she knew she was going to. Without looking at her, Rano lifted one hand off the edge of the tray, a finger's width, and she stopped. It was Omya's to do. Whatever she still had of the fold had to come out of her, where everybody could see it.

Behind her, very low, somebody said, "She did it last month. I watched her."

"That was the blue," someone else said.

"The rust-red," Rano said again, in the voice for a child who had wandered off. "Just the fold. You know this, Omya. Please. You know this."

Omya looked up. Her eyes went past Rano, past the dying lights, and found Teva at the mouth of the chamber. For a moment her face was entirely her own: she knew exactly who she was looking at. Then it went. She turned back to the tray, picked up a blue key, and held it out to Rano with a polite frown.

"It has to rest before it goes in," she told him kindly, as if he were the one who had forgotten.

Teva knew the sentence. It was what Omya said over dough.

Somebody at the opening let out a breath. Feet went back up the passage: one person, and then another, who had seen enough and did not want to be seen having seen it.

Behind her came a scuffle and a cry, and Elana pushed in with her daughter in her arms, past the watchers and past Teva, into the one room in the Enclave where nobody ever brought the sick. Nobody stopped her.

"The rust-red," Elana said. She held Alira's arm out to them. Teva saw the stingburn at the wrist, swollen tight, and the red streak running up from it toward the elbow like something drawn on with a finger. "She needs the rust-red. A whole Seeding. You make it here — give it to her. Please. Give her all of it."

In the quiet after she stopped, Teva heard the child breathe. The air rattled in her throat, slow and then quick and then slow again. Teva had seen a streak that far up an arm before. Even a Seeding would have come too late.

Rano looked from Elana to the child to the dark tray.

"There's no dose," he said. "Elana. There isn't any. That's what this was. We can't make it. Nobody can make it."

Omya was still holding out the blue key.

Rano looked at it. Then, with one stroke of his arm, he swept every key off the tray, and the blue one out of her hand. The gels hit the stone with a wet slap and broke apart at Omya's feet. It was the only violent thing Teva had ever seen him do, and he did it to a tray.

Alira breathed again, that wet rattle, and Teva knew it. She had heard it two years ago, in their own nest, with the mats pushed back and the roof lit gray with morning: her mother, sick for days after a bite, the same spreading red. The Keepers had come with a Seeding, when there were still Keepers enough to make one. It had not taken. Teva had sat beside her and listened to that sound go on and on and then stop. She had not known she still had it in her ears.

Elana looked at Omya's puzzled face, at Rano, at the broken gels, and over her shoulder at the watchers, none of whom had moved; and Teva watched her understand that there was nothing. Her mouth stayed open, but what came out of it was no longer a demand. It came apart into sobbing. Her knees went, and she sank to the floor with the child in her arms and rocked her.

After a long time, the rattle hitched.

Slowed.

Stopped.

Elana's sobbing stopped with it. Then she made a noise that was not a word, low and torn up from under her ribs, and laid her daughter down on the stone and kept one hand on her. Behind Teva, the watchers bowed their heads.

Omya bent down, and Teva heard her knees crack in the silence. She picked up a piece of the broken blue key and looked at it in her palm, frowning.

The child lay on the stone among the lines that should have saved her, and her mother had stopped asking for anything. Teva stood at the mouth of the chamber. She did not cry, and she did not shake, and something in her went hard and stayed that way.
