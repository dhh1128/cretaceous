Benal lay along the top of the barrier with his cheek on the wood, gulping air, while the hull ground against the grating below his feet. The insect wall was all round him, after the surf and seabirds of Genesis. Through the damaged suit it came at him whole, every whine and chirr separate. Under it, getting louder, came voices.

A great many, and running feet, all coming toward the river. The lift units' scream had carried across the Enclave.

The watchers got to the end of the barrier first, spikes up. Behind them people came from the core and down from the nests, more every breath. Benal sat up and watched the faces find the three of them, then the wig heeled against the grating, then the three of them again.

"It's them. It's them, it's the three of them —"

"What is that? What did they come in?"

A woman near the front had both hands over her mouth, her eyes running. Beside her a man had sat down in the mud where he stopped. Hands came out toward the barrier and stopped in the air. Farther back, people had stepped away from the bank and stood staring past them at the hull.

"Get back from the water! Get back from it!"

"They flew it over the wall. Over the wall."

"Down," the nearest watcher said. "Come down off the end, slow. Nobody runs."

They came down onto the bank, Teva with the plates against her chest and Keo last, careful of his torn hands. He turned straight back toward the hull.

"Keep them off the grating. It's pinned, it's not going anywhere. Just keep them off it."

"Keo. Riel's Keo. We had you dead. All three of you, we had you dead."

Somebody pushed a water skin at Teva. She drank and drank and passed it to Benal. He drank before he knew whose it was, and it was taken back.

"We need the Vitarium," Teva said. "Now."

"What's that you're carrying?"

"The protocols. From Genesis."

"That's metal."

The word went back through the crowd behind him.

"It's the fold," Teva said. "The children are overdue."

"Let her through. Let her through, mine's overdue —"

"— took the suits, they took two suits out of the —"

"Quiet!" The watcher turned to the others. "The Vitarium, all three. Hold everyone else at the cleft."

They went up from the river between watchers, the crowd coming after, and at the kiva's cleft the watchers turned and stood across it. Through all of it, his mother's voice reached him.

"Benal! Benal!"

He turned his head toward it. Watchers stood between, with the crowd behind them. He couldn't see her, or his father.

"After! I'll come after —"

"Benal —"

Teva was already through with the plates, and he went after her across the kiva. A few of the Council on the dais turned to watch them cross. The passage took them in under the lightmold, and a few steps along it the rock shut the noise off.

He had never been inside the Vitarium. The air in it did not move, and after two hours in the cabin the stone's cool and damp landed like rain. The light came from lopsided vessels in niches around the walls, rust-red and blue, and a great many of them gray.

Rano stood at the trays with his weight on his hands, thinner than Benal remembered and stained to the wrist. Another journeyman worked beside him. Omya sat on a stool against the side wall with her hands in her lap, her hair plaited tight to her head. A watcher had stopped at the passage mouth.

Rano looked up.

"Teva."

"Rano."

"You went outside. How can you be back?"

Teva set the plates down in front of him. The edge of the bottom one rang on the stone.

"These are the protocols. All of it. Every line."

Rano looked at the metal. "These are the founders'."

"They're hers. Take them."

For one breath he didn't move. Benal heard it go in and stop. Then Rano put out his hands and took the top plate.

Teva ran her finger down the list at the foot of the first plate, found the plate by the number cut in its corner, and laid it on top. Her finger went down it and stopped.

"One-oh-seven. The final fold. Here. The fourth step."

Rano bent over it.

"Four." He read the step out in Keeper words, carefully. Benal had read the plates at Genesis and couldn't follow this one past its third word.

Rano's lips moved over it.

"Four." He read it again, slower, every word set down by itself. His voice cracked. His hands trembled. Then his head came up and turned to the side of the room. "Omya. Omya, it's the —"

"Teva," Omya said from her stool. "Are the figs ripe?"

Teva went to her.

"Not yet."

"You can't hurry a fig."

Teva's hand went to Omya's plait and stopped there. It was pulled hard from the scalp, and the loops at the back were not the ones Benal had seen on Omya all his life. He couldn't have said what was wrong with them. Teva could; he watched her see it. At Genesis, over the raw clams, she had said what she'd do first when they were home, and somebody else had been doing it. Her hand stayed on the plait and didn't take it down. Her eyes had gone wet, and she let it go no further.

Behind him Rano said, "That's it. That's where it stopped. That's all it was."

The breath went out of Rano.

The journeyman at the next tray was crying without a sound, and her hands kept moving.

Rano turned to her. "Fresh gels. The best of the rust-red, the line at the station. Now."

She went along the niches. Rano's hands went to the vessels, and after that he did not look up.

Teva stayed beside Omya, and Keo beside Benal. There was nothing for any of them to do.

Benal had been counting since the passage. Four minutes, roughly. Sixteen days since the fog. Eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->. Noli. Teva on the frame, and Keo at its poles. Sixteen days had twenty-three thousand minutes in them and some over, and four of them were this.

"Four minutes," he murmured to Keo.

"What?"

"From the passage to him working. Four minutes."

Keo looked at Rano's back. "That's all?"

"Roughly."

Keo went to the watcher at the passage mouth.

"Where are my parents?"

"Under guard. Since the night the suits went."

"Both of them?"

"Both."

Keo's hands closed at his sides and opened again.

"Can I see them?"

"That's for the Council to say."

Keo came back and stood beside Benal, closer than he needed to.

Benal had the founders' tablet in one hand and the cylinder under his other arm. He had the sum ready for the room that would be filling by now on the other side of the passage. The other thing he kept. The only people here who could have followed it were at the trays, and he couldn't lay it on the stone and point at it. Nobody had asked what was under his arm.

"Will they follow it?" Keo said. "What you've got there."

"Not tonight. Not all of it. I'll have to start them at the beginning."

The wall they had flown over had been habit, not law. His father had always said so.

He heard the step in the passage before anyone else did, one person, quick. The watcher turned, and a messenger came into the light at the passage mouth. Rano did not look up.

"The Council is sitting. It wants the three of you. Now."
