The top of the barrier was slick under Benal's hands. He lay along it with his cheek on the wood and pulled air into himself while the hull ground against the grating below his feet. Then the insect wall came in. There had been none of it at Genesis, only the surf and the seabirds, and the dead suit on him stopped none of it now. It came into his ears from every side at the one weight it had always had, and under it, getting louder, came voices.

There were a great many of them, and running feet, on the bank and in the groves behind it and farther off, all coming toward the river. The lift units had screamed over the tangle loud enough to reach every nest in the Enclave.

The watchers got to the end of the barrier first, three of them, with their spikes half up. Behind them people came out of the groves and down from the nests, more every breath. Benal sat up and watched the faces find the three of them, and then the wig heeled against the grating, and then the three of them again.

"It's them. It's them, it's the three of them —"

"What is that? What did they come in?"

A woman near the front had both hands over her mouth, and her eyes were running over them. Beside her a man had sat down on the mud where he stopped and stayed there. Others round them were crying openly; hands came out toward the barrier and stopped in the air. Farther back, people had stepped away from the bank and stood looking past the three of them at the hull.

"Get back from the water! Get back from it!"

"They flew it over the wall. Over the wall."

"Down," the nearest watcher said. "Come down off the end, slow. Nobody runs."

They came down off the end of the barrier onto the bank, Teva with the plates held against her chest and Keo last, slow on his torn hands. Keo turned straight back toward the hull.

"Keep them off the grating. It's pinned, it's not going anywhere. Just keep them off it."

The watcher's mouth worked before anything came out of it.

"Keo. Riel's Keo. We had you dead. All three of you, we had you dead."

Somebody pushed a gourd into Benal's hands. He drank before he knew whose it was, and it was taken back.

"We need the Vitarium," Teva said. "Now."

"What's that you're carrying?"

"The protocols. From Genesis."

"That's metal."

Benal heard the word go back through the crowd behind him, mouth to mouth, all the way to the groves.

"It's the fold," Teva said. "The children are overdue."

"Let her through. Let her through, mine's overdue —"

"— took the suits, they took two suits out of the —"

"Quiet!" The watcher turned to the others. "The Vitarium, all three. Hold everyone else at the cleft. Nobody else past the cleft."

They went up from the river with a watcher on either side and the crowd coming after, and at the kiva's cleft the watchers turned and stood across it. The crowd packed up against their backs. Over the insect wall and all of it, one voice reached him clear of every other. He would have known it anywhere.

"Benal! Benal!"

He turned his head toward it and could not see her. There were watchers between them, and bodies behind the watchers, and the ceratops hide swinging in the cleft.

"After! I'll come after —"

"Benal —"

Teva was already through, into the dim of the kiva with the plates, and he went after her. A few of the Council were on the dais and turned to watch them cross. Then the passage took them in under the yellow-green of the lightmold, and a few steps along it the rock shut the noise off behind him.

The Vitarium was cool. After two hours shut in the cabin, the damp of the stone came onto his skin like rain. The air in it did not move. He had never been inside. The light came from the vessels set in niches all round the walls, lopsided lumps of clay glowing rust-red and blue, and a great many of them gray. He was counting before he meant to, and the gray ones were ahead.

Rano stood at the trays with his weight on his hands. He was thinner than Benal remembered him, and stained to the wrist. Another journeyman worked at the tray beside him. Omya sat on a stool against the side wall with her hands in her lap, her hair plaited tight to her head. A watcher had stopped at the passage mouth.

Rano looked up.

"Teva."

"Rano."

"They said you'd gone outside. They said you were —"

Teva set the plates down on the stone in front of him. The edge of the bottom one knocked on the rock, and in that room it was loud.

"These are the protocols. All of it. Every line."

Rano looked at the metal. "These are the founders'."

"They're hers. Take them."

For one breath he did not move. Benal heard it go in and stop. Then Rano put out his stained hands and took the top plate. The other journeyman watched him do it, and went back to her tray.

Teva ran her finger down the list at the foot of the first plate and stopped on a number. She went through the stack by the numbers cut in their corners, drew one out and laid it on top. Her finger went down it and stopped again.

"One-oh-seven. The final fold. Here. The fourth step."

Rano bent over it.

"Four." He read the step out in Keeper words, a run of them, said carefully. Benal had read every plate at Genesis that would let him, and he could not follow this one past its third word.

Rano stopped and looked at Teva, then down again. His lips went over it without a sound.

"Four." He read it again, slower, every word set down by itself. At the end of it his head came up and turned to the side of the room. "Omya. Omya, it's the —"

"Teva," Omya said from her stool. "Are the figs ripe?"

Teva went to her.

"Not yet."

"You can't hurry a fig."

Teva's hand went to Omya's hair and stopped there. The plait was pulled hard from the scalp and tied off thick at the end, and the loops at the back were not the ones Benal had seen on Omya all his life. He could not have said what was wrong with them. Teva could. He watched her see it. At Genesis, over the raw clams, she had said what she would do first when they were home, and here somebody else had done it. Her hand stayed on the plait and did not take it down. Her eyes had gone wet. She kept them on Omya's hair and let it go no further than that.

Behind him Rano said, "That's it. That's where it stopped. That's all it was."

The breath went out of him, all of it, and Benal heard it go.

The journeyman at the next tray was crying. She made no sound, and her hands kept moving.

Rano turned to her. "Fresh gels. The best of the rust-red, the line at the station. Now."

She went along the niches. Rano's hands went to the vessels, and after that he did not look up.

The three of them stood in the middle of the room. Teva was beside Omya, and Keo beside Benal. There was nothing for any of them to do.

Benal had been counting since the passage. Four minutes, roughly, from the noise going behind them to a Keeper with his hands in the cultures. Sixteen days since the fog. Eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->. Noli. Teva on the frame, and Keo between the poles. Sixteen days had twenty-three thousand minutes in them and some over, and four of them were this.

"Four minutes," he said to Keo, low.

"What?"

"From the passage to him working. Four minutes."

Keo looked at Rano's back. "That's all?"

"Roughly."

At the station the journeyman was passing Rano gels one at a time, and neither of them had heard.

Keo went to the watcher at the passage mouth. Benal watched him go, slow on the ankle.

"Where are my parents?"

"Under guard. Since the night the suits went."

"Both of them?"

"Both."

Keo's torn hands closed at his sides and opened again.

"Can I see them?"

"That's for the Council to say."

Keo nodded and came back across the room and stood beside Benal, closer than he needed to.

Benal had the founders' tablet in one hand and the capsule under his other arm. Two hundred and twenty from year three, and this was two hundred and six. He had the sum ready for the room that had to hear it, and that room was filling on the other side of the passage. The other thing he kept. The only two people here who could have followed it were at the trays, and it was not a thing he could lay on the stone and point at. Nobody had asked what was under his arm.

"Will they follow it?" Keo said. "What you've got there."

"Not tonight. Not all of it. I'll have to start them at the beginning."

The wall they had flown over had been habit, not law. His father had said so in the Council for as long as Benal could remember.

He heard the step in the passage before anyone else did: one person, quick, coming down from the kiva. Then the watcher turned, and a messenger came into the light at the passage mouth and stopped beside him. Rano did not look up.

"The Council is sitting. It wants the three of you. Now."
