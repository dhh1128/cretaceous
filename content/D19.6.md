Benal lay along the top of the barrier with his cheek on the wood, gulping air, while the hull ground against the grating below his feet. The old insect noise was all round him again, after the surf and seabirds of Genesis. Through the damaged suit it came at him whole, every whine and chirr separate.

Under it, getting louder, came voices. A great many, and running feet, all coming toward the river.

Watchers got to the end of the barrier first, croc spikes raised. Behind them, on a strip of higher ground, people came running from the core. More came with every breath. Benal sat up and watched the faces find the three of them, then the wig heeled against the grating, then the three of them again.

"It's them. It's them, it's the three of them —"

"What is that? What did they come in?"

A woman near the front had both hands over her mouth, her eyes running. Beside her a man knelt in the muddy grass. Hands came out toward the barrier and stopped in the air. Farther back, people stood staring past them at the hull.

"Don't go near the water! Get back from it!"

"They flew it over the wall. Over the wall!"

"Down!" the nearest watcher shouted. "Come down off the end, slow. Nobody runs."

They came down onto the strip of high ground, Teva with the plates against her chest and Keo last, careful of his torn hands. He turned straight back toward the hull.

"Keep them off the grating. It's pinned, it's not going anywhere. Just keep them off it."

"Keo. Riel's Keo. We had you dead. All three of you, we had you dead."

Somebody pushed a water skin at Teva. She guzzled, passed it to Benal. He drank and drank.

"We need the Vitarium," Teva said. "Now."

"What's that you're carrying?"

"The protocols. From Genesis."

"That's metal!"

A hush fell on the crowd. So the strange craft wasn't all of the heresy.

"It's the fold," Teva said. "For the children who are overdue."

There was a long silence.

"Let her through! Let her through, mine's overdue —"

"— took the suits, they took two suits out of the —"

"Quiet!" The watcher turned to the others. "The Vitarium, all three. Hold everyone else at the kiva."

They went up from the river between watchers, the crowd coming after, and at the kiva's cleft the watchers turned and stood across it. Through all of it, his mother's voice reached him.

"Benal! Benal!"

He turned his head. Too many people stood between. He couldn't see her, or his father.

"After! I'll come after —" he called.

"Benal —"

Teva was already through the opening with the plates, and he went down after her, eyes struggling to adjust late afternoon glare to dim lightmold. A few of the Council were gathered on the dais; they turned in astonishment to watch them cross to the deeper chamber beyond.

The air in the Vitarium did not move, and after two hours in the cabin the stone's cool and damp landed like rain. Faint light came from lopsided vessels in niches around the walls, rust-red and blue, and a great many of them gray.

Rano stood at the trays with his weight on his hands, thinner than Benal remembered and stained to the wrist. Another journeyman worked beside him. Omya sat on a stool against the side wall with her hands in her lap, her hair plaited tight to her head. A watcher had stopped at the passage mouth.

Rano looked up.

"Teva!"

"Rano."

"You went outside. How can you be back?"

Teva set the plates down in front of him. The edge of the bottom one rang on the stone.

"Here are the protocols. All of them. Every line."

Rano looked at the metal. "These are the founders'."

She gestured at Omya. "They're hers. Yours. Ours. Take them."

He didn't move. Benal heard the man's breath go in and stop. Then Rano put out his hands and reverently touched the top plate.

Teva reached out and moved his finger down the list toward the bottom of the first plate, then tapped once and held it there.

"One-oh-seven. The final fold. Here. The fourth step."

Rano bent. His eyes widened.

"Four." He read the step out in keeper words, carefully. Benal had read the plates at Genesis and couldn't follow the biology terms.

"Four." He read it again, slower, every word set down by itself. His voice cracked. His hands trembled. Then his head came up and turned to the side of the room. "Omya. Omya, it's the —"

"Teva," Omya said from her stool. "Are the figs ripe?"

Teva went to her.

"I think they finally are," she whispered.

"That's good. You can't hurry a fig."

Teva's hand went to Omya's plait and stopped there. It was pulled hard from the scalp, and the loops at the back were not the ones Benal had seen on Omya all his life. He couldn't have said what was wrong with them. Teva could; he watched her see it. Her eyes had gone wet.

The apprentice at the next tray was crying without a sound. Rano turned to her. "Get fresh gels. The best of the rust-red, the line at the station. Now."

She jumped up, wiping at her cheeks.

They stood for a long time, relishing the chance to be cool and quiet and do nothing, and watch the keepers work.

Benal's mind was estimating. They'd been gone for sixteen days. That was twenty-three thousand minutes for the fog and razortails, the crocs at the narrows. Noli. Teva on the frame, and Keo at its poles.

"Four minutes," he murmured to Keo.

"What?"

"Four minutes from the kiva to starting the cultures healing. Four minutes. But what those four minutes cost us..."

Keo put a hand on his friend's arm and squeezed once.

They stood some more, watching the keepers bustle. Something was different about their faces, Benal realized. He hadn't seen that expression for a long time.

The keepers were hopeful.

Footsteps and the murmur of voices in the council chamber filtered into the Vitariam from the passage mouth. Keo turned to the watcher who stood there.

"Where are my parents?"

"Under guard. Since the night the suits went."

Benal saw Keo's hands clench.

"Both of them?"

"Both."

"Can I see them?"

"That's for the Council to say."

Keo looked at Benal. His jaw moved.

Benal had the cylinder tucked mostly out of sight under his arm, but he was holding a founders' tablet in one hand. Now Keo gestured at it. "Will they follow what's written there?"

Benal shook his head. "Not tonight. Not all of it. I'll have to start them at the beginning."

A new watcher stepped into the mouth of the passage and cleared his throat.

"The Council is sitting. It wants the three of you. Now."
