The rain had stopped and the light was going by the time Keo reached the cleft. The Council had started without him, and he got no further than the back of the room.

The chamber was full to the walls, people shoulder to shoulder on the floor and three deep behind them, still wet from the storm, the air close and warm with them. Rain-off pattered through the hole in the roof onto the heap of fallen rock. Keo put his back to the cold stone by the entrance. The lattices in the walls glowed yellow-green, brightest along the dais and dim back here.

The lattices grown into the walls gave the room its light, yellow-green and steady, brightest along the dais and dim back here.

On the floor near the front, Marek finished a sentence. When the dais let it go unanswered, he started another.

"— and they are not getting better. Look at them." He swung an arm toward the bench where the Keepers sat. "Ask Rano how close he is. Ask him."

Rano did not look up. At the end of the bench something moved, and Keo's eyes went there without being sent: Teva, sitting very straight, her hand on her grandmother's arm. Omya sat beside her with her silver hair in its loops, looking at the lightmold and nothing else. Keo had brought her flowers more than once. Teva went rigid when Marek pointed. Keo took it for anger.

"Eighty parts in a hundred," Marek said. "That's what the journeymen hold. Eighty parts of a thing that is useless unless it is whole, and the last twenty are in a head that can't give them back." He did not look along the bench to see whose head. "The whole of it is sitting at Genesis. The founders wrote it down, on metal, so it would last, and left it in the Archive, eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from where we are standing, and we let children die eighty kilometers from the answer because we are afraid of the walk. Send a party. Bring it back."

Keo's parents thought well of Marek, and that had always been enough for Keo. Tonight he had his own reason: the Council had been waiting since the flood, and Marek was the only person in the room asking it to stand up. Keo looked for Ben's head above the crowd near the front and did not find it.

From the dark a few paces to his left, a man shouted, "It's not even our future!"

The room let it stand.

"The jump split the world. Whatever we do here, it doesn't touch theirs. It never could. So what are we hiding from?"

"Enough."

Sila had let the shout die. She sat at the center of the dais with her hands open on her knees, and answered as if she had been waiting all evening for that exact question.

"One lost lightcell," she said. "One. Dropped in the mud by somebody in a hurry, and trodden in, and lying there under everything that comes after us, until someone who has never heard our names picks it up. And everything they are comes apart in their hand. That is what we are hiding from." She let it sit. "One lightcell. One metal knife. One footprint."

Then she stood up, like someone who had just woken. The others on the dais sat slumped, folded in on themselves like everyone in the room, and something in Keo rose with her. The Council was finally waking up.

"And we have not hidden well enough," she said, "and there is nothing we can do about it. Listen. The founders passed down a promise made for people waiting on the shore at the very edge of it: that the end would wipe them clean. We have been eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-enclave.inland: 80km --> inland for two hundred years. Not one of you can tell me how far the edge reaches. And the cycad. Every grove we planted. A seed does not stay where you put it; it goes where the birds take it, and it grows. Every plant that comes of it carries our changes in every cell, and no one has to dig anything up to find us. Someone only has to sample one." Her voice had grown stronger. "Those are the two things we cannot fix. So we do not add a third."

She had got up to say no. She was the only one in the room who wasn't tired, and she had spent all of it on *no*. Lately Keo had begun to wonder whether the people on the dais knew as much as everyone let them think.

Hesh, beside her, spoke without getting up.

"Sila is right that we cannot fix it," he said. "So let us at least count." He held up one hand. "Two hundred people <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> left Genesis, every one of them in a suit. They had rifles. They had plasma cutters. They had everything we will never have again, and they left anyway: no water, nothing that would grow, and the disorientation, whatever it was, that had people there seeing and smelling things that were not there." He closed the hand. "Ninety-four of them reached this place."

"We have twelve suits <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 -->. Ten of them work. Someone is going to say, then send ten." Keo had been about to think exactly that. "Ten, in old suits, with croc-spikes, is not a smaller version of what they did. It is a worse one. I will not send ten people <!-- @WF-suits.working: 10 --> to die in the country that killed more than half of the best-equipped party this world will ever see."

The silence went on long enough that Keo looked at his parents, two meters <!-- @WF-keo.parents.distance: 2m --> off at the near edge of the crowd, properly for the first time since he came in.

His father sat on the floor with his arms crossed. His mother stood beside him, crying.

Her face was perfectly still while the tears ran down it. She did not wipe them. That morning she had been counting his sighs across the mat and laughing at him. Hesh had just given the right number, the one she would have given herself, and Keo could not see what she was crying about.

His father saw him looking.

He got up, the only time he had moved all evening, and stepped into the crowd, and two people shifted to let him through, opening a narrow view of the dais for Keo. His father stood there saying nothing, as he might have moved a branch out of Keo's way on a trail.

Keo looked away. He was sixteen, not six.

A woman near the front spoke up. "Who would you send? Tell me who. The best Explorers we have, and the Watchers off the wall, and the tangle left with no one on it while they're gone. And if they don't come back we've lost the palisade and the walk both." Her voice was only tired. "It's suicide."

She meant his mother and father. And she was not wrong.

"Suicide," said Marek. He was on his feet now, his voice climbing. "Suicide is staying! Suicide is sitting in this cave counting suits while the cultures die in their pots!"

He ran out of breath.

"Better to die with a clean soul," Sila said, "than to live as contaminants."

The chamber erupted.

Half of them were on their feet. The noise came off the bare stone and doubled, and the only voice Keo could pick out was Marek's, still going. Lunet had both hands on his arm, trying to pull him down, saying his name over and over.

Then the hide at Keo's elbow moved.

Elana came in so close that her sleeve brushed his arm. She did not see him. She did not see anyone. She walked in two steps and stopped with her arms at her sides, and the noise died in a ragged wave from the back to the front.

"She didn't die from a croc," Elana said. Her voice was low, and in that silence it carried. "She didn't fall from a nest." She looked at the dais. "You're talking about the protocol. About what's clean. They are just words. They are air." Her voice did not break. "My daughter is gone."

She turned and went out the way she had come. The hide fell closed behind her, and she went alone.

Keo looked for Teva again. Everyone around her had sagged, and she still sat upright beside Omya, looking at Sila, memorizing her.

"We can still send —" Marek's voice failed him. He stood with his mouth open, Lunet's hands still on his arm, and after a moment he let her sit him down.

Sila waited until he was sitting.

"The grief is raw," she said gently. "Nobody here is without it. But the protocol does not bend for grief. We will not decide this in one night, in this state. We will take the time to decide it well."

"You said that," said Teva.

She said it flat, from the end of the bench, without standing. Sila's eyes went to her once and came away.

Keo stared at her. He had stood at the back of this room for an hour with the same thing in him. From him it would have come out loud and too fast, like a child stamping. From her it sounded true. *Somebody has to do something* had sat in him all evening with no shape to it. Now it was a thought, and he had heard it said aloud.

"The Council will sit again," Sila said, "when it has considered."

That was all. Nobody voted.

People began to get up and shuffle toward the cleft, the room's one way out. As those nearest Keo shifted, a gap opened between him and the near edge of the floor. His father was looking straight at him.

There was nothing in his face but fear.
