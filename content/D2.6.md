The rain had stopped and the light was going by the time Keo reached the cleft. It had started without him, and he got no further than the back of the room.

Not a head turned. The chamber was full to the walls, people sitting shoulder to shoulder on the floor and standing three deep behind them, still wet from the storm, and the air was close and warm with them. Cooler air came in at the cleft behind him and rose out through the hole in the roof, where rain-off still pattered onto the heap of fallen rock. Keo put his back to the cold stone by the entrance and looked over the heads.

The lattices grown into the walls gave the room its light, yellow-green and steady, brightest along the dais and dim back here.

On the floor near the front, Marek finished a sentence. When the dais let it go unanswered, he started another.

"— and they are not getting better. Look at them." He swung an arm toward the bench where the Keepers sat. "Ask Rano how close he is. Ask him."

Rano did not look up. At the end of the bench something moved, and Keo's eyes went there without being sent: Teva, sitting very straight, her hand on her grandmother's arm. Omya sat beside her with her silver hair in its loops, looking at the lightmold and at nothing else. Keo had brought her flowers more than once. Teva went rigid when Marek pointed. Keo took it for anger. The room would have made anybody angry.

"Eighty parts in a hundred," Marek said. "That's what the journeymen hold. They've told us so. Eighty parts of a thing that is useless unless it is whole, and the last twenty are in a head that can't give them back." He did not look along the bench to see whose head. "And the whole of it is sitting at Genesis. The founders wrote all of it down, on metal, so it would last, and they left it in the Archive, eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from where we are standing, and we let children die eighty kilometers from the answer because we are afraid of the walk. Send a party. Bring it back. That is all I am asking."

Keo's parents thought well of Marek, and that had always been reason enough for Keo. Tonight he had a reason of his own. The Council had been waiting since the flood, and Marek was the only person in the room asking it to stand up. Keo looked for Ben's head above the crowd near the front and did not find it.

From the dark a few paces to his left, a man shouted, "It's not even our future!"

The room let it stand.

"The jump split the world. Whatever we do here, it doesn't touch theirs. It never could. So what are we hiding from?"

"Enough."

Sila had waited for the shout to die. She sat at the center of the dais with her hands open on her knees, and she answered at once, as if she had been waiting all evening for that exact question.

"One lost lightcell," she said. "One. Dropped in the mud by somebody in a hurry, and trodden in, and lying there under everything that comes after us, until someone who has never heard our names picks it up. And everything they are comes apart in their hand. That is what we are hiding from." She let it sit. "One lightcell. One metal knife. One footprint."

Then she stood up.

All evening the others on the dais had sat slumped, folded in on themselves like everyone in the room. Sila got to her feet like someone who had just woken, and something in Keo rose with her. The Council was finally waking up.

"And we have not hidden well enough," she said, "and there is nothing we can do about it. Listen. The founders passed down a promise that was made for people waiting on the shore at the very edge of it: that the end would wipe them clean. We have been eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-enclave.inland: 80km --> inland for two hundred years. Nobody can tell me where the edge is. Not one of you can tell me how far it reaches." She turned, taking them all in. "And the cycad. Every grove we planted. A seed does not stay where you put it; it goes where the birds take it, and it grows. Every plant that comes of it carries our changes in every cell, and no one has to dig anything up to find us. Someone only has to sample one." Her voice had grown stronger. "Those are the two things we cannot fix. So we do not add a third."

She had got up to say no. She was the only one in the room who wasn't tired, and she had spent all of it on *no*. Keo had always thought of the Council as dull adult business that went on over his head. Lately he had begun to wonder whether the people on the dais knew as much as everyone let them think.

Hesh, beside her, spoke without getting up.

"Sila is right that we cannot fix it," he said. "So let us at least count." He held up one hand. "Two hundred people <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> left Genesis. Every one of them in a suit. They had rifles. They had plasma cutters. They had everything we will never have again, and they left anyway: no water, nothing that would grow, and the disorientation, whatever it was, that had people there seeing and smelling things that were not there. Nobody ever explained it." He closed the hand. "Ninety-four of them reached this place."

"We have twelve suits <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 -->. Ten of them work. Someone is going to say, then send ten." Keo had been about to think exactly that. "Two hundred, suited and armed, came out at ninety-four. Ten, in old suits, with croc-spikes, is not a smaller version of that. It is a worse one. I will not send ten people <!-- @WF-suits.working: 10 --> to die in the country that killed more than half of the best-equipped party this world will ever see."

The silence went on long enough that Keo looked at his parents, two meters <!-- @WF-keo.parents.distance: 2m --> off at the near edge of the crowd, properly for the first time since he came in.

His father sat on the floor with his arms crossed, as he had all evening. His mother stood beside him. She was crying.

Her face was perfectly still, and the tears ran down it, and she did not wipe them. That morning she had been counting his sighs across the mat and laughing at him. Hesh had just given a number, and it was the right one, the number she would have given herself. And she was crying, and Keo could not see what about.

His father saw him looking.

He got up, the only time he had moved all evening, and stepped into the crowd. Two people shifted to let him through, which opened a narrow view of the dais for Keo. His father stood there, practical, saying nothing, the way he might have moved a branch out of Keo's way on a trail.

Keo looked away. He was sixteen, not six. He didn't need a space cleared for him.

A woman near the front spoke up. "Who would you send? Tell me who. The best Explorers we have, and the Watchers off the wall, and the tangle left with no one on it while they're gone. And if they don't come back we've lost the palisade and the walk both." Her voice was only tired. "It's suicide."

She was talking about his mother and father. Keo understood that all at once, and understood too that she was not wrong.

"Suicide," said Marek. He was on his feet now, his voice climbing. "Suicide is staying! Suicide is sitting in this cave counting suits while the cultures die in their pots!"

He ran out of breath.

"Better to die with a clean soul," Sila said, "than to live as contaminants."

The chamber erupted.

Half of them were on their feet. The noise came off the bare stone and doubled, voices on top of voices, and the only one Keo could pick out was Marek's, still going. Lunet had both hands on his arm, trying to pull him down, saying his name over and over.

Then the hide at Keo's elbow moved.

Elana came in through it. She passed so close that her sleeve brushed his arm. She did not see him. She did not see anyone. She walked in two steps and stopped, and the noise died in a ragged wave from the back to the front, as the people nearest her went quiet and the people in front of them turned to see why.

She stood there with her arms at her sides.

"She didn't die from a croc," Elana said. Her voice was low, and in that silence it carried. "She didn't fall from a nest." She looked at the dais. "You're talking about the protocol. About what's clean. They are just words. They are air." Her voice did not break. "My daughter is gone."

She turned and went out the way she had come. The hide fell closed behind her, and she went alone.

Keo looked for Teva again. Everyone around her had sagged, and she still sat upright at the end of the bench beside Omya, looking at Sila steadily, memorizing her.

Marek tried to take it up again. "We can still send —" His voice failed him. He stood with his mouth open. Lunet's hands were still on his arm, and after a moment he let her sit him down.

Sila waited until he was sitting.

"The grief is raw," she said gently. "Nobody here is without it. But the protocol does not bend for grief. We will not decide this in one night, in this state. We will take the time to decide it well."

"You said that," said Teva.

She did not stand or raise her voice. She said it from the end of the bench, flat. Sila's eyes went to her once and came away again.

Keo stared at her. He had stood at the back of this room for an hour with the same thing in him. He knew how it would have come out of him, loud and too fast, like a child stamping. From her it had sounded true. *Somebody has to do something* had sat in him all evening with no shape to it. Now it was a thought, and he had heard it said aloud.

"The Council will sit again," Sila said, "when it has considered."

That was all. Nobody voted.

The people on the floor began to get up and shuffle toward the cleft, the room's one way out. As those nearest Keo shifted, a gap opened between him and the near edge of the floor. His father was looking straight at him.

There was nothing in his face but fear.
