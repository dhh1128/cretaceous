It had started without him, and Keo got no further than the back of the room.

He came in unremarked; not a head turned. The chamber was full to the walls, people sitting shoulder to shoulder on the floor and standing three deep behind them, wet from the storm, and the air was close and warm with all of them. Cooler air still came in at the cleft behind him and went up and out through the hole in the roof. Rain-off still pattered down that hole onto the heap of fallen rock under it. Keo stood with his back against the cold stone by the entrance and looked over the heads.

The lattices grown into the walls gave the room its light, yellow-green and steady, brightest along the dais and dim back here, where the lattice was mostly left to itself. The walls were bare. He had never seen one that wasn't.

On the floor near the front, Marek finished a sentence, and the dais let it go unanswered, so he started another.

"— and they are not getting better. Look at them." He swung an arm toward the bench where the Keepers sat. "Ask Rano how close he is. Ask him."

Rano did not look up. And beyond him, at the end of the bench, something moved, and Keo's eyes went there without being sent: Teva, sitting very straight, with her hand on her grandmother's arm. Omya sat beside her with her hands in her lap and her silver hair in its loops and looked at the lightmold as if it were the only thing in the room. Teva went rigid when Marek pointed. Keo took it for anger, which the room would have made anybody feel.

"Eighty parts in a hundred," Marek said. "That's what the journeymen hold. They've told us so. Eighty parts of a thing that is useless unless it is whole, and the last twenty are in a head that can't give them back." He did not look along the bench to see whose head. "And the whole of it is sitting at Genesis. The founders wrote it down. They wrote all of it down, on metal, so it would last, and they left it in the Archive, eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from where we are standing, and we sit here and let children die eighty kilometers from the answer because we are afraid of the walk. Send a party. Bring it back. That is all I am asking. Send someone and bring it back."

From the dark at the back, somebody shouted, "It's not even our future!"

Keo did not see who. The voice came out of the crowd a few paces to his left, a man's, and the room let it stand.

"The jump split the world. Whatever we do here, it doesn't touch theirs. It never could. So what are we hiding from?"

"Enough."

Sila had waited for the shout to die away before she said it. She sat at the center of the dais with her hands open on her knees, and she looked out over all of them, and she spoke as if she had been waiting for that exact question all evening.

"One lost lightcell," she said. "One. Dropped in the mud by somebody in a hurry, and trodden in, and lying there under everything that comes after us, until someone who has never heard our names picks it up. And everything they are comes apart in their hand. That is what we are hiding from." She let it sit. "One lightcell. One metal knife. One footprint."

Then she stood up.

Keo had watched the dais since he came in, and the others on it sat slumped, folded in on themselves like everyone in the room. Sila got to her feet as if she had just woken.

"And we have not hidden well enough," she said, "and there is nothing we can do about it. Listen. The founders passed down a promise that was made for people waiting on the shore at the very edge of it: that the end would wipe them clean. We have been eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-enclave.inland: 80km --> inland for two hundred years. Nobody can tell me where the edge is. Not one of you can tell me how far it reaches." She turned, taking them all in. "And the cycad. Every grove we planted. A seed does not stay where you put it; it goes where the birds take it, and it grows. Every plant that comes of it carries our changes in every cell, and no one has to dig anything up to find us. Someone only has to sample one." Her voice was stronger than when she had started. "Those are the two things we cannot fix. So we do not add a third."

Keo watched her and thought: she's the only one in here who isn't tired. He thought it was strength.

Hesh, beside her, had not moved. He waited until she had finished and sat down again, and then he spoke without getting up.

"Sila is right that we cannot fix it," he said. "So let us at least count." He held up one hand. "Two hundred people <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> left Genesis. Every one of them in a suit. They had rifles. They had plasma cutters. They had everything we will never have again." He closed the hand. "Ninety-four of them reached this place."

"We have twelve suits <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 -->. Ten of them work. Someone is going to say, then send ten." No one had said it yet. Keo had been about to think it. "Two hundred, suited and armed, came out at ninety-four. Ten, in old suits, with croc-spikes, is not a smaller version of that. It is a worse one. I will not send ten people <!-- @WF-suits.working: 10 --> to die on a walk that killed more than half of the best-equipped party this world will ever see."

Nobody answered him.

The silence went on long enough that Keo looked at his parents. He had seen them when he came in, two meters <!-- @WF-keo.parents.distance: 2m --> off at the near edge of the crowd, and he had not looked at them properly since. He looked now.

His father was sitting on the floor with his arms crossed, as he had been the whole time. His mother was standing beside him. She was crying.

She was not doing it loudly, or so that anyone else would see. She stood with her arms at her sides and her face perfectly still, and tears ran down it, and she did not wipe them. Keo stared at her. Hesh had just given a number, and it was right, and she had nothing to correct and nothing to say, and she was crying, and Keo could not see what about.

His father saw him looking.

He got up, the only time he had moved all evening, and stepped into the crowd, and two people shifted to let him through, and that opened a narrow view of the dais for Keo. Then his father stood there, practical, saying nothing, the way he might have moved a branch out of Keo's way on a trail.

Keo looked away.

In the crowd, a woman spoke up, near the front. "Who would you send?" she said. "Tell me who. The best Explorers we have, and the Watchers off the wall, and the tangle left with no one on it while they're gone. And if they don't come back we've lost the palisade and the walk both." Her voice was not angry. It was only tired. "It's suicide."

She was talking about his mother and father. Keo understood that all at once, and understood too that she was not wrong.

"Suicide," said Marek, and he was on his feet now, and his voice had gone up and was going higher. "Suicide is staying! Suicide is sitting in this cave counting suits while the cultures die in their pots!"

Marek ran out of breath.

"Better to die with a clean soul," Sila said, "than to live as contaminants."

The chamber erupted.

Everyone was talking at once, half of them on their feet, and the noise came off the bare stone and doubled and came back, voices on top of voices, nothing in it Keo could pick out but Marek's, still going, and near him Lunet with both hands on his arm, trying to pull him down, saying his name over and over.

Then the hide at Keo's elbow moved.

Elana came in through it. She passed so close to him that her sleeve brushed his arm, and she did not see him. She did not see anyone. She walked in two steps and stopped, and the noise stopped with her, not all at once but in a ragged wave from the back to the front, as the people nearest her saw her and went quiet and the people in front of them turned to see why.

She stood there in the doorway with her arms at her sides.

"She didn't die from a croc," Elana said. Her voice was low, and in that silence it carried. "She didn't fall from a nest." She looked at the dais. "You're talking about the protocol. About what's clean. They are just words. They are air." Her voice did not break. "My daughter is gone."

She turned and went out the way she had come, and the hide fell closed behind her, and she went alone.

Keo looked for Teva again. She still sat upright at the end of the bench beside Omya, when everyone around her had sagged, and she was looking at Sila as if she meant to remember her.

Marek tried to take it up again. "We can still send —" His voice failed him. He stood there with his mouth open and nothing coming out of it, and Lunet's hands were still on his arm, and after a moment he let her sit him down.

Sila waited until he was sitting.

"The grief is raw," she said gently. "Nobody here is without it. But the protocol does not bend for grief. We will not decide this in one night, in this state. We will take the time to decide it well."

"You said that," said Teva.

She did not stand or raise her voice. She said it from the end of the bench, flat, and the dais did not answer her.

Keo looked at her for a long time.

"The Council will sit again," Sila said, "when it has considered."

That was all. Nobody voted.

The people on the floor began to get up and turn toward the cleft, slowly, the whole room shuffling toward its one way out, and as those nearest Keo shifted, a gap opened between him and the near edge of the floor, and his father was looking straight at him.

There was nothing in his face but fear.
