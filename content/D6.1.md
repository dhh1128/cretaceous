They were barely out of camp at first light when the first web took Benal across the mouth.

He spat and clawed at it, and it came away in strings that clung to his fingers. Keo, who had walked straight under it, turned to look. Benal took two steps into the next one, eye-high this time, and something the size of a thumbnail ran across his eyebrow and into his hair.

"They're all at your height," Keo said. "They've been waiting for you all night."

"Possibly they're waiting for everyone," Benal said, picking at his eyelashes, "and I'm the only one tall enough to be caught." He walked into a third.

After that he went first, arm up before his face, the webs breaking across it one after another and the razor-club knocking at his hip. It was the stupidest thing that had happened to him since the tangle. At home, about now, he'd be folded into his corner with the board on his knees, and nothing would be waiting for him to walk into it. But the light had come up clean and gold, the first in two days, a dry breeze moved in the crowns, and the damp bindings hung off the packs to dry as they walked.

The band across his chest was still there. He breathed in halves and walked.

No breakfast again. The hunger was a hollow under the band. The woodland had opened into pale-barked oak and walnut, with low conifers between, and the air smelled of turned earth and green sap and resin.

The cones under the conifers had opened in the dry. "Pinyons," he said, and bent, carefully, and shook one, and the nuts fell into his palm.

Keo cracked one in his teeth and went still. They filled a pack. Shelling was slow, so they saved the nuts and walked, and the walking was easy.

By midmorning they came to a stream, wide and shallow over gravel, every stone on the bottom showing. On a bar on the far side a croc lay in the sun.

All three stopped dead, as every child in the Enclave was taught to. Benal stood with his heart going and looked at it.

It was small: a meter and a half, possibly less, slim and young. It looked at them a long moment. Then it slid off the bar into the shallows and was gone, and nothing happened.

Nothing went on happening. The stream ran. A bird somewhere upstream said the same thing twice.

"It doesn't want us," Benal said at last. "I think. It's too small. We're too big. Possibly it's more afraid of us than we are of it, which I've never said about a croc before, and I'd like it noted."

Keo held his spike up after the water had closed, then let the point down until it rested on the gravel.

"At home," he said, "that one would be—"

Benal saw his face and knew the rest. At home a small croc found inside the perimeter was killed where it lay, and since Keo's sister everybody knew why. This, Benal thought, was the first one Keo had ever been able to leave alone.

Keo looked at the water a while longer, then turned upstream.

They did not wash where it had gone in. Upstream the gravel ran under a hand's depth of water, with nowhere for anything to be. They filled the skins there. Keo knelt and splashed his face from cupped hands, again and again. Teva crouched at the very edge and washed her face one-handed, eyes on the water.

Past the stream, Benal smelled it.

He'd been smelling everything all morning and had stopped sorting it, as he'd learned to. Then, upwind, under the sap: resin, and something sweet in the resin, so faint he thought he'd made it up out of wanting it. He stood with his face to the breeze until he was sure.

"Wait," he said.

They stopped. They waited. Neither of them looked tired of it.

"Honey," he said. "I think. That way."

"How do you know?" Teva said. "From here. How do you tell honey from resin?"

He looked at her. She was waiting for the answer.

"Resin's sharp," he said. "It sits at the top of your nose, like the conifers. This has the sharp, but there's something under it, warm, like a fig gone soft in the sun. Resin doesn't have anything under it. And it comes and goes on the breeze, so it's a small place, not a whole tree weeping." He sniffed again. "Possibly a very small place. I nearly missed it."

She nodded, and let him lead.

He followed it through the oaks with his nose up, like Noli, Keo told him, until he could hear them: a high, close drone from a pale limb overhead where the tree had gone hollow. Small bees came and went from a short spout of hardened resin at the hole.

Keo climbed. Benal couldn't lift his arms over his head without the band stopping his breath, and Teva had one good arm. Keo went up on his torn hands. The scabs split on the bark and the blood started, and he didn't slow down.

The bees came out to meet him. They did not sting; they could not. They mobbed him, into his hair and his ears and the corners of his eyes, biting. He hung one-handed, hacking at the limb with his knife and spitting them off his lips. Benal could hear every one.

Then Keo was passing down pots, soft brown lumps the size of a fig, waxy and leaking, and Teva laid them on a leaf with her good hand. He came down with bees still in his hair and his eyes swollen half shut, grinning.

They ate the first pots standing up. Benal bit into one. The honey ran out over his tongue, and he had to sit down.

Two days of snails, and then this, so sweet it was almost a pain at the hinge of his jaw. His eyes watered and his hands shook. He ate the wax too. Keo was licking his split fingers. Teva had her eyes shut.

Noli had come back at the smell and stood over them with her head cocked. "Four of us," Keo said, when he could talk, sorting the pots on the leaf. "And two suits." He held hers out on his palm. She took it delicately, swallowed it wax and all, and went off into the oaks again.

Teva stirred her suit's share into water in the hollow of a leaf and let it run into the well under her ribs. Benal did the same. Then he did it again.

"Ben," Keo said.

"It's growing my elbow back." He licked his fingers. "I'd rather have the elbow. Possibly."

Keo pushed his own last pot across the leaf to him. Benal pushed it back. They did that twice more before Teva took it and split it three ways.

The rest went into a skin they emptied for it, a slow brown rope, until the skin was heavy.

Noli came back with a grounder in her jaws and dropped it at Keo's feet, then brought another. They cooked them on a small fire of deadwood and ate those too.

Then the grounders came to watch. Three of them sat up at the edge of the trees, small and gray, with long bare tails and bright black eyes, to see what the people were doing. They didn't seem to mind that the people were eating two of their relations, or Noli, who was asleep.

Teva tore a corner off a leaf, smeared a little honey on it from her own share, and held it out.

The nearest one came. It came all the way, a step and a stop and a step, and took the leaf in both its hand-like paws and licked it clean. Then it sat back and washed its whole face with both paws, very thoroughly, ears and all, as if it had been sent to do it.

Teva laughed.

It came out short and surprised, and she put her hand over her mouth. Benal looked at the grounder and not at her. Keo looked at the fire.

By the time the heat came down they were slow and sticky and stupid with sugar. A conifer had a fork low enough to reach and broad enough for three. Teva went up first and reached down her good hand, and Keo got his shoulder under Benal's foot.

"Up," Keo said.

"I'm going up."

"You're going sideways. Ben. Up."

Benal laughed, and the band across his chest caught the laugh and turned it into something else. "Don't. Don't make me laugh. It hurts. Possibly I'll die up here, and you'll have to explain it to everybody."

"Then you'll die fed," Keo said, and pushed.

They lay in the fork, close together.

"I'll take the first watch," Keo said.

Benal settled his arm. The dead gray patch at his elbow had a rim to it now, a thin dark line of living film creeping in at the edge. The last thing he heard was the bees, far off.
