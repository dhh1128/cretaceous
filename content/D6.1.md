The first web took Benal across the mouth.

He stopped and spat and clawed at it, and it came away in long gray strings that would not leave his fingers. Keo, who had walked straight down the game trail under it, turned round to look. Benal took two steps and walked into the next one, at the height of his eyes this time, and felt something the size of a thumbnail run across his eyebrow and away into his hair.

"They're all at your height," Keo said. "Every one. They've been waiting for you all night."

"Possibly they're waiting for everyone," Benal said, picking at his eyelashes, "and I'm the only one tall enough to be caught." He walked into a third.

He went on down the trail like that, first in line now because there was no point in anyone else going first, with his arm up in front of his face, the webs breaking across it one after another and the razor-club Keo had picked up out of the leaves knocking at his hip. It was the stupidest thing that had happened to him since the tangle. He found that he did not mind. The light had come up clean and gold, the first clean light in two days. The clouds had gone in the night, and a breeze moved in the crowns, dry on his face. Everything that had been wet since the fog steamed gently in it. They had hung the damp bindings off the backs of the packs to dry as they walked, and nobody was dripping.

The band across his chest was still there. He breathed in halves on it and walked.

There had been no breakfast again, and the hunger was a hollow place under the band. But the woodland had changed as well as the sky. The understory had thinned, and the trees were broad and pale-barked, oak and walnut, with low pines between them. Under them the ground was turned up in patches where something had been digging. It smelled of earth and green sap and pine.

The cones under the pines had opened in the dry. "Pinyons," he said, and bent, carefully, and shook one over his palm, and the nuts fell out into it, small and brown.

Keo cracked one in his teeth, ate it and went still. After that they filled a pack with them, because nuts keep and nothing else they had found did. Shelling them was slow, so they saved them and walked, and the walking was easy.

By the middle of the morning they came to the stream.

It ran clear over gravel, wide and shallow, and he could see every stone on the bottom. On a bar on the far side a croc lay in the sun.

All three of them stopped dead. Benal's whole body went cold under the suit, the way it was made to, the way every child in the Enclave was made to, and he stood there with his heart going and looked at it.

It was small: a meter and a half, possibly less, slim and young. It looked at the three of them for a long moment. Then it slid off the bar into the shallows and was gone, and nothing happened.

Nothing went on happening. The stream ran. A bird somewhere upstream said the same thing twice.

"It doesn't want us," Benal said at last. "I think. It's too small. We're too big. Possibly it's more afraid of us than we are of it, which is a thing I have never said about a croc before, and I would like it noted that I'm saying it now."

"They come in sizes," Keo said. He had his spear up still. "I've seen little ones from the trips. I've never been this close to one that didn't matter."

That was it, Benal thought. They had all been taught it. But his body had not believed a croc could look at him and let him go. His body did not know what to do with that, and went on being cold.

They did not wash where it had gone in. Upstream the gravel ran under a hand's depth of water, with nowhere for anything to be. They filled the skins there. Keo knelt and put his whole head in and came up gasping. Teva crouched at the very edge and washed her face with one hand and did not take her eyes off the water.

It was after the stream that Benal smelled it.

He had been smelling everything all morning and had stopped sorting it, the way he had learned to stop. Then, upwind, under the sap, there was resin, and something sweet in the resin. It was so faint he thought he had made it up out of wanting it. He stood with his face to the breeze until he was sure.

"Wait," he said.

They stopped. They waited. Neither of them looked tired of it.

"Honey," he said. "I think. That way."

He followed it through the oaks with his nose up, like Noli, Keo told him. It got stronger until he could hear them as well: a high close drone of a great many small wings, from a pale limb above their heads where the tree had gone hollow. At the hole there was a short spout of hardened resin, and small dark bees were going in and out of it, a few at a time.

Keo climbed. There was no question about it. Benal could not have gotten his arms over his head without the band across his chest stopping his breath, and Teva had one good arm. Keo went up the trunk on his torn hands. Benal saw the scabs split on the bark and the blood start. Keo did not slow down.

The bees came out to meet him. They did not sting; they could not. They mobbed. They went into his hair and his ears and the corners of his eyes, biting, a humming cloud around his head. He hung there one-handed, cutting at the limb with his knife and blowing them off his lips. Benal could hear every one of them.

Then Keo was passing things down, soft brown lumps the size of a plum, waxy and leaking, which Teva took in her good hand and laid on a leaf. He came down with bees still in his hair and his eyes swollen half shut, grinning.

They ate the first pots standing up. Benal bit into one, and the honey ran out over his tongue, and he had to sit down.

It was too much. Two days of snails, and then this, so sweet it was almost a pain at the hinge of his jaw. His eyes watered and his hands shook. He ate the wax as well. Keo was licking it off his split fingers. Teva had her eyes shut.

Noli had come back at the smell of it and was standing over them with her head on one side. "Four of us," Keo said, when he could talk, sorting the pots on the leaf. "And two suits." He held hers out on his flat palm, and she took it delicately and swallowed it, wax and all, and went off into the oaks again.

So they gave the suits theirs. Teva stirred her share into water in the hollow of a leaf and bent over and let it run into the well under her ribs. Benal did the same. Then he did it again. The suit would be drawing on whatever it had to grow his elbow back. His own hunger said no, and he did it anyway. Keo watched him and said nothing.

What was left went into a skin they emptied for it, a slow brown rope of it, until the skin was heavy.

When Noli came back it was with a grounder in her jaws, and she dropped it at Keo's feet. Then she brought another. They cooked them on a small fire and ate those too.

The grounders came to watch. One moment there was nobody, and the next there were three of them at the edge of the trees, small and gray, with long bare tails and bright black eyes, sitting up to see what the people were doing. They did not seem to mind that the people were eating two of their relations, or Noli, who was asleep.

Teva tore a corner off a leaf and smeared a little honey on it, from her own share, and held it out.

The nearest one came. It came all the way, a step and a stop and a step, and took the leaf in both its hand-like paws and licked it clean. Then it sat back and washed its whole face with both paws, very thoroughly, ears and all, as if it had been sent to do it.

Teva laughed.

It came out of her short and surprised, and she put her hand over her mouth afterward. Benal looked at the grounder and not at her. Keo looked at the fire. Nobody said a word.

There was a stonefruit later, high on a tree the animals had not found. They shared it. Benal knew he would be hungry again, but for the moment he did not care.

By the time the heat came down they were stupid with it, slow and sticky with sugar. There was a conifer with a fork low enough to reach, broad enough for three, and Keo and Teva got him up into it between them, one pushing and one pulling, while he laughed and told them not to make him laugh. They lay in the fork, close together.

"I'll take the first watch," Keo said.

Benal settled his arm. The dead gray patch at his elbow had a rim to it now, a thin dark line of living film creeping in at the edge. The last thing he heard was the bees, far off.
