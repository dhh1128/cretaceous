The smallest hollow had gone down again in the night, and Teva drank from the next one along. When she bent her elbows to the water, the film on them flaked off in scraps, gray as a line gone out in the Vitarium.

Back inside, the dry air pulled at her lips again. The light had no flame in it, and there was the hum.

Benal went straight through the archive to the door at the far end of the racks, found the handle under its cover and pulled, and the door came open on a lit room. He went in without looking back.

A stack of leaves lay open on the floor by the shelves, where he had sat with it at first light, before she was awake. He read everything. Teva left it there. Yesterday she had walked past these shelves for the plates, and the plates had been for Omya. She wanted to know whose these were.

She took down the stack nearest the door and sat on the floor with it. Its leaves were held together along one edge, so that they turned like fronds on a stem, and the whole of it was heavier on her knees than leaves should be. Nothing at home was written on anything meant to last. At the head of it, cut into the metal, it said *Arrival log*, and in the corner was the same kind of stamp as on the plates: *Jump plus one day*.

Keo came and sat down against the shelves beside her, with the bad foot out in front of him.

"What are you reading?"

"The day they came." She found the place with her finger. "It says where they arrived. Here."

"And then?"

"Then the ones who didn't come through."

"Read them."

She read them out slowly, as they were written. There were only a handful, each a name in two parts, and after it what that person had been sent here to do. Under them it said, *Others were nearly lost.* Then it went on to the work.

She ran her thumb along the first name. She had the names already. She could have said them over without looking, the way she said the positions.

At home everyone said the name once at the returning, the closest last. Then the balm went on and the mats went over, and by morning the mats lay flat. After that the name was nowhere. Her mother's was nowhere.

"They wrote them down," she said.

"What?"

"Their dead. They wrote them down, every one, so they'd still be somewhere."

Keo looked at the leaf a long time.

"If you could write one for Noli," she said, "what would it say?"

He was quiet so long that she thought she had got it wrong.

"That she crossed first," he said. "Before any of us. And that she waited for me."

Teva said it over to herself once, and kept it, so that it would be somewhere.

She turned on, and each leaf went over with a small tick of metal on metal. The first days were a run of short entries, and a few leaves on, between a count of the stores and a note about the water, there was a paragraph set apart from the rest.

"*Nothing we make here may outlast the impact,*" she read. "*We do not know whether a change made here might reach the time we came from. The impact is the only thing that guarantees it cannot.*"

"That's the Council," Keo said.

"That's where the Council got it."

She had heard it from the dais all her life, and it had always sounded like a thing that was so because they said it. Here it had a reason after it, written down by the people who had been given the job.

"So the Council's right," Keo said.

"About this one." What the Council said about the rest, she did not know yet.

"Did they know what they were coming to?" he said.

"It doesn't say."

"Were they scared?"

She had been wondering that herself, since the names.

"It doesn't say that either. It says what they did each day."

"What did they do?"

She turned to a leaf further on and read him one. "*Water works running. Membrane laid on the last of the roofs. First cultures started.*"

"That's it?"

"That's a day. Ours would be shorter." She thought about it. "*Walked. Ben talked.*"

He laughed through his nose, and she found she was pleased with herself.

The open stack on the floor had a heading too. She pulled it over, and it said *Personnel register*. Under the heading, the leaf was a face.

It was a man's face. It was in the metal itself, gray on paler gray, every hair of his eyebrows and every crease at the corners of his eyes, the shine on the end of his nose. It was exactly a person. She kept waiting for him to blink. The nearest thing she knew was a face in still water, and this one did not move when she did. Under it was his name, whole, and what he did. She wanted to know what his voice had been like.

Keo leaned across her to see.

"Who drew these?"

"Nobody. Nobody could draw that."

She turned the leaf. There was a woman, and then another man. Keo stayed bent over them. He could look at a face without anybody waiting for him to finish it.

"They're young," he said. "Look at him. He's younger than the Council. I always thought they'd be old."

"So did I."

She turned slowly, and looked at every face and read every name. The names at home were short and worn smooth with saying. These were long and had all their corners.

"What are you looking for?" Keo said.

"My mother's." She put her finger under the next name. "Not her. Whoever she came down from. If it's in here, it's the long way."

"And Omya's?"

"And Omya's."

"Read them out, then. I'll listen for it."

She read him the first leaves, one at a time. He listened with his eyes shut and his head back against the shelves. Twice he stopped her with "Again." She said it slower, and both times he shook his head. Every one of them could have worn down into anything.

After a dozen leaves she went on to herself, and kept turning. The founders had written down their dead. Nobody had written down her mother, and until this morning she had never thought that strange.

The light in the room never changed. She lost track of how long she had been at it until Keo shifted beside her and got his good foot under him.

"The water'll be off the reef soon," he said.

Benal came out of the far room then, with dust on his hands. He stopped behind her, reached past her shoulder and turned the register back a few leaves, to a young man whose left eyebrow was broken in two by a scar, a bare notch through it.

"Temporal lab," he said, and let the leaves fall back where she had had them.

She did not ask. He would have read every leaf in it by now.

"Ben," Keo said. "Coming?"

"I'll watch from the rocks."

Nobody went to the water alone, even here. Keo stopped in the doorway.

"Want anything brought back?"

She was hungry enough for anything he brought. "No."

He would bring her something anyway. He went, with Benal after him. She heard Keo's voice going away down the cut, and then only the hum.

She had stopped hearing it hours ago. Now it came up through the floor into her, and she sat with the register on her knees until she stopped hearing it again.

She went on to the end. After the last face there were more leaves, cut later than the rest; the stamp in their corners said so. They had no faces on them. She turned past them and went back to the faces.

Halfway back she stopped.

She had seen this one two nights ago, in the water building. The roof had been whole, the floor dry, and the light had come from overhead as it did in here. People with loads on their backs had stood toward the doorway, and the rest had stood facing them. One face among the ones with loads had held her, a woman's, turned to the people in front of her. It was this one.

So the water building had been a real day. These were the people who had been in it.

She sat with the leaf on her knees. The woman had been frightened then. Here she was only a face, looking straight out of the metal as if someone had told her to keep still for it and she was doing her best. There was nobody to call to.

Under the face was the name: *Cecilia Arroyo*. Teva said the first of it aloud, slowly, as it was spelled.

"Cecilia."

In the middle of it was the name of the woman at the center of the dais.

Sila.
