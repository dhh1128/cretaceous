The smallest hollow had gone down again in the night, and Teva drank from the next one along. When she bent to the water, the film on her elbows flaked off in scraps, gray as a line gone out in the Vitarium.

Back inside, the dry air pulled at her lips. The light had no flame in it, and there was the hum.

Benal went straight to the door at the far end of the racks, found the handle under its cover and pulled. The door opened on a lit room, and he went in without looking back.

A stack of leaves lay open on the floor by the shelves, where he had sat with it at first light, before she woke. Teva left it there. Yesterday she had walked past these shelves to the plates, for Omya. Now she wanted to know whose these were.

She took down the stack nearest the door and sat on the floor with it. Its leaves were held along one edge, so they turned like fronds on a stem, and it lay heavier on her knees than leaves should. Nothing at home was written on anything meant to last. At its head, cut into the metal, it said *Arrival log*, and in the corner was a stamp like the plates': *Jump plus one day*.

Keo sat down against the shelves beside her with his legs out.

"What're you reading?"

"The day they came." She found the place with her finger. "Where they arrived."

"And then?"

"Then the ones who didn't come through."

"Read them."

She read them out slowly. There were only a handful, each a name in two parts and what that person had been sent here to do. Under them it said, *Others were nearly lost.* Then it went on to the work.

She ran her thumb along the first name. She had them already, as she had the positions.

At home everyone said the name once at the returning, the closest last. Then the balm went on and the mats went over, and by morning the mats lay flat. After that the name was nowhere. Her mother's was nowhere.

"They wrote them down," she said.

"What?"

"Their dead. They wrote them down, every one, so they'd still be somewhere."

Keo looked at the leaf a long time.

"If you could write one for Noli," she said, "what would it say?"

He was quiet so long that she thought she had got it wrong.

"That she crossed first," he said. "Before any of us. And that she waited for me."

Teva said it over to herself once, and kept it, so that it would be somewhere.

She turned on, each leaf going over with a small tick of metal on metal. The first days were short entries, and a few leaves on, between a count of the stores and a note about the water, a paragraph stood apart from the rest.

"*Nothing we make here may outlast the impact,*" she read. "*We do not know whether a change made here might reach the time we came from. The impact is the only thing that guarantees it cannot.*"

"That's the Council," Keo said.

"That's where the Council got it."

She had heard it from the dais all her life, and it had always sounded like a thing that was so because they said it. Here it had a reason after it, written down by the people who had been given the job.

"So the Council's right," Keo said.

"About this one." What the Council said about the rest, she did not know yet.

"The Branchers say it doesn't matter," Keo said. "That the jump made a branch, and nothing here can touch theirs."

"Maybe they're right. The founders didn't know." She put her finger on the line. "*We do not know.* So they made sure."

"Were they scared?" he said.

"It doesn't say. It says what they did each day."

"What did they do?"

She turned on and read him one. "*Water works running. Membrane laid on the last of the roofs. First cultures started.*"

"That's it?"

"That's a day. Ours would be shorter. *Walked. Ben talked.*"

He laughed through his nose, and she was pleased with herself.

She pulled Benal's open stack over. It said *Personnel register*, and under the heading the leaf was a face.

A man's face, in the metal itself, gray on paler gray: every hair of his eyebrows, every crease at the corners of his eyes, the shine on the end of his nose. She kept waiting for him to blink. The nearest thing she knew was a face in still water, and this one did not move when she did. Under it was his name, whole, and what he did. She wanted to know what his voice had been like.

Keo leaned across her to see.

"Who drew these?"

"Nobody. Nobody could draw that."

She turned the leaf: a woman, then another man. Keo stayed bent over them. He could look at a face without anybody waiting for him to finish it.

"They're young," he said. "Look at him. He's younger than the Council. I always thought they'd be old."

"So did I."

She turned slowly, looking at every face, reading every name. Names at home were short and worn smooth with saying. These were long and had all their corners.

"What are you looking for?" Keo said.

"My mother's." She put her finger under the next name. "Not her. Whoever she came down from. If it's in here, it's the long way."

"And Omya's?"

"And Omya's."

"Read them out, then. I'll listen for it."

She read him the first leaves. He listened with his eyes shut and his head back against the shelves. Twice he stopped her: "Again." She said it slower, and both times he shook his head. Any of them could have worn down into anything.

After a dozen leaves she went on to herself. Nobody had written down her mother, and until this morning she had never thought that strange.

The light never changed, and she lost track of time until Keo got his feet under him.

"The water'll be off the reef soon," he said.

Benal came out of the far room then, with dust on his hands. He stopped behind her, reached past her shoulder and turned the register back a few leaves, to a young man whose left eyebrow was broken in two by a scar, a bare notch through it.

"Temporal lab," he said, and let the leaves fall back where she had had them.

She did not ask. He would have read every leaf in it by now.

"Ben," Keo said. "Coming?"

"I'll watch from the rocks."

Keo stopped in the doorway. "Want anything?"

She was hungry enough for anything he brought. "No."

He would bring her something anyway. They went. She heard Keo's voice going away down the cut, and then only the hum.

She had stopped hearing it hours ago. Now it came up through the floor into her, and she sat with the register on her knees until she stopped hearing it again.

She went on to the end. After the last face came leaves cut later than the rest, by the stamps in their corners, with no faces on them. She turned past them and went back to the faces.

Halfway back she stopped.

She had seen this one two nights ago, in the water building: the roof whole, the floor dry, the light from overhead as it was in here, people with loads on their backs toward the doorway and the rest facing them. One face among the ones with loads had held her, a woman's. It was this one.

So the water building had been a real day, and these were the people in it.

She sat with the leaf on her knees. The woman had been frightened then. Here she was only a face, looking straight out of the metal as if someone had told her to keep still for it and she was doing her best.

Under the face was the name: *Cecilia Arroyo*. Teva said the first of it aloud, slowly, as it was spelled.

"Cecilia."

In the middle of it was the name of the woman at the center of the dais.

Sila.
