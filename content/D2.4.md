Teva came up through the floor of the nest, and her grandmother looked at her and said, "You haven't slept. Your left eye droops when you haven't."

Omya was sitting upright on her mat with her back to the vine wall and her hands folded in her lap, and she was looking straight at Teva, exactly, with all of herself in it.

Teva stood with one foot still on the top rung and did not move, because she knew that look and had not seen it in days, and she was afraid that if she came the rest of the way in, the look would go.

"Well," said Omya. "Come in or go down. You're letting the heat up."

Teva came in.

It was the worst of the afternoon. The air in the nest had gone thick and still, the kind that comes before the storm, and Teva's body had been holding the heat since noon with nowhere to put it. Sweat collected at the base of her neck. She knelt behind her grandmother on the mat and began to take down the plaits, because they needed doing and because it gave her hands something to be.

Omya's hair was silver and very fine, and it had been plaited in a Master's loops every day of Teva's life. The loops had slipped. Teva worked them loose one at a time and combed them out with her fingers.

"Leave me enough to be returned with," said Omya.

Teva's fingers stopped.

It was the kind of thing Omya said. It was dry and exact and a little unkind, and there had been a time when Teva would have laughed at it. She did not laugh. She sat with her hands in her grandmother's hair and tried to see the side of her face, and could not tell whether Omya had said it because she knew Alira had been laid out for returning last night in front of half the Enclave, or because she did not.

Teva did not ask, and she did not tell her. She took up the first strand and began to plait.

"Lineages," said Omya.

So it was going to be a drill. Teva had known it would be. A lucid hour was for drilling; that was what a Master did with one, and Omya had drilled her for as long as Teva could remember.

"The blue," Teva said. "Year one. The rust-red, year one."

"And?"

"The wrapping archaea. Year three. Here."

"Here," Omya agreed. "Go on."

Teva went on, and she knew the lineages well, the way she knew the rungs of their own ladder in the dark, and she gave them back in order, every line the Vitarium kept. Omya let her run. When the list was done she said, "Protocol. Position one."

Teva gave it to her.

"Two."

She gave that too, vector into culture and culture into sequencing, and she had it to the twentieth position without a stumble, because she had said it in her sleep so many times, and then the positions started coming to her in pieces, a number without its step or a step without its number, and she lost the thread between them and found it and lost it again.

"Again," said Omya. "From fourteen."

Teva went back to fourteen.

"Precision, Teva."

"I know."

"Twenty-two."

Teva gave her twenty-two, and got it wrong.

"No. The culture first. Again."

Teva gave it back right. Her hands had not stopped moving the whole time. The first loop was done. She started on the second.

"We are scientists, not shamans," said Omya, to the vine wall. "This work is not magic. It is knowledge. And knowledge must be perfect."

Teva had heard the words so often that they usually went past her without touching. Today she heard every one of them, and kept her eyes on the plait.

"You have a better memory than your mother had," said Omya. "Lumea had the senses for it and none of the patience. She would have got to fourteen and then argued with me about fifteen."

"She did argue with you."

"She did." Omya was quiet a moment. "She was wrong, mostly. She was very good at being wrong. It's a rarer gift than you'd think."

Teva tied off the second loop and did not trust herself to say anything about her mother.

"The sky," said Omya.

Teva's hands slowed.

"It's checked every clear night," Omya said, as if Teva might have forgotten. "Every one. You start with the fields."

And then Omya recited the fields without a mistake.

She did not look up, because there was nothing above them but thatch. She did not need to. She sat with her hands folded and her eyes half closed and gave the guarded fields in the even cadence she used for protocols, one after another, every star in each and how many there were, down to the faintest. The Organs, nine <!-- @WF-watch.organs.count: 9 -->: the bright pair at the foot, the five along the bend, and the two that are hardly there, and the faintest of those sits a finger's width above the bend and you will not see it on a hazy night, so you count it anyway. Trinity, four <!-- @WF-watch.trinity.count: 4 -->: three you can see from any clearing and one between them that only good eyes ever find. She went through them field after field, and then the moon's calendar, which was the other half and the harder: which of those stars the moon walked over, in which months, and how the months crept, so that the covering of the bright one at the Organs' foot came round again only every nineteen years or so, and a Keeper who had seen it twice was old.

Teva could follow it. She could follow it a long way, much further than she had ever had any use for, all through the Organs and Trinity and the fields after them, with her lips moving under her breath on the counts the way Rano's had moved last night. She knew the faintest stars in the fields she could follow by name and by number, and had never once seen any of them. Her eyes had not taken the upgrade. She had the list and not the sky.

Somewhere past the sixth field she lost it, and let Omya go on without her.

It went on for a long time. Teva plaited, and listened, and counted what was going: the fold, and the rust-red with it, and the children in the Enclave who were overdue for their Seedings and were not going to get them. She counted it all, the way she had been counting it for three weeks, and it was a long list, and the sky was nowhere on it.

Omya came to the end and opened her eyes. Her voice had thinned over the long recitation.

"Don't let them test you again," Teva said.

Omya did not turn round. "There must be three."

That was all. Four words, and Teva heard the whole of her grandmother in them: the last Master of the Enclave, who could not stop being the last Master even with the protocol leaking out of her, who would go down that passage again and fail again in front of anyone who came to watch rather than admit there was no point. Teva's jaw set. She said nothing. She took up the last plait.

"The fold," said Omya. "One-oh-seven."

Teva's hands went still in her hair.

Omya did not wait to be answered. She began it herself, the final fold, the one Rano had been pleading for on the Vitarium floor, and she began it well. She had the first step and the second, said exactly, in the voice that had taught it to two generations of Keepers. She had the third. She reached for the fourth.

There was nothing there.

It was not a stumble. Teva had heard her stumble, on the bad days, and this was not that. It was a hand reaching into a basket and finding the bottom of it. Omya stopped. Teva felt her go still under her hands.

Then Omya lifted her hands out of her lap and looked at them.

She looked at them for a long time, turning them a little in the brown light, the backs and then the palms. Teva could see the side of her face. It was not confused. It was the face she used to wear over a culture that had failed in the night, when she had already worked out why.

She knew. Teva saw her know.

There was nobody to be angry at. Teva had come up that ladder with a day's worth of it in her, and she looked for somewhere to put it, and there was only an old woman on a mat looking at her own hands.

Omya put them back in her lap.

"It isn't ripe yet," she said. "You can't hurry a fig."

Teva closed her eyes.

The light in the vine walls had gone brown while she wasn't watching. The storm was coming on, the way it came on most afternoons, and the dark was gathering in the corners of the nest, and Teva caught herself wanting it to mean something. She did not let it. It was weather. It would have come if Omya had been sixty and sharp as a blade.

Omya lay down on her side on the mat. She did it slowly, and Teva helped her, and then sat back on her heels and looked at the last plait, half done, with the end of it loose in her fingers. She had a drill to get to. The yazhi would be at the pool by now, being loud.

She finished the plait anyway. She did it properly, the full Master's loop, tight and even, the way it was meant to be done, on a woman who might not remember, when she woke, who had done it.

Omya's eyes were nearly closed.

"Teva," she said. "Was the sky watched last night?"

Teva had been awake under it all night, at the returning ground, with her eyes on a fire. It had been overcast when Elana carried Alira out, and it had been clear at dawn, and somewhere between the two the sky had come out over her head while she looked at the fire. Who had been watching it?
