The heat drove Teva home. She came up through the floor of the nest, and her grandmother looked at her and said, "You haven't slept. Your left eye droops when you haven't."

Omya sat upright on her mat against the vine wall with her hands folded in her lap, looking straight at Teva with all of herself in it.

Teva stopped with one foot still on the top rung. She had not seen that look in days, and she was afraid that if she came the rest of the way in, it would go.

"Well," said Omya. "Come in or go down. You're letting the heat up."

Teva came in.

The air in the nest had gone thick and still ahead of the storm, and Teva had been holding the heat since noon with nowhere to put it. Sweat collected at the base of her neck. She knelt behind her grandmother and began to take down the plaits.

Omya's hair was silver and fine, and it had been plaited in a Master's loops every day of Teva's life. The loops had slipped. Teva worked them loose one at a time and combed them out with her fingers.

"Leave me enough to be returned with," said Omya.

Teva's fingers stopped.

It was the kind of thing Omya said, dry and exact and a little unkind, and once Teva would have laughed. She tried to see the side of her grandmother's face, and could not tell whether Omya had said it because she knew Alira had been laid out for returning last night in front of half the Enclave, or because she did not.

Teva did not ask, and she did not tell her. She took up the first strand and began to plait.

"Lineages," said Omya.

So it was going to be a drill. A lucid hour was for drilling.

"The blue," Teva said. "Year one. The rust-red, year one."

"And?"

"The wrapping archaea. Year three. Here."

"Here," Omya agreed. "Go on."

Teva went on, every line the Vitarium kept, in order, as she knew the rungs of their ladder in the dark. When the list was done Omya said, "Protocol. Position one."

Teva gave it to her.

"Two."

She gave that too. She had it to the fourteenth position without a stumble. Then the positions came to her in pieces, a number without its step or a step without its number, and she lost the thread between them and found it and lost it again.

"Again," said Omya. "From fourteen."

Teva went back to fourteen.

"Precision, Teva."

"I know."

"Twenty-two."

Teva gave her twenty-two, and got it wrong.

"No. The culture first. Again."

Teva gave it back right. The first loop was done. She took a small white flower from the neck of her tunic, picked at the fig rows on the way back, and tucked the stem into the loop where it would hold.

Omya's hand came up and found it. "The Explorers' boy used to bring me those," she said. "Up the ladder with them in his teeth, and he couldn't say a word till he'd spat them out."

"Keo," said Teva.

"Keo," Omya agreed. "They were weeds, mostly. I kept them anyway." She put her hand back in her lap. "Twenty-three."

Teva gave her twenty-three, and started on the second loop.

"We are scientists, not shamans," said Omya, to the vine wall. "This work is not magic. It is knowledge. And knowledge must be perfect."

Teva had heard the words so often that they usually went past her. Today she heard every one of them, and kept her eyes on the plait.

"You have a better memory than your mother had," said Omya. "Lumea had none of the patience. She would have got to fourteen and then argued with me about fifteen."

"She did argue with you."

"She did." Omya was quiet a moment. "She was wrong, mostly. She was very good at being wrong. It's a rarer gift than you'd think."

Teva tied off the second loop and did not trust herself to say anything about her mother.

"The sky," said Omya.

Teva's hands slowed.

"It's checked every clear night," Omya said, as if Teva might have forgotten. "Every one. You start with the fields."

And then Omya recited the fields without a mistake.

There was only thatch above them, and she did not need the sky. She sat with her hands folded and her eyes half closed and gave the guarded fields in the even cadence she used for protocols, one after another, every star in each and how many there were, down to the faintest. Dead Man's Journey, nine stars <!-- @WF-watch.deadmansjourney.count: 9 -->: the bright pair at the foot, the five along the bend, and the two that are hardly there, and the faintest of those sits a finger's width above the bend and you will not see it on a hazy night, so you count it anyway. Trinity, four <!-- @WF-watch.trinity.count: 4 -->: three you can see from any clearing and one between them that only good eyes ever find. She went through them field after field, and then the moon's calendar, which was the other half and the harder: which of those stars the moon walked over, in which months, and how the months crept, so that the covering of the bright one at Dead Man's Journey's foot came around again only every nineteen years or so, and a Keeper who had seen it twice was old.

Teva could follow it a long way, further than she had ever had any use for, her lips moving on the counts. She knew the faintest stars by name and by number, and had never seen one of them. Her eyes had not taken the upgrade. She had the list and not the sky.

Somewhere past the sixth field she lost it, and let Omya go on without her.

Teva plaited and counted what was going: the fold, and the rust-red with it, and the children overdue for their Seedings who were not going to get them. She had been counting it for three weeks. The sky was nowhere on the list.

Omya came to the end at last and opened her eyes. Her voice had thinned.

"Don't let them test you again," Teva said.

Omya did not turn around. "There must be three."

"There aren't three."

"Then there must be one."

Teva heard the whole of her grandmother in it: the last Master of the Enclave, who could not stop being the last Master even with the protocol leaking out of her, and would go down that passage and fail again in front of anyone who came to watch rather than admit there was no point. Teva's jaw set. She took up the last plait.

"The fold," said Omya. "One-oh-seven."

Teva's hands went still in her hair.

Omya began it herself, the final fold, the one Rano had been pleading for on the Vitarium floor, and she began it well. She had the first step and the second, said exactly, in the voice that had taught it to two generations <!-- @WF-omya.protocol.generations: 2 --> of Keepers. She had the third. She reached for the fourth.

There was nothing there.

It was not a stumble. It was a hand reaching into a basket and finding the bottom of it. Omya stopped. Teva felt her go still under her hands.

Then Omya lifted her hands out of her lap and looked at them.

She looked at them a long time, turning them a little, the backs and then the palms. Teva could see the side of her face. It was not confused. It was the face she used to wear over a culture that had failed in the night, when she had already worked out why.

She knew. Teva saw her know.

There was nobody to be angry at. Teva had come up that ladder with a day's worth of it in her. She looked for somewhere to put it, and there was only an old woman on a mat looking at her own hands.

Omya put them back in her lap.

"It isn't ripe yet," she said. "You can't hurry a fig."

"No," Teva said. "You can't."

The light in the vine walls had gone brown while she wasn't watching, and the dark gathered in the corners of the nest. Teva caught herself wanting it to mean something, and did not let it. It was weather. It would have come if Omya had been sixty and sharp as a blade.

Omya lay down on her side, slowly, with Teva's help. Teva sat back on her heels, the end of the last plait loose in her fingers. She had a drill to get to. The yazhi would be at the pool by now, being loud.

She finished the plait anyway. She did it properly, the full Master's loop, tight and even, on a woman who might not remember, when she woke, who had done it.

Omya's eyes were nearly closed.

"Teva," she said. "Was the sky watched last night?"

Teva had been awake under it all night, at the returning ground, with her eyes on a fire. It had been overcast when Elana carried Alira out and clear at dawn, and somewhere between the two the sky had come out over her head. Who had been watching it?
