Keo came out through the hide into air that had been washed cool, and the first thing he found was Teva.

He would have found her in any crowd. She stood a little way off by the big cycad at the edge of the open ground, with her grandmother on her arm. Water was still dripping off everything. Somewhere out past the groves the insects were starting up again after the rain, one and then a hundred.

A woman Keo knew by sight, one of Omya's friends, had come to take Omya home. Teva was not handing her over yet. She was straightening her grandmother's wrap, which had slipped off one shoulder, and tucking the end in so it would not slip again. Then she said something to the woman, too low for Keo to hear. The woman nodded twice. Then she took Omya's arm, and the two of them went off slowly toward the nests, the old woman's head turning to look at the dark as if it were something she was trying to place.

Teva watched them go. Keo watched Teva. A strand of her rain-wet hair had stuck to her temple. He wanted to move it and did not.

There were still people about. He could feel them without trying, the way he always could: a few by the cook pits, a pair going off between the nest trees, one standing quite still against the rock beside the hide behind him. He did not turn round.

He crossed the wet ground to her.

"They're useless," he said.

"I know."

That was all, for a while. Keo stood beside her and looked where she was looking, toward the groves and the tangle beyond them, which he could not see from here and did not need to. His parents were still on his mind. He said nothing about them.

"I'm going," said Teva.

He looked at her. "Going where?"

"Genesis."

It took him a moment to understand.

"You can't," he said. "Teva. You can't. You heard Hesh. Two hundred went out of there with rifles and suits, every one of them, and ninety-four got here. It's eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> to Genesis, through swamp and rivers. You'd need — you'd need water you could drink, and a way across the rivers, and someone who knows the ground, and you'd need to — it's not —"

It ran out on him. He had been out past the tangle on supervised trips with his parents. Once he had stood on a bank and watched flat brown water with snouts lying in it, several of them, as still as logs, and his mother had said the number under her breath. He did not say that either.

"It's not what, Keo?"

"It's suicide."

"Alone, yes," said Teva. "For them, yes." She did not raise her voice. "Not for us."

"Us?"

"We're not them." She turned and looked at him then, properly, for the first time. In the dark he could barely make out her face, only that it was turned toward him and very still. "They walked out of Genesis with nothing in them. They had suits. We have things they never had. You can hold an animal with your head. I can find north with my eyes shut. We have a chance they didn't, and they want us to sit in that cave and wait."

She could, too. When they were eight, a whole gang of them had got caught in the upper groves by a fog so thick they could not see the trees. They had all known home lay north of the fig rows, and none of them could keep to it, and the others had started to cry. Teva had stood still with her eyes closed for a while, and then she walked them home in a straight line. Keo had held the back of her shirt the whole way.

He did not have an answer. She did not have a plan; he could hear that. She had a direction, and her grandmother.

"You've been out," said Teva. "What's the worst of it?"

He did not even have to think. "The river."

"Then we cross the river."

She said it the way she might have said *then we go round the puddle*.

"And Noli," she said. "Can you get her past the Barrier?"

"Not on her own." He had been trying for a year. "With me holding her. For a while."

Teva nodded, as if that settled something.

"My grandmother is why," she said. "She's slipping away. Every day there's less of her. The rust-red is going with her, and everyone in that room knows it. I'm not waiting on the Council." Her voice did not change at all. "I'm going, Keo. With you or without you."

She meant it. He could see that she meant it, even in the dark. She would walk out through the tangle alone if she had to, with her magnetic sense and nothing else, and the river would be waiting for her with no way across it.

The fear in him did not go away. It changed. It had been fear of going, of the swamp and the snouts in the brown water and the eighty kilometers. Now it was something else, something with her in the middle of it.

She had come out here and waited for him. Out of everyone in that whole room, she had stood by this cycad in the dark until he came out, and then she had told him first. *We were made for this. You can hold an animal with your head.* She had chosen him. He was nearly sure of it.

He had agreed before he knew he had.

"You're not going alone," he said.

Something in her shoulders let go, a little.
