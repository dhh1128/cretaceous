Keo came out through the hide into air the rain had washed cool, and the first thing he found was Teva.

He would have found her in any crowd. She stood a little way off by the big cycad at the edge of the open ground, with her grandmother on her arm. Water still dripped off everything. Out past the groves the insects were starting up again, one and then a hundred.

One of Omya's friends had come to take her home. Teva was not handing her over yet. She straightened her grandmother's wrap and tucked the end in so it would not slip again. Then she murmured something to the woman. The woman nodded twice and took Omya's arm, and the two of them went off toward the nests, the old woman's head turning to the dark as if it were something she was trying to place.

Teva watched them go. Keo watched Teva. A strand of rain-wet hair had stuck to her temple. He wanted to move it and did not.

There were still people about. He could feel them without trying: a few by the cook pits, a pair going off between the nest trees, one standing quite still against the rock beside the hide behind him.

He crossed the wet ground to her.

"They're useless," he said.

"I know."

He stood beside her and looked where she was looking, toward the groves and the tangle beyond them.

"Your parents," Teva said. "Why didn't they say anything?"

"They don't, in there. My father stood up once." He heard how it was going to sound and said it anyway. "To get out of my way."

"The whole room sat. Not only them." She was still looking at the groves. "I'm going."

"Going where?"

"Genesis."

"You can't," he said. "Teva. You heard Hesh. Two hundred went with rifles and suits, and ninety-four got here. It's eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> through swamp and rivers. You'd need — you'd need water you could drink, and a way across the rivers, and someone who knows the ground, and you'd need to — it's not —"

It ran out on him.

"It's not what, Keo?"

"It's suicide."

"Alone, yes," said Teva. "For them, yes. Not for us."

"Us?"

"We're not them." She turned to him. In the dark he could make out only that her face was very still. "They had suits, and nothing in them. We have things they never had. You can hold an animal with your head. I can find north with my eyes shut. We have a chance they didn't, and they want us to sit in that cave and wait."

"I know you can," Keo said. "The fog."

When they were five, a gang of them had been caught in the upper groves by a fog so thick they lost the trees. Home lay north of the fig rows, and none of them could keep to it. The others cried. Teva stood with her eyes closed for a while, and then she walked them home in a straight line.

"You held the back of my tunic," said Teva. "The whole way."

He hadn't known she remembered.

She didn't have a plan; he could hear that. She had a direction, and her grandmother.

"You've been out," said Teva. "What's the worst of it?"

"The river. I stood on a bank once and watched it. Flat brown water, and snouts lying in it still as logs. My mother counted them under her breath."

"Then we cross the river."

She said it the way she might have said *then we go around the puddle*.

"You don't just cross it," he said. "There's one place. The narrows." Every child in the Enclave knew that much of the First Walk: the narrows, where the river ran between the stones. Nobody had ever told them what it looked like. "Miss it and you're walking a croc bank looking for it."

"Then we don't miss it," said Teva. "And Noli. Can you get her past the Barrier?"

"Not on her own." He had been trying for a year. "With me holding her. For a while."

Teva nodded, as if that settled something.

"My grandmother is why," she said. "She's slipping away. Every day there's less of her. The rust-red is going with her, and everyone in that room knows it. I'm not waiting on the Council."

"Who'll look after her?"

"Her friends will." Her voice did not change at all. "I'm going, Keo. With you or without you."

She meant it. She would walk out through the tangle alone if she had to, with her magnetic sense and nothing else, and the river would be waiting for her.

The fear in him changed. It had been fear of going, of the swamp and the snouts in the brown water and the eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->. Now it had her in the middle of it.

"I waited out here for you," she said. "You and Noli."

Out of everyone in that room, she had waited by this cycad in the dark for him, and told him first. She had chosen him. He was nearly sure of it.

He had agreed before he knew he had.

"You're not going alone," he said.

Something in her shoulders let go, a little.
