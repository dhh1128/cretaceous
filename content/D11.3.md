She kept looking up.

She did not want to. Each time, the sky was clear and blue and enormous with nothing in it at all, and each time she looked down she wanted to look back, until her neck ached with it. She made herself watch the ground instead: the fern thinning, pale shelves of rock coming up through it, the land beginning to break and tilt toward the north, where the gulches were.

Keo walked ahead of her and never looked up once. He had walked like that since dawn, steady and blank, with the fieldpack on his back and his spike in his hand. Twice she caught herself looking past him into the fern on the left, where Noli would have been ranging, and made herself stop.

If something came down out of that sky, it would come down on him, out in front with no suit and nothing between him and it. She had known that since the tangle and let him walk. Behind her, Ben's breath caught now and then, on a chest that still hurt him. She was the one still standing, and she was afraid of it: of being the last one up when the other two went down.

The shadow went over them without a sound.

It crossed the fern ahead of Keo, and Keo, and her, so big and so fast that it was gone before her eyes had understood it was a shadow. She looked up into an empty sky. There was only the sun, high and white, too bright to look into.

She was on the ground. Benal lay flat beside her with his face in the fern. Keo was on his knees, staring up into the sun with his mouth open, and she reached out and pulled him down by his tunic.

Nothing came.

When her legs would work, she said, "The rocks."

They went bent low, off the open fern and down into the broken ground, where the gulches cut through the limestone and there was a wall to put your back to. The gulch they came into had water in its floor that had not been there long, a thin brown sheet of it sliding over the stones from the west. Out that way the dark line she had seen at dawn had become a wall, and under it, on the high ground, it was already raining.

Across the gully floor, between them and the rocks, the litter was moving.

She thought at first it was the water. It was ants. They poured up out of the bank where the water had got into it, small and dark and so many that the leaves looked as though they were boiling, over each other and over everything, spreading. One came up the shaft of her spike faster than she could believe, and the sting on her hand was a small hot needle.

"The water's got into their nest," Benal said. He was keeping well back. "They're coming out of the bank. There must be thousands. More than thousands, possibly, if it's an old—"

"Around," Teva said.

They went around by the open lip of the gully, along the top, with the sky over them again.

Benal stopped on the lip. He looked down at the ants, and up at the sky, and down at the ants.

"Sky or ground," he said. "Pick your death."

"Shut up, Benal."

It was out of her before she could stop it, and something went through her with it, dark and quick, that was very nearly a laugh. Ahead of them Keo snorted. It was the first sound he had made all morning that was not an answer to something.

"The ants have the better odds," Teva said. "Possibly."

Benal looked at her. Then he went on.

The front came over while they were still on the lip. The light went first, all at once, the blue gone gray from one side of the sky to the other. Then the rain came, warm and hard and everywhere, roaring on the rock.

She stopped where she was and put her face up into it.

It ran into her eyes and her mouth and down her neck, warm as the air, and the heat that had been sitting in her since yesterday went out of her into it. She had not known how much of it was still there until it left. It was the best thing that had happened to her since the tangle.

A little further along, on their own side of the gulch, a long shelf of rock jutted from the high wall, and the rain was already pouring off its edge in a sheet. They drank from it with their heads back, all they wanted. Then Benal fetched all four skins, and they filled them from the falling sheet and took their time.

They got down to it along the wall on their own side, well away from the ants, and in under the shelf, and sat with their backs to the rock. The rain came down in front of them so thick that the far side of the gulch was gone. This was worse ground than the fern, all drop and scree and water going where it liked, and the ants were down there somewhere in it.

The sky had closed over gray and low from edge to edge. Anything could be up there now, above it, going over, and there would be no shadow to see.

She looked up anyway.
