She kept looking up.

Each time the sky was clear and enormous with nothing in it, and each time she looked down she wanted to look back. She made herself watch the ground instead: the fern thinning, pale shelves of rock coming up through it, the land beginning to break toward the gulches.

Keo walked ahead of her, steady and blank, and never looked up once. Twice she caught herself looking past him into the fern on the left, where Noli would have been ranging.

If something came down out of that sky, it would come down on him, out in front with no suit. She had known that since the tangle and let him walk. Behind her, Ben's breath caught now and then, on a chest that still hurt him. She was the one still standing, and she was afraid of it: of being the last one up when the other two went down.

A shadow crossed the fern ahead of Keo, and Keo, and her, so fast it was gone before she understood it. She looked up into an empty sky and the sun.

She was on the ground. Benal lay flat beside her with his face in the fern. Keo was on his knees, staring up into the sun with his mouth open, and she reached out and pulled him down by his tunic.

Nothing came.

When her legs would work, she said, "The rocks."

They went bent low into the broken ground, where the gulches cut through the limestone and there was a wall to put your back to. A thin brown sheet of new water was sliding over the stones of the gulch floor from the west, where the dark line she had seen at dawn had become a wall. Under it, on the high ground, it was already raining.

Across the gully floor, between them and the rocks, the litter was moving. It was ants. They poured up out of the bank, small and dark, so many that the leaves seemed to boil. One came up the shaft of her spike faster than she could believe, and the sting on her hand was a small hot needle.

"The water's got into their nest," Benal said. He was keeping well back. "They're coming out of the bank. There must be thousands. More than thousands, possibly, if it's an old—"

"Around," Teva said.

They went around by the lip, under the open sky again.

Benal stopped on the lip. He looked down at the ants, and up at the sky, and down at the ants.

"Sky or ground," he said. "Pick your death."

"Shut up, Benal."

It was out of her before she could stop it, with something dark and quick that was very nearly a laugh. Ahead of them Keo snorted. It was the first sound he had made all morning that was not an answer to something.

"The ants have the better odds," Teva said. "Possibly."

Benal looked at her. Then he went on.

The front came over while they were still on the lip. The blue went gray from one side of the sky to the other, and then the rain came, warm and hard, roaring on the rock.

She stopped where she was and put her face up into it.

It ran into her eyes and her mouth, and the heat that had been sitting in her since yesterday went out of her into it. It was the best thing that had happened to her since the tangle.

A little further along, a long shelf of rock jutted from the high wall, and the rain poured off its edge in a sheet. They drank from it with their heads back, all they wanted, and Benal filled all four skins.

They got in under the shelf along the wall, well away from the ants, and sat with their backs to the rock. The rain came down so thick that the far side of the gulch was gone. This was worse ground than the fern, all drop and scree and water going where it liked, and the ants were down there somewhere in it.

Anything could be up there now, above the cloud, and there would be no shadow to see.

She looked up anyway.
