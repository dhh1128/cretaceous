The sun climbed, and the heat came up off the fern. Teva looked up again.

The sky was blue and empty overhead. In the west the thread of cloud had thickened into a wall, the color of old ash at the bottom and white where the sun caught it. She made herself look down at the fern and at Keo's back in front of her. Twenty steps later she was looking up again.

All her life, flybeaks had been things that scratched on the thatch at night. You heard one go over and did not lift your head. Now there was a place between her shoulders where it would hit. She could feel it with every step.

Keo walked with the fieldpack and his spike. He had not looked up once. The back of his neck was burned where the tunic ended. Twice she caught herself watching the fronds ahead of him for the ripple that would be Noli.

"It's coming faster than I said," Benal said behind her. "An hour. Possibly less."

"Good."

A shadow came across the fern from the left. It was huge. It went over Keo and over her without a sound, and was gone. She looked up into the sun and saw only glare.

"Down."

She was flat in the fern before the word was out, with stems against her cheek and the roll of her suit pressing into her back. Keo lay a body's length ahead with his face turned sideways and his eyes open.

Nothing came.

"The rocks. Go."

They ran bent over. The fern thinned over stone and gave out on scree that slid under her boots. A gulch opened in front of them, cut into the limestone, and they climbed down into it. From the bottom the sky was a strip. She breathed.

A little way on, the floor was moving. It was a sheet of ants from one wall to the other, pouring over the litter and the stones and each other, and through the middle of it a skim of muddy water was coming down.

"It's the rain up there," Benal said, and pointed west at the cloud. "It's coming down the gulch, and it's flooded them out."

"Can we go through?"

"I could, in the suit. Keo couldn't. They sting."

She looked at the lip of the gulch, where the rock stood bare in the sun, with nothing over it.

"Then we go round."

Benal looked up at the sky, and down at the ants, and up again.

"Sky or ground," he said. "Pick your death."

"Shut up, Benal."

It was out before she could stop it, and something went through her with it, dark and quick, that was very nearly a laugh.

Keo snorted. It was the first sound she had heard out of him since they left the camp.

"Ground," she said. "Better odds. Possibly."

Benal opened his mouth, and shut it again. The ants went on pouring over the stones.

They climbed out and went round by the lip. She kept her eyes on the stone under her feet and did not look up, and looked up.

The front caught them on the lip. Its shadow ran across the broken country toward them, and then the sun was gone and the rain came down.

It was warm, and it came so hard she could not see the far wall. She stopped and put her face up into it. It ran into her eyes and her mouth and down inside her collar. Since yesterday noon she had been hot all the way through. Now she was not. Nothing so good had happened to her since the tangle.

Farther along, a shelf of rock stood out from the wall below the lip, and the water sheeted off its edge. They climbed down to it and drank with their mouths open, as much as they wanted, and then more.

"Skins," she said.

They held all four under the edge. It took a long time to fill them. Nobody minded. Benal stood with his skin held up and his eyes closed. Keo held his out in both hands and watched the water go into it.

"We lie up here," she said.

They got in under the shelf. It was dry at the back, barely, and it smelled of wet stone. Below them the gulch was running brown from wall to wall.

She sat at the edge with the drip in front of her face. Back up the slope was the fern, where she had been able to see the sky from one side to the other. They had left it for this: rock, and floodwater, and a gray that came down to meet it.

The thing that had made that shadow was up in it somewhere. She could not see it now, and she watched the gray anyway.
