Outside, the day's heat still hung in the air. Teva stood in it with the chamber's chill under her suit and listened for the surf. There was none. In its place was the insect wall, and under that the frogs in the flooded ring, a crowd of them, croaking over one another.

The sky was clear to every edge, with the moon past the half in the west. She went up the rock to keep the Watch.

It was bare limestone over the cave, and nothing rooted in it grew tall enough to come between her and the sky. She kept well back from the hole.

Omya was there.

She sat on the rock where the Keepers sat, straight-backed, with her face turned up. One side of her hair was plaited and the other hung loose. A little way off sat one of Omya's friends, the woman who had taken her home the night they left. She saw Teva and got up, and went past her down toward the cleft.

Teva stopped where she was.

Then she went and sat down behind her grandmother, with Omya between her knees.

The plait that was done was tight at the scalp and slack at the end, in loops that were not the Master's. Teva worked it loose a strand at a time and combed it out with her fingers. Omya's hair was fine and light, and there was not much of it. Her fingers knew the loops, and she began again at the crown. A flake of the gray suit came off her sleeve and lay on Omya's shoulder. She brushed it away.

When the first loop was done she unzipped the pocket on her chest and took out the flower. It was dry and pressed thin. She tucked its stem into the loop, where it would hold.

She looked up at the stars.

"The Organs," she said, low. "The bright pair at the foot. Five along the bend. Two that are hardly there. Nine."

She had never seen the two that were hardly there. Omya leaned back against her knees.

The moon stood close by one of the stars the table named. Somebody ought to lay tonight against the table written on the metal plate, she thought. Her hands went on in Omya's hair.

"I miss Lumea," she said.

Omya did not respond.

"Trinity, four."

Omya's head came round a little under her hands.

"Teva. Was the sky watched last night?"

Teva's hands slowed. Last night she had been on the crown at Genesis, believing every star of the Watch a lie. She had looked up, and her eyes had gone round the fields one after another before she knew they were doing it.

"Yes," she said.

Omya's hand came up and found hers in her hair, and stayed there. It was dry, and warmer than hers. Teva's breath caught, once.

The fourth step that Omya had reached for and not found was in the Vitarium now, on the founders' plates and under Rano's hands, and no child would go overdue for want of it. Omya was not better. What her losing it had cost was undone.

After a while Omya's hand went back to her lap, and Teva went on with the plait.

Benal came up the rock alone, a long shape against the stars, and folded himself down beside her.

"What are you counting?" he said, keeping it low.

"Trinity."

He tipped his head back. "I can see three."

"Four."

"Where?"

"Between them. Only good eyes find it. I've never seen it."

"Then how do you—"

"You count it anyway."

Benal looked at the dark place between the three for a long time.

"Four," he said.

Keo came up last, with a water skin. He went to Omya first and knelt in front of her.

"Drink some, Omya. Here."

He held the skin for her while she drank. Then he shifted on his knees and held it out to Teva.

"Teva."

She drank and gave it back, and he reached it across her.

"Ben."

He stayed on his knees, looking at Omya's hair.

"Somebody's brought you a flower."

Omya's hand went up and touched it. "The Explorers' boy brings them."

Keo looked at it a moment longer. "He does," he said.

Teva started the second loop.

Keo sat down on her free side, so that she had one of them at each shoulder, and leaned back on his hands with his face to the sky.

"Is it up there now?"

"Probably," Benal said. "It's too far out and too faint to see. When it's close enough, it'll show in one of the fields first."

"How much warning will that give us?"

"Not long."

"You said the good news was we'd see it eventually."

"Eventually got shorter."

"Not tonight," Teva said. "There's nothing new in the ones I can see."

Keo looked at the sky, and then across her at Benal.

"Ben. Did you make your father sit down and look at it?"

"The plate's in the wig. In the river."

"You were going to stand in his doorway for days."

"He already listened. And then he did the talking."

"Mine did the talking too," Keo said. "Two words." He set his face still and said, in his mother's voice, "So efficient." He snorted, but it was gentle, not bitter.

"Sila's sitting again tomorrow," Benal said. "She'll want the wig out of the river and gone. And us with it, if she can manage it."

"Tomorrow," Keo said. "Tonight my parents are home."

Teva's fingers were on the last loop.

"I did hers," she said.

Keo snorted again and put a hand over his mouth, for Omya. Benal's shoulder shook against hers.

She finished the loop and left her hands where they were. Above the rock the fields hung where they had always hung, with nothing in them that had not been there for two hundred years.

"Somebody has to be looking," she said. "Even when there's nothing up there. Because there's nothing, that's what—"

She stopped.

Beside her Benal tipped his head back, and on her other side Keo did the same.

Later they lay back on the rock. It still held the sun, and the heat came up into her back. Omya came down with her and settled against her side, with her head on Teva's arm.

The last time the three of them had had nothing to do under a clear sky but look at it was on the savanna, the night Keo found every star she named. There had been someone else with them then, lying in the fern against the outside of the thorns. Nobody said her name.

Omya was asleep. Her breath came slow against Teva's arm, and the flower was a pale spot in her hair. The three of them lay side by side on the rock, looking up.

"Name one," Keo said. "I'll find them."

"The Organs. Start at the bright pair."

"Got it."

"Show me," Benal said.
