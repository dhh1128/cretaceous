Outside, the day's heat still hung in the air. Teva stood in it with the chamber's chill under her suit and listened for the surf. Instead came the insect wall, and under it the frogs in the flooded ring, croaking over one another.

The sky was clear to every edge, with the moon past the half in the west. She went up the rock to keep the Watch.

Nothing rooted in the bare limestone over the cave grew tall enough to come between her and the sky. She kept well back from the hole.

Omya was there.

She sat on the rock where the Keepers sat, straight-backed, with her face turned up. One side of her hair was plaited and the other hung loose. A little way off sat the friend who had taken Omya home the night they left. She saw Teva, got up, and went past her down toward the cleft.

Teva stopped where she was.

Then she went and sat down behind her grandmother, with Omya between her knees.

The finished plait was tight at the scalp and slack at the end, in loops that were not the Master's. Teva worked it loose a strand at a time and combed it out with her fingers. Omya's hair was fine and light, and there was not much of it. She began again at the crown, in the loops her fingers knew. A flake of the gray suit came off her sleeve onto Omya's shoulder. She brushed it away.

When the first loop was done she unzipped the pocket on her chest and took out the flower, dry and pressed thin, and tucked its stem into the loop.

She looked up at the stars.

"Dead Man's Journey," she said, low. "The bright pair at the foot. Five along the bend. Two that are hardly there. Nine."

She had never seen the two that were hardly there. Omya leaned back against her knees.

The moon stood close by one of the stars the table named. Somebody ought to lay tonight against the founders' table, she thought. Her hands went on in Omya's hair.

"I miss Lumea," she said.

Omya did not respond.

"Trinity, four."

Omya's head came round a little under her hands.

"Teva. Was the sky watched last night?"

Teva's hands slowed. Last night she had been on the crown at Genesis, believing every star of the Watch a lie. She had looked up, and her eyes had gone round the fields before she knew it.

"Yes," she said.

Omya's hand came up and found hers in her hair, and stayed there. It was dry, and warmer than hers. Teva's breath caught, once. The fourth step was in the Vitarium now, under Rano's hands. Omya was not better.

After a while Omya's hand went back to her lap, and Teva went on with the plait.

Benal came up the rock alone, a long shape against the stars, and folded himself down beside her.

"What are you counting?" he whispered.

"Trinity."

He tipped his head back. "I can see three."

"Four."

"Where?"

"Between them. Only good eyes find it. I've never seen it."

"Then how do you—"

"You count it anyway."

Benal looked at the dark place between the three for a long time.

"Four," he said.

Keo came up last, with a water skin. He went to Omya first and knelt in front of her.

"Drink some, Omya. Here."

He held the skin for her while she drank, then shifted on his knees and held it out to Teva.

"Teva."

She drank and gave it back, and he reached it across her.

"Ben."

He stayed on his knees, looking at Omya's hair.

"Somebody's brought you a flower."

Omya's hand went up and touched it. "The Explorers' boy brings them."

Keo looked at it a moment longer. "He does," he said.

Teva started the second loop.

Keo sat down on her other side, so that she had one of them at each shoulder, and leaned back on his hands, face to the sky.

"Is it up there now?"

"Probably," Benal said. "Too far out and too faint to see. When it's close enough, it'll show in one of the fields first."

"How much warning will that give us?"

"Not long."

"You said the good news was we'd see it eventually."

"Eventually got shorter."

"Not tonight," Teva said. "There's nothing new in the ones I can see."

Keo looked at the sky, and then across her at Benal.

"Ben. Did you make your father sit down and look at it?"

"The plate's in the wig. In the river."

"You were going to stand in his doorway for days."

"He already listened. And then he did the talking."

"Mine did the talking too," Keo said. "Two words." He set his face still and said, in his mother's voice, "So efficient." He snorted, but it was gentle, not bitter.

Teva kept her eyes on the plait. "The thing you dug up on the crown."

"It's in my nest." Benal was quiet a moment. "A man buried it. I saw him do it, the night before we flew, in one of the flashes. Elias Sandoval. He's in the register, Teva. The notch in his eyebrow."

She remembered the notch.

"He was old," Benal said. "And there was no Genesis behind him. No arches. Just the rock."

Keo turned his head. "Before the founders?"

"Before. He went back with the others, and he got old there, and he put it in the ground. It says *for whoever comes back* on the lid."

"That's us," Keo said.

"Possibly."

"You didn't open it," Teva said.

"The lid won't turn. If I pry it, whatever's inside might break." He let out a breath. "I'll grease the seam and let it soak a few days. Then wedge it in a fork and get a cord round the lid." He looked up. "Not tonight."

"Sila's sitting again tomorrow," Benal said. "She'll want the wig out of the river and gone. And us with it, if she can manage it."

"Tomorrow," Keo said. "Tonight my parents are home."

Teva's fingers were on the last loop.

"I did hers," she said.

Keo snorted again and put a hand over his mouth, for Omya. Benal's shoulder shook against hers.

She finished the loop and left her hands where they were. Above the rock the fields hung where they had always hung, with nothing in them she could see that had not been there for two hundred years.

"Somebody has to be looking," she said. "Even when there's nothing up there. Because there's nothing, that's what—"

She stopped.

Beside her Benal tipped his head back, and on her other side Keo did the same.

Later they lay back on the rock. It still held the sun, and the heat came up into her back. Omya came down with her and settled against her side, with her head on Teva's arm.

The last time the three of them had had nothing to do under a clear sky but look at it was on the savanna, the night Keo found every star she named. There had been someone else with them then, lying in the fern against the outside of the thorns. Nobody said her name.

Omya was asleep. Her breath came slow against Teva's arm, and the flower was a pale spot in her hair. The three of them lay side by side on the rock, looking up.

"Find me the fourth one in Trinity," she said. "I've never seen it."

Keo lay still a while, looking.

"Show me where to start," he said. "Ben can count. We'll find them together. All of them."
