Keo's arms still had the weight of the plate in them, though he had put it back in its slot an hour ago. *We can't carry all of that.* It was true, and it was the only thing he had said all afternoon that either of them needed. Since then he had stood about. At the far end of the outer rooms the doorway had gone from gold to red.

"I'm going to have a look round," he said.

"Mm," Benal said. He was at the dark door again, with his face close to its panel.

Teva did not look up from the plates. "Not near the water."

"Not near the water."

He drank at the hollows on the crown in the last of the light, on his knees, a long drink. The water in the smallest stood further below its dark line than it had this morning. When he stood up, the fin was there, beyond the end of the banked row and down past the crown's far shoulder, standing up out of the shallows against the evening. It had been a thing none of them could name this morning, and it still was. He went along the crown toward it at the ankle's pace, and the hum fell away behind him.

Round the shoulder the slope came down again toward the lagoon, and on a shelf of it there was a floor. It was flat and gray and poured, like the plant house's, and as big as the ground under three nest trees. It was concrete. He had been taught the word with the law, as the stuff that lasts. Here it was under his feet, and it had lasted. Small things went off it sideways into the cracks ahead of him. His sense did not feel them at all; there was no more mind in them than in a snail. It found the birds settling along the crown above him, and far out, faint, the fish in the lagoon.

From the edge of the concrete a ramp ran down into the water. Off its foot the fin stood up, and it was the tail of a boat. The boat sat level in the shallows with its back just out of the water, wide and flat and crusted white, and on either side of it, low down, stuck out short wings like a fish's fins. The still air brought the smell of it up the ramp: salt, and weed, and under them metal the sea had been working at since before there was an Enclave.

He kept back from the top of the ramp.

At the back of the concrete stood two mounds banked to the roof like the ones on the crown, a short one and a long high one. The long one's end faced the ramp. It had a pair of tall doors in it, and beside them a flap of metal like the one by the door into the crown.

This morning he had pulled one because Benal told him to. This one he pulled without asking anyone. The handle came down stiff and then all at once, and the seal broke with a breath of warm air. He leaned his weight on one door and it swung out, then the other. Strips along the roof came on as they opened, one after another, all the way to the back.

Under them, on a frame on wheels, sat a second boat with wings. This one was dry and whole. Its tail stood up near the roof. He walked round it slowly. Along the roots of the wings and down the middle of its back there were round places, as wide as his arms could go round, with nothing in them. A cord as thick as his thumb came out of the wall the long mound shared with the short one and went into the boat's side. There was a step up to a door in its side, and the door opened when he pulled it.

Inside were four seats, two and two, behind a curved clear sheet he could see straight through, and in front of the first two a dark panel. He sat in the front seat. It gave under him, and went on giving, all the way through. Nothing at home was soft all the way through.

"You'd have hated this, Noli," he said. "You'd have had it torn open by now, to see what was in it."

The panel lit.

"Pilot detected," said a voice. "Please identify yourself as pilot."

Words ran across the panel as it spoke, faster than he could have read them even on a good day. He let them go and listened.

"Who's that?"

"Please identify yourself as pilot."

There was no face anywhere, and nothing in the air. The suits at home told you things all day long, on a shield and in a glow at the forearm, and in his whole life he had never heard one say a word. He reached for whoever was speaking without meaning to, the way he would have reached for Noli in the dark. There was nobody. The hull was as empty to his sense as the concrete.

It waited. He could not tell what for.

"Keo," he said.

"Keo. State your rating."

"My what?"

"Rating not recognized. Logging you as unqualified operator. Maintenance access only. A qualified pilot is preferred."

"There isn't one."

The voice was level. Its sentences ended on the same note they began on, and it never stopped to breathe.

"What are you?" he said. "Where are you?"

"I am an artificial intelligence. I am this craft's maintenance and flight-training system."

"Who's talking, though? Who's in there?"

"There is nobody in the craft. I am not a person."

"Then how are you talking?"

"I was built to talk. The mission's" — a word went missing out of the middle — "team designed me to recognize speech and to reply."

"That's who. I asked how."

"I do not understand the question."

He sat back in the soft seat. "Can you hear what I'm thinking?"

"I do not understand the question."

It was the first thing in two days that had answered him about itself. He waited, and it did not ask him anything back. It was the voice, then. Whatever it said it was, it was the voice.

"Can you fly?" he said. "This. Can it fly?"

"This craft cannot fly. Main cell bank: removed for storage. Reserve: empty. Surface power membrane: not responding. Lift units: fitted. Current supply: station feed, through the charge lead."

He looked out of the door at the wing.

"There's nothing in the round places," he said. "On the wings, and along the back. They're empty."

"The lift unit mounts. I have no record of their removal. Logging a sensor fault."

"And the other boat with wings. The one out in the water."

"Craft two is moored."

"It's sunk. Its tail's sticking up out of the lagoon."

"Logged."

It had been wrong twice now, and it said so in the same voice it was right in.

"When did you last fly?"

"Last flight: river survey, inland from the coast." It gave a name for the pilot, a long founder name, and he had lost the front of it before it reached the end. "After landing, this craft was powered down for storage. Days since last flight —" and a number, thousands on thousands of them.

"Say it again."

It said it again, exactly the same. He could not hold that one either.

"That's…" he said, and left it.

He looked down the hull at the empty places and the cord in its side.

"What would it take? To fly."

"Power. The main cells are stored dry in the store beside this hangar. The electrolyte is sealed in drums. Carry the cells to the bay dry. Fill them in the bay. Link them in series. Charge from the station."

"How long?"

"Full charge, about one day." Then, after a moment: "Measuring station supply. Revised. The station's generators are at about half their rating. A full charge will take two to three days <!-- @WF-wig.charge.days: 2-3 -->. Dimming hangar lights to spare the supply."

The strips along the roof went down to a glow.

"And then how far does it go?"

"On a full charge, about one hundred and twenty kilometers <!-- @WF-wig.range.claimed: 120km -->."

He sat still.

"That's farther than home," he said.

"Destination not recognized."

The short mound's end door had the same flap beside it. He pulled that handle too. Inside, the light was flat and white and the hum was louder. There were racks of gray blocks, row on row, each with two short posts and a cap on top. A row of drums stood against one wall. Along the back lay long boxes, sealed. He looked at them and left them, and the voice said nothing about them. By the door stood a tall metal cupboard, which came open stiffly.

"There's a cupboard in here," he called.

"Kit locker."

He carried what was in it out to the boat and held each thing up at its door, and the voice named them one at a time.

"Goggles. Wear them when filling."

"Gloves."

"Measuring jug. Funnel."

"Torque wrench."

The last was a long metal tool with a jaw at one end. He put it all down by the boat's side.

Then he carried the blocks. He took them one at a time off the rack, held each against his chest, and went out of the store's door, a few paces along the concrete and in at the long mound's doors to the boat's side. Each was heavier than it looked, a dead weight that wanted to fall away from him, and the rib found every lift. The ankle found every step. After the first few he set one down by the bay and straightened. With a cap on top for filling, each one was a jar to him.

"Where do the jars go?"

"Cells. The cells go in the bay, in the floor of the hull."

"Cells," he said, and went for the next jar.

"Turning on the bay lights."

He had lifted the lid off a long trough in the boat's floor. Now lights came on low along it. He went to and fro. The moon had been a thin curve in the west when he started. It went down while he was carrying. After that there was starlight on the concrete between the two doors, and the light from each. All day he had gone up every slope with a hand on Benal's shoulder. Nobody's shoulder was under his hand now. Seventy-odd times he went to the rack and back. His hands were doing a thing that needed doing, and nobody else could do it.

He put the gloves on to fill them, and the first time he closed his hand the fingers split across the knuckles like old bark. He pulled them off. The goggles held.

"Fill each cell to the line."

He tipped a drum into the jug, and the jug through the funnel into the first jar, slowly, until the voice read him the line. What was in the drums was clear and smelled of nothing. A drop ran down the funnel onto his thumb, and he rubbed it against his finger. It was slick as a snail's track. Then it bit, and the bite grew after he had wiped it on his tunic.

"It bites," he said.

"Electrolyte on skin. Wash it off at once with plenty of water."

"With what?"

"With plenty of water."

"There isn't plenty of water. There's the hollows, and that's what we drink. And there's the lagoon, and it's dark."

"Wash it off at once with plenty of water."

He wiped his hands on his tunic and went slower.

"It's full right up," he said, at the next one.

"Not understood."

He thought about it. "The level is at the line."

"Confirmed. Next cell."

So he learned to say things its way, the long way, as the founders must have talked to it. It answered better when he did. He went on filling, and somewhere in the middle of the row he began to talk to it about other things.

"I had a sicklefoot," he said. "Noli. She ate crawlers out of my hand, and she'd steal them out of the beds the moment I let go of her. She thought I didn't know."

"I do not understand. Confirm fill level, cell thirty-four."

"At the line." He moved the funnel along. "When she was pleased she'd put her head against my chest. Hard. And leave it there."

"Confirm fill level, cell thirty-five."

He told it anyway, the rest of it, while the jug went up and down.

Between jars he asked it things. It had been places he had not.

"Where is everybody? The people who flew you."

"I do not know. This craft was powered down after its last flight. You are the first voice since."

"Why did they come here? The founders."

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"What were they for, then? What was all of it for?"

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"If I was mission personnel, what would you tell me?"

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

It said it the same every time. He could have asked it all night and got the same.

"What did you do, then? You. Not them."

"Survey. Craft of this class flew survey from the station, inland. Survey objective: fresh water."

"And the station? What was it?"

"The station was a laboratory. The station roster lists the personnel who worked there. Laboratory programs are restricted."

"What used to make you go? When nobody was charging you."

"A power membrane covered this craft and the station roofs. A living skin, grown to make power from light. The membrane is not responding."

He looked up at the boat's back, at the dull gray of it, and tried to see it alive.

"The river you flew last," he said. "Where is it? Which way?"

"Specify a reference point."

"From here. From…" He did not know how to give it the coast, or the bank, or the narrows, in any words it would take. "Never mind."

Later, when the last jar was full, he said, "Thank you."

It did not answer. There was nothing in it to answer.

"Link cell one positive to cell two negative. Lay the strap from post to post. Secure each end."

The straps lay in a tray at the end of the bay, strips of metal with a hole at each end, and with them the things that held them.

"Secure it with what?" he said. "There's round things. Flat, with a hole in the middle. And long straight things, like a pin, with grooves running round them."

"Nuts and bolts. The bolt goes through the strap into the post. The nut goes on the bolt."

"A nut's what you eat."

"I do not understand."

"Never mind. Nut."

"Tighten with the torque wrench until it clicks. Confirm."

He fitted the jaw of the tool over the nut and turned it until it clicked in his hand.

"Cell one, positive," he said. "The nut's tight. It clicked."

"Confirmed."

It read him every link after that, and had him say each one back. Plus of one to minus of the next, strap, bolt, nut, click. His fingers had blistered where the drops had been, and every turn of the tool found the blisters.

"On charge, leave the bay open, and the hangar doors. The cells give off gas when charging. It must not gather. No open flame."

"There isn't a flame on this whole island," Keo said. "Our kindler went down with the fieldpack."

"Not understood. No open flame."

It was well past the middle of the night when the last nut clicked.

"Last link confirmed. Close the isolator."

"The what?"

"The isolator is the lever on the wall beside the bay. Move it to closed."

He found it. "Which way's closed?"

"Down."

He put his hand on it and pulled it down.

On the panel a number came up and stayed where it was.

"Isolator closed. Charging. The first cycles form the cells after storage. Charge level will not rise tonight."

"Then how do I know it's doing anything?"

"Charging."

He stood by the lever with his burned hands hanging. He knew what the Council would call this. Nobody had voted on it, and nobody had been asked. He had done it by himself, on his own say, in the dark.

He left the doors open, as it had told him. He walked back along the crown by starlight, and when he could not see the way he went by the hum, which grew as he came, to the door into the crown. The night was warm, and he was cold. In the dry room the other two were asleep against the wall.

He lay down by them. Teva stirred.

"Where were you?" she said.

"Looking round."

She was asleep again before he could have said anything more. His hands burned. It was not anything yet. When it was, he would tell them.
