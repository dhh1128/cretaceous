*We can't carry all of that.* It was the only thing he had said all afternoon that either of them needed. At the far end of the outer rooms the doorway had gone from gold to red.

"I'm going to have a look round," he said.

"Mm," Benal said, bent to the dark door's panel.

Teva didn't look up from the plates. "Not near the water."

"Not near the water."

He drank at the hollows in the last of the light, on his knees. The water in the smallest had sunk farther below its dark line since morning. When he stood, the fin was there, beyond the end of the banked row and down past the crown's far shoulder, jutting out of the shallows against the evening. He went along the crown toward it, limping less than he had that morning, and the hum fell away behind him.

Round the shoulder the slope came down again toward the water inside the reef, and on a shelf of it lay a floor poured in one piece, as big as the ground under three nest trees. It was concrete, like the water building, and it had lasted. Small things rattled sideways off it into the cracks; his sense did not feel them. It found the birds settling along the crown above him and, far out and faint, the fish in the still water.

From the edge of the concrete a ramp ran down into the water. Off its foot the fin was the tail of a boat sitting level in the shallows, its back just out of the water, wide and crusted white, and on either side of it a darker shape under the water, short and spread like a fish's fins.

He kept back from the top of the ramp.

At the back of the concrete stood two mounds banked to the roof like the ones on the crown, a short one and a long high one. In the long one's end, facing the ramp, was a pair of tall doors, and beside them a metal flap like the one by the door into the crown.

This morning he had pulled one because Benal told him to. This one he pulled without asking anyone. The handle came down stiff and then all at once, and something in the doors gave with a dull knock. They swung out under his weight. Strips along the roof lit one after another as they opened.

Under them, on a frame on wheels, sat a second boat with wings, dry and whole, its tail up near the roof. Along the roots of the wings and down its back were round places as wide as his arms could circle, empty. A cord as thick as his thumb ran out of the wall the long mound shared with the short one and into the boat's side. He climbed the step and pulled the door open.

Inside were four seats, two and two, behind a curved clear sheet, and in front of the first two a dark panel. He sat in the front seat. Its cover had gone stiff and cracked, and what was under it crunched to grit under his weight. Under that it was hard.

"Noli, you'd have had this torn open by now," he said. "Just to see what was in it."

The panel lit.

"Pilot detected," said a voice. "Please identify yourself as pilot."

Keo was out of the seat, his back against the door frame and the tooth-knife in his hand. His heart slammed against the rib. He reached with his sense for whoever was in here, and there was nobody. There was a voice, and no one to have it.

Nothing he knew of talked without a mind behind it. Nothing.

"Please identify yourself as pilot."

The same words, in the same voice, as if he had not moved. Words ran across the panel as it spoke, faster than he could have read them even on a good day.

"Who's that?" It came out high. He made it lower. "Where are you?"

"Please identify yourself as pilot."

There was nothing behind the seats, nothing under them but grit and the frame. When the voice came again he turned his head after it and found it low beside the panel: a disk no bigger than his palm, pierced all over with tiny holes. He put his ear to it. It buzzed faintly against his ear when it spoke. Under his fingertip the disk trembled the way a frog's throat did when it called. The voice lived in there, then, or came out of there, and there was no room in it for anybody.

The suits at home told you things all day long, on a shield and in a glow at the forearm. He had never heard one say a word.

He sat back down, slowly, the knife still in his hand.

"Keo," he said.

"Keo. State your rating."

"My what?"

"Rating not recognized. Logging you as unqualified operator. Maintenance access only. A qualified pilot is preferred."

"There isn't one."

Its sentences ended on the note they began on, and it never stopped to breathe.

"What are you?"

"I am an artificial intelligence. I am this craft's maintenance and flight-training system."

"Who's in there, though?"

"There is nobody in the craft. I am not a person."

"Then how are you talking?"

"I was built to talk. The mission's" — and a word dropped out — "team designed me to recognize speech and to reply."

"That's who. I asked how."

"I do not understand the question."

"Can you hear what I'm thinking?"

"I do not understand the question."

It was the first thing that had answered him about itself. It asked him nothing back. Whatever it said it was, it was the voice. He put the knife away.

"What is this?" he said. "This thing I'm sitting in."

"This is a survey craft." Then a class and a number he could not keep hold of. "It flies low and fast over water and level ground."

"Flies." He looked out at the wing, long and flat and still, and thought of the flybeaks over the river at home, skimming with their wingtips nearly in the water. "Like a flybeak?"

"It flies on lift and thrust. It does not flap."

He let that go. He tapped the curved clear sheet. It was hard and cool, and he could see the doors through it as if nothing were there. "And this?"

"Windscreen. It keeps the wind off the pilot in flight."

"The lights in the roof. There's no fire in them."

"The lights draw on power from the station, through the charge lead." The cord from the wall. "Power is what makes the lights work. And me."

"Can it fly now?"

"This craft cannot fly. Main cell bank: removed for storage. Reserve: empty. Surface power membrane: not responding. Lift units: fitted."

"There's nothing in the round places," he said. "On the wings, and along the back."

"The lift unit mounts. I have no record of their removal. Logging a sensor fault."

"And the other boat with wings. The one out in the water."

"Craft two is moored."

"It's sunk. Its tail's sticking up out of the water."

"Logged."

It had been wrong twice now, in the same voice it was right in.

"When did you last fly?"

"Last flight: river survey, inland from the coast." It gave the pilot's name, a long founder name, and he had lost the front of it before the end. "After landing, this craft was powered down for storage. Days since last flight —" and a number, thousands on thousands, too long for him to hold.

"What would it take? To fly."

"Power. The main cells are in dry storage beside this hangar. The electrolyte is sealed in drums. Carry the cells to the bay dry. Fill them in the bay. Link them in series. Charge from the station."

"How long?"

"Full charge, about one day." Then, after a moment: "Measuring station supply. Revised. The station's generators are at about half their rating. A full charge will take two to three days <!-- @WF-wig.charge.days: 2-3 -->. Dimming hangar lights to spare the supply."

The strips along the roof sank to a glow.

"And then how far does it go?"

"On a full charge, about one hundred and twenty kilometers <!-- @WF-wig.range.claimed: 120km -->."

"That's farther than home," he said.

"Destination not recognized."

The short mound's end door had the same flap beside it, and he pulled that handle too. Strips came on inside, and the hum was louder. There were racks of blocks, each with two short posts and a cap on top, and a row of drums against one wall. Along the back lay long sealed boxes. He looked at them and left them. By the door stood a tall metal cupboard.

"There's a cupboard in here," he called.

"Kit locker."

He carried what was in it out to the boat and held each thing up at its door, and the voice named each: goggles, gloves, a measuring jug, a funnel. The last was a long metal tool with a jaw at one end.

"Torque wrench."

Then the blocks, one at a time against his chest. Each was a dead weight that wanted to fall away from him. The rib hurt at every lift, and the ankle at every step. With a cap on top for filling, each one was a jar to him.

"Where do the jars go?"

"Cells. The cells go in the bay, in the floor of the hull."

"Cells," he said, and went for the next jar.

"Turning on the bay lights."

He had lifted the lid off a long trough in the boat's floor, and lights came on low along it. He went to the rack and back seventy-odd times. The moon had been a thin curve in the west when he started, and it went down while he carried. After that there was starlight on the concrete between the two doors. That morning he had climbed the crown with a hand on Benal's shoulder. Now his hands were doing a thing that needed doing.

The first time he closed his hand in the gloves, the fingers split across the knuckles like a dry pod. He pulled them off. The goggles held.

"Fill each cell to the line."

He tipped a drum till it gurgled into the jug, and the jug through the funnel into the first jar, slowly, until the voice read him the line. What was in the drums was clear and smelled of nothing. A drop ran down the funnel onto his thumb, and he rubbed it against his finger. It was slick as a snail's track. Then it bit, and the bite grew after he had wiped it on his tunic.

"It bites," he said.

"Electrolyte on skin. Wash it off at once with plenty of water."

"There isn't plenty of water. There's the hollows, and that's what we drink. And there's the sea, and it's dark."

"Wash it off at once with plenty of water."

Keo spit and rubbed. He went slower.

"It's full right up," he said, at the next one.

"Not understood."

He thought about it. "The level is at the line."

"Confirmed. Next cell."

So he learned to say things its way, the long way, and it answered better when he did. Somewhere in the middle of the row he began to talk to it about other things.

"I had a sicklefoot," he said. "Noli. She ate crawlers out of my hand, and she'd steal them out of the beds the moment I let go of her. She thought I didn't know."

"I do not understand. Confirm fill level, cell thirty-four."

"At the line." He moved the funnel along. "When she was pleased she'd lean on my leg with her whole weight. And leave it there."

"Confirm fill level, cell thirty-five."

He told it the rest anyway, while the jug went up and down.

Between jars he asked it things.

"Where is everybody? The people who flew you."

"I do not know. This craft was powered down after its last flight. You are the first voice since."

"Why did they come here? The founders. What was it all for?"

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"If I was mission personnel, what would you tell me?"

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"What did you do, then? You. Not them."

"Survey. Craft of this class flew survey from the station, inland. Survey objective: fresh water near higher, defensible land."

"And this place? What was it?"

"The station was a laboratory. The station roster lists the personnel who worked there. Laboratory programs are restricted."

"What used to make you go? When nobody was charging you."

"A power membrane covered this craft and the station roofs. A living skin, grown to make power from light. The membrane is not responding."

Outside the door the wing's skin had gone the gray of dead bark. He tried to see it alive.

Later, when the last jar was full, he said, "Thank you."

It did not answer.

"Link cell one positive to cell two negative. Lay the strap from post to post. Secure each end."

The straps lay in a tray at the end of the bay, strips of metal with a hole at each end, and with them the things that held them.

"Secure it with what?" he said. "There's round things. Flat, with a hole in the middle. And long straight things, like a pin, with grooves running round them."

"Nuts and bolts. The bolt goes through the strap and the post. The nut goes on the bolt."

"A nut's what you eat."

"I do not understand."

He let it go. "Nut."

"Tighten with the torque wrench until it clicks. Confirm."

He fitted the jaw over the nut and turned until it clicked in his hand.

It read him every link after that, and had him say each one back. Plus of one to minus of the next, strap, bolt, nut, click. His fingers had blistered where the electrolyte had been, and every turn of the tool pressed on the blisters.

"On charge, leave the bay open, and the hangar doors. The cells give off gas when charging. It must not gather. No open flame."

"There isn't a flame on this whole island," Keo said. "Our kindler went down with the fieldpack."

"Not understood. No open flame."

It was well past the middle of the night when the last nut clicked.

"Last link confirmed. Close the isolator."

"The what?"

"The lever at the end of the bay."

He found it. "Which way's closed?"

"Down."

He pulled it down.

A number came up on the panel and stayed there.

"Isolator closed. Charging. The first cycles form the cells after storage. Charge level will not rise tonight."

"Then how do I know it's doing anything?"

"Charging."

He stood by the lever with his burned hands hanging. He knew what the Council would call this. Nobody had voted on it. He had done it on his own say, in the dark.

He left the doors open and walked back along the crown by starlight, and where he could not see his footing he went by the hum. The night was close. He was shivering. In the room by the inner door the other two were asleep against the wall.

He lay down by them. Teva stirred.

"Where were you?" she said.

"Looking round."

She was asleep again before he could have said anything more.

His hands burned.

It was not anything yet.
