Keo had scraped a patch inside the fence down to bare earth, to keep the fire off the fern. He knelt in it with what he'd thought to pick up along the trodden way, Teva helping: herd dung baked light as bark, and dead cycad fronds. He crumbled a frond, worked a pinch of resin paste from the kindler into it, and it caught. He fed it the dung a piece at a time until it took.

He was good at this, and he knew it. The flames came up, and the dark closed in and stopped at the edge of the light.

Something scratched at the thorns by the gap.

He knew who it was before he looked. He pulled the fronds aside. Noli trotted in with a grounder swinging from her jaws, went straight past him, and dropped it at Benal's feet, still warm, its neck broken.

"Thank you," Benal told her, gravely.

Noli folded her legs under her with her chest out, and every feather on her said that she had done something remarkable and that it was about time somebody noticed.

Keo pushed praise at her, warm, the smell of food: *good-pack-good-food*. A fat glow of satisfaction with herself came back down the bond. Then she looked at him, arched her neck and made a small hard sound in her throat, and he was sure he knew what she meant. She had brought it to Ben because Ben sat still and could be trusted with things. Keo would not have been grateful enough.

"She's disgusted with you," Teva said. She sat a little apart from the fire, knees up, her suit in a heap on the cycad fronds.

"She's not disgusted. She's—" Keo considered Noli, and Noli considered him back. "Okay, she's a bit disgusted."

"Your animal brought dinner. You brought dung."

"It's very good dung."

He reached to scratch the back of his neck and hissed. In the firelight his forearm was a tight dark red up to where his sleeve had been all day.

"I'm burned," he said, and laughed. "I've been cooking all day and never felt a thing."

"You were busy singing," Benal said.

Keo skinned the grounder with the tooth-knife and spitted it over the fire on a frond stripped to the stem. Once Noli had her share it would be a few mouthfuls each, the first meat since the honey day. The smell came up off the fire, and they went quiet watching it.

Across the fire, Teva sat back out of the heat, her face turned to the dark.

"You okay?" he said.

"Fine."

She looked like someone who'd had enough of the day and taken it off. He thought nothing more about it.

Benal shook the last skin. "Three mouthfuls. Possibly four, if nobody's greedy."

Keo held it across the fire to Teva without drinking. She looked at him, then drank once and passed it to Benal, who drank once and stopped and gave it back. She hung it on her belt.

They ate the grounder off the stem, burning their fingers. Noli took the pelt to the edge of the light and shook it hard, several times, as if it might still try something. She ate the head with enormous dignity. Then she brought a leg bone back, laid it beside Benal and looked at Keo.

"She's training you," Teva told Benal.

"I'm very trainable."

Afterward the itch under Keo's tunic got unbearable, and he pulled it off over his head. Benal sucked air through his teeth.

"Hold still. You're a tick farm. And the mites have the rest of you." He knelt behind Keo and picked the ticks off one at a time with his long fingers, flicking them into the fire. "There's a theory," he said, working, "that if enough of them have you, eventually there's nothing left to eat and they move on. Maybe that's how you die out here. Not the crocs. Not the razortails. A very large number of very small animals, all at once, in no hurry."

"Thanks, Ben."

"Possibly the likeliest one. I've been working on the odds all afternoon."

"Then you'd better keep picking," Teva said, from the dark. "Or there won't be enough of him left to return."

Keo laughed so suddenly he jerked, and Benal told him to hold still. Teva smiled before she could stop it. Keo saw, and felt better than he had all day, which was saying something.

When Benal finished, his hand rested on Keo's shoulder a moment longer than it needed to. Then he took it away and wiped it on his leg.

The fire burned down to coals. Keo eased his tunic back on and went to the far side of the fence, his back to the glow. His parents had taught him the faint stars only came if you gave them time, so he gave them time: long enough to count the others' breathing, to hear Noli settle and the fern tick as it cooled, long enough that his eyes stopped seeing the ghost of the fire. Twenty minutes, maybe more.

Then the sky came out all at once, as it had at the river, crowded down to the fern on every side.

"Teva," he said. "Come here. Away from the coals."

She came and stood beside him, not close, and looked up. He knew she was seeing a lot less of it than he was.

"Name one of your fields," he said. "I'll find them."

She pointed. "Dead Man's Journey. Start at the bright pair, low, there. The left one, then the other. Then follow the bend up and to the left."

The bend went up the sky where she said it would, five stars in a long curve.

She named them one by one, in Omya's order. Each time she said where, he looked, and it was there. Some were so faint he had to look a little to one side of them to see them at all. He found every one. He said *yes* each time, quieter as it went on, and it felt like something only the two of them in the world could do.

"That's all of them?"

"That's all of them."

He counted what was there against what she had named. The numbers came out the same; there was nothing in it she had not given him. He did not know what that meant.

Benal had come up behind them, looking up with his ordinary eyes and not finding much.

"Where's the moon?" he said.

"Not up," Teva said. "It's nearly new. It'll be back in a few days, thin, after sunset."

"And the other half. Omya's other half. Where the moon goes."

"Which stars it covers, in which months. On the long cycle." Teva did not look away from the sky. "It covers the bright one in Dead Man's Journey in the dry months, the table says, when its path comes round. Every eighteen years and a bit."

"The table says. Whose table?"

"The founders'. Omya says nobody's ever changed a word of it."

Benal went very still.

"Nobody's ever changed it," he said. "In two hundred years. They made it from where they thought they were. When they thought they were. And if they were wrong about when—and they were, they must have been—then the moon's been coming in the wrong months against it all this time, and nobody—" He stopped. "One covering. You'd only need one. Watch it happen and lay it against the table and the difference is—" He stopped again. "It's the drift factor. The one I've never filled in. It's sitting in Omya's head."

"I don't get it," Keo said.

"Nobody gets it. That's the point. It's been sitting there two hundred years, waiting for someone to." He looked at Teva. "And the fields you lost. Past the sixth. Nobody's watching them now."

Teva looked at the sky.

Keo found another star, a faint one off to the side of Dead Man's Journey, and showed her. "There's one you didn't name."

"That's not in the field. That's just a star."

He laughed, and let it go.

They stood a while longer under a sky with nothing new in it. Somewhere up there was the thing that was going to end the world.

"The records say they saw things there," Benal said, still looking up. "At Genesis. Lights. Smells that weren't there. It's one of the reasons they left."

"Three years <!-- @WF-genesis.occupancy: 3y --> with no water," Keo said. "I'd see things too."

Benal laughed.

Teva took the first watch, Benal the second. Keo asked for the last, because he'd slept least the night before and he liked the dawn, he said.

He opened the gap for Noli, and she lay down in the fern against the outside of the thorns, near where he would lie. He held her lightly in his head, as he had every night since she was small enough to carry, and she let him.

He lay down by the coals. In a few hours he would have the dawn.
