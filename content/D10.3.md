Keo had scraped a patch inside the fence down to bare earth, wide enough that no spark could reach the fern, and now he knelt in the middle of it with the wood he had gathered at the cycads the night before. Dry branches, bound with cord to the outside of his pack all day. He was the only one who had thought of it. He shaved tinder off the driest of them with his knife and blew the coal from the kindler into it, then fed it twigs until it took.

He was good at this, and he knew it. The flames came up, small and bright, and the dark came in around them and stopped at the edge of the light.

Something scratched at the thorns by the gap.

He knew who it was before he looked. He pulled the fronds aside. Noli came in low and quick with something hanging from her jaws, walked straight past him without a glance, and dropped it at Benal's feet: a grounder, fat and brown and still warm, its neck broken.

Benal looked down at it, and up at her. "Thank you," he said, gravely.

Noli sat down on her haunches with her chest out, and every feather on her said that she had done something remarkable and that it was about time somebody noticed.

*Good-pack-good-food,* Keo pushed at her, warmly, and what came back down the bond was a fat glow of satisfaction with herself. Then she looked at him, at the fire, at Benal. She arched her neck and made a small hard sound in her throat, and he knew exactly what she meant. She had brought it to Ben because Ben was the one who sat still and could be trusted with things. She did not think Keo would have been grateful enough.

"She's disgusted with you," Teva said.

She was sitting a little apart from the fire, with her knees up and her suit lying beside her on the cycad fronds in a gray heap.

"She's not disgusted. She's—" Keo considered Noli, and Noli considered him back. "All right, she's a bit disgusted."

"Your animal brought dinner," Teva said. "You brought sticks."

"They're very good sticks."

He reached up to scratch the back of his neck and hissed, because it hurt. He took his hand away and looked at it in the firelight. The skin of his forearm was a dark tight red all the way up to where his sleeve had been all day.

"I'm burned," he said, and started to laugh. "Look at that. I'm cooked. I've been cooking all day and I never felt a thing."

"You were busy singing," Benal said.

Keo skinned the grounder with the tooth-knife and spitted it on a green stick over the fire. It was small; once it was cooked it would be a few mouthfuls each, four ways, and it was the first meat any of them had eaten since the honey day. The smell of it came up off the fire and every one of them went quiet watching it.

Across the fire, Teva had her suit off, and she was sitting back from the fire, out of the heat of it, with her face turned to the dark.

"All right?" he said.

"Fine."

She looked like someone who had had enough of the day and taken it off. He thought nothing more about it.

Benal shook the last skin; there was something in it, not much.

"Three mouthfuls," he said. "Possibly four, if nobody's greedy."

Keo took the skin, drank one mouthful, and held it out across the fire to Teva. She looked at him, then took it and drank, and drank again. She kept it, and he let her.

They ate the grounder off the stick with their fingers, burning them, and it was the best thing he had tasted since the honey. Noli got the skin and the head and the parts the rest of them did not want, and had views about all of them. She took the skin away to the edge of the light and shook it, hard, several times, as if it might still try something. She ate the head with enormous dignity. She brought one leg bone back and laid it next to Benal again and looked at Keo.

"She's training you," Teva said to Benal.

"I'm very trainable," Benal said.

Afterward Keo pulled his tunic up over his head, because the itch under it had gone from bad to unbearable, and Benal looked at his back in the firelight and made a noise.

"Hold still."

"What is it?"

"You're a mite farm," Benal said. He knelt behind Keo and began picking them off, one at a time, with his long fingers, and dropping them in the fire, where they made very small sounds. "There's a theory," he said, working, "that if you let enough of them have you, there's eventually nothing left for them to eat, and they move on. Possibly that's how you die out here. Not the crocs. Not the razortails. A very large number of very small animals, all at once, in no hurry."

"Thank you, Ben."

"It's statistically the likeliest death," Benal said. "I've been working on it all afternoon."

"Then you'd better keep picking," Teva said, from the dark. "Or there won't be enough of him left to return."

Keo laughed so suddenly he jerked, and Benal swore at him and started again on the shoulder. Even Teva's mouth had moved, and Keo saw it, and felt better than he had felt all day, which was saying something.

When Benal finished, his hand rested on Keo's shoulder a moment longer than it needed to, and then he took it away and wiped it on his leg.

The fire burned down to coals. Keo put on his tunic again, carefully. He walked to the far side of the fence, away from the glow, and stood with his back to it and waited. He had learned on trips with his parents that the faint stars only came if you gave them time, so he gave them time: long enough to count the breaths of the others behind him, to hear Noli settle, to hear the fern tick in the cooling air, long enough that his eyes stopped seeing the ghost of the fire. Twenty minutes, maybe more.

Then the sky came out, all at once, as it had on the first night, crowded right down to the fern on every side.

"Teva," he said. "Come here. Away from the coals."

She came and stood beside him, not close, and looked up. He knew she was seeing a lot less of it than he was.

"Name one," he said. "One of your fields. I'll find them."

After a moment she lifted her arm and pointed. "The Organs," she said. "Start at the bright one, low, there. That's the first."

"Got it."

"Then up and left, a hand's width. Two together, close."

"Yes."

She named them one by one, in the order Omya had given them to her. Each time she said where, he looked, and each time it was there. Some of them were so faint he had to look a little to one side of them to see them at all. He found every one. He said *yes* each time, quieter as it went on, and it felt like something the two of them were doing together that no one else in the world could do.

When she stopped he said, "That's all of them?"

"That's all of them."

He counted what was there against what she had named. The numbers came out the same. Nothing in it she had not given him. He did not know what that meant, and neither did she.

Behind them, Benal had come up quietly. He was looking up, too, with his ordinary eyes, and not finding much.

"Where's the moon?" he said.

"There isn't one tonight," Teva said. "It's under the edge. It'll be back in a few days, thin."

"And the other half," Benal said. "Omya's other half. Where the moon goes."

"Which stars it covers, in which months. On the long cycle." Teva did not look away from the sky. "It covers the bright one in the Organs in the dry months, the table says. Every eighteen years and a bit."

"The table says. Whose table?"

"The founders'. It was theirs. Omya says nobody's ever changed a word of it."

Benal had gone very still.

"Nobody's ever changed it," he said. "In two hundred years. They made it from where they thought they were. When they thought they were. And if they were wrong about when — and they were, they must have been — then the moon's been coming in the wrong months against it all this time, and nobody — " He stopped. "One covering. You'd only need one. Watch it happen and lay it against the table and the difference is—" He stopped again. "It's the missing term. It's sitting in Omya's head."

"I don't follow," Keo said.

"Nobody follows," Benal said. "That's the point. It's been sitting there for two hundred years waiting for someone to follow it." He looked at Teva. "And the fields you lost. Past the sixth. Nobody's watching them now."

Teva looked at the sky.

Keo found another star, a faint one off to the side of the Organs, and showed her. "There's one you didn't name."

"That's not in the field," she said. "That's just a star."

He laughed, and let it go.

They stood a while longer looking up at a sky that had nothing new in it. Somewhere up there was the thing that was going to end the world, and it looked exactly like everything else.

Teva took the first watch and Benal the second. Keo asked for the last one, into the dawn, because he had slept least the night before and he liked the dawn, he said.

He opened the gap for Noli, and she went out and lay down in the fern close against the outside of the thorns, near where he would lie. He held her lightly in his head, as he had every night since she was small enough to carry, and she let him.

He lay down by the coals. In a few hours he would have the dawn.
