They came out from under the last of the trees in the middle of the morning, and there was nothing over them but sky.

The fern went away on every side, head-high, green going gray-green in the distance, with a trodden way through it where herds had gone and the ground was packed hard between the fronds. Here and there an old cycad stood up out of it like a post. The sun was already high. The heat came off the fern in a shimmer and lay on everything, and the air smelled of dust and hot fern.

Keo felt wonderful.

He had felt the heat come into him under the trees all the way from the tangle, and it had always gone out again. Out here it came in faster and went out just as fast, and he could feel his body doing it, the way he could feel it breathe, steady and easy and without his asking. He was the only one of them with no suit. He was the only one of them who did not need one. He set the pace and it felt like nothing. His heel caught once on the packed ground, the same heel as before, and he shortened his step on that side and did not slow.

After a while he was singing.

He did not decide to. It was the long one, the song Liaso hummed on the watch, and he had breath to spare for all of it. He sang it at the pace of his feet. Behind him Benal knew some of the words. He came in on the chorus a breath late and out of breath, and Keo sang louder to carry him.

He did not look back at Teva. She was four steps behind him. She had kept four steps behind him since they left the trees.

Halfway through the fourth verse, something crossed the trodden way ahead of them.

Keo saw them first. A whole band, a dozen or more, long-legged and long-necked, coming out of the fern on the left at a dead run and going across the open way and into the fern on the right without breaking stride, so fast he heard them after he saw them. Then they were gone and the fronds were shaking where they had been.

He stopped. He was grinning.

"*Ornithomimus*," he said. "Did you see that? Did you see how fast? Nothing catches those. Maybe a tyrannosaur, if it's lucky and they're stupid. They're not stupid." He turned around to say it to Teva.

She had not looked up. She was standing in the trodden way with her eyes on the ground in front of her, as if nothing had crossed it at all.

That was Teva, he thought. A dozen of the fastest things alive go past, and she looks at her feet. He turned back and walked on, still grinning, and took up the song where he had left it.

Benal had sweat running down his face under the open helmet, though his suit was doing its work. "I'm being eaten," Benal said. "By something. Several things. Right where the suit stops."

"Next time," Keo said, "try not to look so delicious. You're making them hungry."

Benal laughed, wheezing. "You're the one with nothing on. They're just practicing on me."

The heat built. By late morning the shimmer had come up off the fern so that the far edge of the world wavered, and the old cycads stood in it like people waiting.

"We should lie up," Benal said.

Keo looked at him.

"In the fern," Benal said. "It's shade, if you crouch. It's the hottest part of the day. Everything we were ever taught says lie up now. The odds on walking through this—"

"Listen," Keo said.

Far off, across the fern, something was calling. Low and long and mournful, like a horn blown over water, and then another answering it. A herd of *Kritosaurus*, somewhere out to the right, moving.

"They're going to water," Keo said. "That's what that is. That's the sound they make when they're going to drink. At home the work herd does it every evening." He shook the skin at his belt; it was light. "And we need water. We've got a few mouthfuls each. If we lie up in the fern we lie up thirsty, and we're still thirsty when it's dark. If we follow that, we drink tonight."

"Possibly," Benal said. "Or possibly they're just calling."

"They're going to water, Ben."

He said it kindly. He was sure. He could hear it in the calls, the same pull he had heard in the work herd a thousand evenings, and he looked at Teva, because she was the one who decided things.

She was looking at the ground. Her face did not change.

All right, then, Keo thought. That was a yes, from Teva.

Benal looked from one of them to the other. Then he let his breath out and nodded.

They walked on through the middle of the day. The flying things that had followed them through the woods for a week were gone; nothing whined at his ears out here. It was too dry for them. Something else was working up under the hem of his tunic and around his waist, small and itching, and he scratched without noticing and walked on.

By the middle of the afternoon the calls had moved off and away. The fern went on, and there was no water in it anywhere.

"Half a canteen," Benal said. He had all three belt skins in his hands, weighing them. "Between three. That's what we've got. Possibly a little less."

"We'll find it," Keo said. "They were going somewhere."

Teva took her skin back from Benal and drank her share, fast, in two swallows, and hung it on her belt again.

Keo drank from his own skin and handed Benal back his. The backs of his hands and his forearms below the sleeves had been bare to the sun all day. He did not look down at them.

He set the pace again, and it still felt like nothing. Behind him the other two came on through the fern, and neither of them asked him to slow down.

He took that as agreement too.
