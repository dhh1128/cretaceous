By the middle of the morning, when Keo looked back, the last of the trees were a dark line behind them, and there was nothing overhead but sky.

The fern stood head-high on every side, shoulder-high in places, with a trodden way through it where herds had packed the ground hard. Here and there an old cycad stood up out of it like a post. Heat shimmered off the fern, and the air smelled of dust and hot fronds.

Keo felt wonderful.

All the way from the tangle the heat had come into him and gone out again. Out here it came in faster and went out just as fast, and he could feel his body doing it, like breathing. He was the only one of them with no suit, the only one who didn't need one. He set the pace and it felt like nothing. His heel caught once on the packed ground. He didn't slow.

After a while he was singing.

It was the long one, the song Liaso hummed on the watch, and he had breath for all of it. He sang it at the pace of his feet. Behind him Benal knew some of the words and came in on the chorus late and wheezing, and Keo sang louder to carry him.

He didn't look back at Teva. She had kept four steps behind him since they left the trees.

Halfway through the fourth verse Keo saw them first: a band of a dozen or more, long-legged, long-necked, bursting out of the fern on the left at a dead run, across the trodden way and into the fern on the right without breaking stride. Then they were gone, the fronds shaking where they'd been.

He stopped, grinning.

"*Ornithomimus*," he said. "Did you see that? Did you see how fast? Nothing catches those. Maybe a tyrannosaur, if it's lucky and they're stupid. They're not stupid." He turned to say it to Teva.

She hadn't looked up. She stood in the trodden way with her eyes on the ground in front of her.

That was Teva, he thought. A dozen of the fastest things alive go past, and she looks at her feet. He walked on, still grinning, and took up the song where he'd left it.

Benal had sweat running down his face under the open helmet, though his suit was doing its work. "I'm being eaten," he said. "By something. Several things. Right where the suit stops."

"Next time," Keo said, "try not to look so delicious. You're making them hungry."

Benal laughed, wheezing. "You're the one with nothing on. They're just practicing on me."

The heat built. By late morning the far edge of the world wavered, and the old cycads stood in the shimmer like people waiting.

"We should lie up," Benal said.

Keo looked at him.

"In the fern," Benal said. "It's shade, if you crouch. It's the hottest part of the day. Everything we were ever taught says lie up now. The odds on walking through this—"

"Listen," Keo said.

Far off across the fern, something bugled, long and mournful, and another answered. *Kritosaurus*, a herd somewhere out to the right, moving.

"They're going to water," Keo said. "The work herd makes that sound every evening at home, going to drink." He shook the skin at his belt; it was light. "We've got a few mouthfuls each. If we lie up, we lie up thirsty. If we follow them, we drink tonight."

"Possibly," Benal said. "Or they're just calling."

"They're going to water, Ben."

He said it kindly. He could hear it in the calls. He looked at Teva, because she was the one who decided things.

She was looking at the ground. Her face didn't change.

All right, then, Keo thought. That was a yes, from Teva.

Benal let his breath out and nodded.

They walked on through the middle of the day. The flying things that had whined at his ears through the woods for a week were gone; it was too dry for them out here. Something else was working up under the hem of his tunic and around his waist, small and itching, and he scratched without noticing.

By the middle of the afternoon the calls had moved off, and what the herd had left behind was a hollow of trodden mud and dung that nobody could drink.

"Half a skin," Benal said, weighing all three belt skins in his hands. "Between three. Maybe less."

"That's where they wallow," Keo said. "Not where they drink. They were going somewhere."

Teva took her skin back from Benal, drank her share fast, in two swallows, and hung it on her belt.

Keo drank from his own. The backs of his hands and his forearms below the sleeves had been bare to the sun all day. He didn't look down at them.

He set the pace again, and it still felt like nothing. Behind him the other two came on through the fern. Neither of them asked him to slow down.

He took that as agreement too.
