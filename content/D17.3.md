Teva held the register against her chest with both arms crossed over it. In her head the moon table was still going in Omya's voice, which star the moon would cover and in which month, line after line, and she could not make it stop.

They had come back through the archive to the room by the inner door, where the hum was lower, and kept clear of the outer room and its seam. None of them sat. Benal stood turning the round of glass from the lab in his fingers. Keo had his shoulders against the wall and his weight off the bad ankle.

"They lied to us," she said.

Keo came off the wall. "They did." He was breathing hard through his nose. "All of it. The tangle, my whole life. The suits on the rack. Every time I asked, it was *You're not an adult yet, Keo.* Not yet. Not yet for what? There wasn't ever going to be a yet."

"It isn't that," Benal said. "Or it isn't only that. Listen to me. The sky has seventeen years <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y --> at the most, and it could be any one of them. The cure is in every child at home. It's in you. It was meant to go back, and there's nobody alive who can take it. That's what we found in there. Not a lie. A job nobody can—"

"Nobody alive," Teva said. "No. Nobody ever could."

She heard her voice go level, the voice for the positions in the Vitarium, one after another with nothing left out.

"Listen. The wrapping culture is in the lineages. Year three, at home. I've fed it. I could tell you its line in the dark. They grew it at the Enclave, and the Council stands up every session and calls it founder law. It isn't. Nothing here was ever wrapped. Not one thing on those shelves. That's one.

"The Council says centuries. Every yazhi knows it. Centuries, and time enough to do it right. The founders knew better. It's in there on a tablet, cut in metal so it would last, and they left it here, eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from anyone who needed to read it. That's two.

"The Mission. Two hundred years of the Mission. Every rule we have is for the day somebody goes home, and nobody born at the Enclave could ever have walked that road. Not one of us. Not my mother. Not anybody who ever sat on the dais and told us to wait."

Her head had begun to ache behind the eyes, and she kept on.

"And Omya. Sixty years <!-- @WF-omya.watch.years: 60 --> at the Watch. Every clear night, their table against the sky, and the positions every morning, word for word, keeping it perfect for a road home she could never—"

Her throat shut on the rest. She saw Omya with her face turned up to the dark, saying the stars to nobody, and she held the register tighter and looked at the floor until it passed.

Benal had stopped turning the glass. "Then what would you tell them?" he said. "If you were standing in the kiva. What would you say?"

It was her kind of question, and she had no answer for it.

"I don't think they lied," Benal said. "I think they didn't know. The founders had the old number, the centuries one; that's what they came with, possibly. The ones who stayed worked out the new one, here, after the others had gone, and then they took it with them. Further back. I think nobody at home ever had it. You can't lie about a number you've never seen."

"What difference does it make?" Keo said. "Lied, didn't know. They kept us in either way."

"Yes," Teva said.

"Yes, but if they—"

Benal looked from one of them to the other, and then down at the glass in his hand.

Keo pushed his hair back off his face with both hands. "We need to go up to the water," he said. "Before the light goes. Both of you. Come on."

For a while none of them moved.

When they did go, the heat met her at the outer door and lay on her face and arms the whole way up the cut. On the crown the sun came straight down on rock too hot to put a hand on, and the hollows sat in it, lower again. They drank on their knees, one at a time, while the other two watched the sky and the slope.

When Benal stood up from his turn he stopped and looked at the back of his hand.

He held the glass over it. Teva watched him move it, closer and back. On his skin a spot of light drew down small and went fierce, until he snatched the hand away and shook it.

"Huh," he said. He did it again, more carefully, and watched his own skin. "That's warm. That's the glass doing that." He looked up at them. "The records said a curved clear thing bends light. Nobody ever said it bent the heat too."

Keo was staring at him. "That's why you wanted the crayfish kept."

"I wasn't sure. I'm still not, possibly."

"Well, I've got two in my tunic, and you don't eat crayfish raw."

Above the hollows lay the driftwood Keo had carried up from the reef flat at midday, split and bleached, drying on the rock. Benal crouched over it, shaved a curl of fiber off a stick with his knife, and rubbed it apart between his palms into a nest of shreds. He set the stick on the rock beside it and held the glass over the shreds.

The point of light went white, and she could not look at it. A thread of smoke stood up off the wood, with no spark struck and nothing rubbed.

"Let me," Keo said. He got down on his elbows, cupped his hands round the shreds and breathed on them, long and slow, and a flame came up out of the smoke.

"That's glass," Teva said.

"I know." Benal had not moved the glass yet. "I keep waiting for it to do something terrible."

Every child at home was taught glass by name, with concrete and steel and knapped stone: the things that last, the things you never make. Nobody she knew had ever held any. The kindler was at the bottom of the sea with the fieldpack, and Benal had made fire with the forbidden thing in his bare hand.

Keo fed it splinters, then sticks. The sea was still in the wood: the flame came up yellow, crackling and spitting as the salt went, and the smoke had the reek of the shore in it.

He took the two crayfish out of his tunic. They were the pools' kind, flat and wide, with too many legs, and he laid them on the coals and turned them with a stick until their shells went red, as a river crayfish's did. Then he lifted one off with two sticks and juggled it on the rock until he could hold it.

"No tail," he said. He turned it over. "Where does it keep its meat?"

"Possibly it's all legs," Benal said.

Keo cracked a leg at the joint with his teeth and looked inside, and then broke the body open with his thumbs. There was meat in the legs, and more in the body, in pockets between thin walls of shell. He pulled out a fan of something gray and soft and threw it off the edge of the rock.

"Don't eat the gray bits."

He broke off the biggest leg and held it out to her. "Here. Teva. Eat."

She took it. The shell was hot enough to sting her fingers, and the meat came out of it in threads. It was sweet, like crayfish, but finer. The heat of the fire was on her face and her shins. All day she had been in rooms that were never warm or cold, and now there was this. It was the first hot food since the grounder Noli had dropped at Benal's feet. She looked at Keo, bent over the shell with his thumbs, and kept it to herself.

"That's crayfish," Benal said, with his mouth full. "That's better than crayfish."

"Eat the body too. There's more in there than in the legs."

They ate while the sun went down the sky behind the mounds, and the shadows of the arches lay long across the rock.

"That fin we saw from the top," Keo said. "It's a tail. It's a boat with wings, sunk in the shallows round the far end, past the last mound." He pulled a thread of meat out of a leg. "And there's another one. Whole. Shut up in a long mound like the ones up here. There's a voice in it. It answers you."

Benal had stopped chewing.

"I filled its jars last night," Keo said. "Seventy-odd of them. It's charging."

"Jars?" Benal said.

"They go in the floor of it." Keo looked at the fire. "I don't know if it'd go home. I said home and it didn't know where that was."

"A voice," Benal said. "Saying what?"

"Hardly anything." Keo flung a piece of shell into the fire, hard, and it cracked there. "I asked it why they came here, what it was all for, where they all went, and I couldn't get anything out of it. Nothing. It said there wasn't a pilot."

So it had said a few words and gone quiet, and he was sore about it. Teva knew old-tech that told you things. Her suit had told her things all the way from home, on its shield and in the glow along her arm: a heading, a count, a warning, the same few things over and over until it died, and none of it had ever been an answer to anything she asked. A thing in a mound that said a few words and stopped was the same.

Benal turned the glass over once in his fingers and looked back at the fire.

The case was still in her mouth.

"Not now," she said, and went back to the leg.

Keo looked at her a moment. Then he bent to the second shell and went in after the meat with his thumb.

The fire burned down to coals and the salt in them ticked. Teva wiped her fingers on her knees and opened the register on her lap, and turned the leaves until she came to the one she had marked with her thumb all day.

The woman looked out at her from the metal. She was the one who had written *what returns is the platform* on the leaf on the lab wall and signed it. At home Sila stood up every session and spoke for this woman, and had never read one word she wrote. Nobody at home had. The words were here.

Teva went through it again, in order, as she would say it standing in the kiva with the whole Enclave on its feet behind her. The wrapping. The centuries. The road that was never there. One, two, three, level, with no tears in it for anyone to point at.

She closed the register on the face. Omya she would keep out of it.
