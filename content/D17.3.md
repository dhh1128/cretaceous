Teva held the register against her chest with both arms crossed over it. In her head the moon's calendar was still going in Omya's voice, which star the moon would cover and in which month, line after line, and she could not make it stop.

They had come back through the archive to the room by the inner door. None of them sat. Benal stood turning the round of glass in his fingers. Keo had his shoulders against the wall and his weight off the bad ankle.

She had turned it over all the way back from the lab, every way she knew, and it came out the same every time.

"They lied to us," she said.

Keo came off the wall. "They did." He was breathing hard through his nose. "All of it. The tangle, my whole life. The suits on the rack. Every time I asked, it was *You're not an adult yet, Keo.* Not yet. Not yet for what? There wasn't ever going to be a yet."

"Every time?" she said.

"Same words. I could have said them for my mother."

"It isn't that," Benal said. "Or it isn't only that. Listen to me, Teva. It's in you, and there's nobody alive who can take it home. That's what we found in there. Not a lie. A job nobody can—"

"Nobody alive," Teva said. "No. Nobody ever could."

She heard her voice go level, the voice for the positions in the Vitarium.

"The wrapping culture is in the lineages. Year three, at home. I've fed it. They grew it at the Enclave, and the Council stands up every session and calls it founder law. Nothing on those shelves was ever wrapped, Ben. Not one leaf. And the centuries. Centuries, and time enough to do it right. Your tablet says seventeen years <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y -->."

"At the most," Benal said. "That's what I'm—"

"Then the founders knew. They cut it in metal, and they left it here, eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from anyone who needed to read it. I heard you read it once. Did I hear it wrong?"

"No," Benal said. "That's the number."

She had wanted him to say yes.

"And the Mission," Keo said.

"Two hundred years of it. Every rule we have is for the day somebody goes home, and nobody born at the Enclave could ever have walked that road. Not my mother. Not anybody who ever sat on the dais and told us to wait."

Her head had begun to ache behind the eyes, and she kept on.

"And Omya. Sixty years <!-- @WF-omya.watch.years: 60 --> at the Watch. Every clear night, their table against the sky, and the positions every morning, word for word, keeping it perfect for a road home she could never—"

Her throat shut on the rest. She saw Omya with her face turned up to the dark, saying the stars to nobody. She had walked her up to the Watch on clear nights, and been proud to. She held the register tighter and looked at the floor until it passed.

Benal had stopped turning the glass. "Then what would you tell them?" he said. "If you were standing in the kiva."

It was her kind of question, and she had no answer for it.

"I don't think they lied," Benal said. "I think they didn't know. The founders had the old number, the centuries one. That's what they came with, possibly. The ones who stayed worked out the new one, here, after the others had gone, and then they took it with them. Further back. I think nobody at home ever had it. You can't lie about a number you've never seen."

He said it the way he said everything he was not sure of. To her it sounded like the dais, excusing itself.

"What difference does it make?" Keo said. "Lied, didn't know. They kept us in either way."

"Yes," Teva said.

"Yes, but if they—"

Benal looked from one of them to the other, and then down at the glass in his hand.

Keo pushed his hair back off his face with both hands. "We need to go up to the water," he said. "Before the light goes. Both of you. Come on."

For a while none of them moved.

When they did go, the heat lay on her face and arms the whole way up the cut. On the crown the hollows sat in the hard sun, lower again. They drank on their knees, one at a time, while the other two watched the sky.

When Benal stood up from his turn he stopped and looked at the back of his hand.

He held the glass over it. Teva watched him move it, closer and back. On his skin a spot of light drew down small and went fierce, until he snatched the hand away and shook it.

"Huh," he said. "That's warm. That's the glass doing that." He looked up at them. "The records said a curved clear thing bends light. At the plate I wondered if it bent the heat too. I never thought to try it." He looked round the crown. "I want something dry."

There was nothing up here but rock and the mounds.

Keo looked down past the brow to the bank, where the storm's weed lay in a line. "Above the weed, where the storm didn't reach, there's old wood. It's dry."

"Not on your own," Teva said.

"Then come and watch for me."

She went down with him across the drained low ground, with the heat coming up off the rock, and stood on the bank watching the water out to the reef while he worked along above the weed. Behind her she heard him snap a stick to hear how dry it was.

Then he went out on his good foot onto the rocks at the water's edge, where the sideways crayfish were, and crouched.

"What are you doing?"

"If his glass works, there's a fire." The first one came up kicking. The second went out from under his hand three times, sideways round the same rock, before he had it. "And you don't eat crayfish raw."

He carried the wood up, an armful of it bleached and light as bone, and she carried the register under her arm and the crayfish by their backs in one hand, their legs going.

Benal crouched over the wood, shaved a curl of fiber off a stick with Keo's knife, and rubbed it apart between his palms into a nest of shreds. He held the glass over the shreds.

The point of light went white, and she could not look at it. A thread of smoke stood up off the wood, with no spark struck and nothing rubbed.

"Let me," Keo said. He got down on his elbows, cupped his hands round the shreds and breathed on them, and a flame came up out of the smoke.

"That's glass," Teva said.

"I know." Benal had not moved the glass yet. "I keep waiting for it to do something terrible."

It was the first fire since the kindler went with the fieldpack.

Teva went into the shade of the nearest mound. The bank had put more heat into her than she could shed, and she would not sit by a fire in the sun.

Keo fed the fire splinters, then sticks. The old salt was still in the drift: the flame came up yellow, crackling and spitting as it burned out, and the smoke had the reek of the shore in it.

He laid the two crayfish on the coals and turned them with a stick until their shells went red, as a river crayfish's did. He lifted one off with two sticks and turned it over.

"No tail," he said. "Where does it keep its meat?"

"Possibly it's all legs," Benal said.

Keo cracked a leg with his teeth, then broke the body open with his thumbs. There was meat in pockets between thin walls of shell, and a fan of something gray and soft, which he threw off the rock.

"Don't eat the gray bits."

He broke off the biggest leg and brought it over to her. "Here. Teva. Eat."

She took it. The shell was hot enough to sting her fingers, and the meat came out of it in threads. It was sweet, like crayfish, but finer. Even from the shade she could feel the fire on her face. It was the first hot food since the grounder Noli had dropped at Benal's feet. She kept that to herself.

It was the biggest leg there was. She cracked it at the joint and held out the thick half.

"That's yours," Keo said.

"Half of it is."

He came and took it, and sat down by her in the shade. Benal was eating with both hands.

"Eat the body too," Keo said. "There's more in there than in the legs."

"You'd know," Teva said. "You chased it round that rock long enough."

Benal laughed with his mouth full. After a moment, so did Keo.

"That fin we saw from the top," Keo said. "It's a tail. It's a boat with wings, sunk in the shallows round the far end, past the last mound. And there's another one. Whole. Shut up in a long mound like the ones up here. There's a voice in it. It answers you."

Benal had stopped chewing.

"I filled its jars last night," Keo said. "Seventy-odd of them. It's charging."

Teva looked at his hands. The fingers were blistered across the tips, and that much she understood: whatever went into those jars had burned him, and he had gone on filling them.

"Jars?" Benal said.

"They go in the floor of it." Keo looked at the fire. "I don't know if it'd go home. I said home and it didn't know where that was."

"A voice," Benal said. "Saying what?"

"Hardly anything." Keo flung a piece of shell into the fire, hard, and it cracked there. "I asked it why they came here, what it was all for, where they all went, and I couldn't get anything out of it. Nothing. It said there wasn't a pilot."

So it had said a few words and gone quiet, and he was sore about it. Her suit had told her things all the way from home: a count, a warning, the same few over and over until it died. A thing in a mound that said a few words and stopped was the same kind of thing.

Benal turned the glass over once in his fingers and looked back at the fire.

The case was still in her mouth.

"Not now," she said, and went back to the leg.

Keo looked at her a moment. Then he got up for the second shell and went in after the meat with his thumb.

The fire burned down to coals and the salt in them ticked. Teva wiped her fingers on her knees and opened the register at the leaf she had kept her thumb in all day.

The woman looked out at her from the metal. She had written *what returns is the platform*. At home Sila stood up whenever the Council sat and spoke for this woman, and had never read one word she wrote. Nobody at home had. The words were here.

Teva went through it again, in order, as she would say it standing in the kiva with the whole Enclave on its feet behind her. The wrapping. The centuries. The road that was never there. One, two, three, level, with no tears in it for anyone to point at.

She closed the register on the face. Omya she would keep out of it.
