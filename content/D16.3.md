With the fish in him Benal could think again, and he read the inner door's panel once more from the top. The words across the middle of it were the same as outside: *Authorized personnel only. Identify.* He had read those a dozen times. This time he went on to the end, and along the bottom edge, in small letters he had taken for part of the frame, it said: *Station sealed. Internal doors: press to open.*

He put his palm to the door and pressed. Something gave inside the wall, and the door came open.

"What did you do?" Teva said.

He pointed at the small letters. She bent and read them, and let her breath out through her nose. "You read to the end. Nobody reads to the end."

The room beyond was lit like the others. The hum was louder. Racks stood in rows back from the door, and in their slots, on edge, stood plates of metal, row on row of them. Along one wall ran shelves stacked with something thinner still: leaves of metal, so many to a stack that he could not have counted them. Along the other wall, panels were lit, as at the door.

Benal stepped in. The sound of surf faded. He had not known how much of it had still been reaching him through the outer rooms until it was gone. For the first time since they left home, nothing came to him but the hum and their own feet on the floor. He stood and felt the lack of it, like something set down off his back.

"Listen," he said.

Teva stopped. "To what?"

"Nothing. That's what. There's nothing out there any more."

"There's the hum," Keo said, from behind them.

"That's all there is."

Teva was looking at Benal, not at the room. "Is it better? In here?"

"Much."

"Then stay in here as long as you like."

The panels along the wall gave off hot dust, the door's smell that morning without the lightning in it. Even that was faint.

He drew out the nearest leaf from a stack. It was thin enough to give under his thumb, and when he let it go it sprang back straight. The stack it came from was heavier than any stack of anything that thin had a right to be.

Keo had stopped in the doorway, out of the light, with his hands at his sides and nothing in here for him to read. Benal saw him there. In math lessons Keo had turned his letters round on the board, a b for a d, and Benal had wondered about it ever since. He went back to the racks.

"Nothing in here was wrapped either," he said.

"Not now," Teva said. "Hold on to it, though. The protocols first, and then you can tell me why."

He kept it.

The racks were labeled at the ends, in the founders' plain words, and he went along them reading. "Charter," he said. "Charter. Charter. Three copies." The next three said *Paradox constraints*. "Three of those too." He drew a plate partway out of its slot. It rasped against the rack. It was cool and heavier than it looked, and the letters were cut into it so cleanly that one edge caught his thumbnail.

At the head of the charter racks, set in a frame under a clear cover, there was a plate on its own. He read it aloud.

"*Records disposition. Permanent archive. Jump minus six weeks. Ruling on the question raised at Tuesday's session. Mission rationale, charter, and the paradox constraints go to the permanent archive, in durable substrate, in triplicate. These are the things a settlement must not lose. Operational instruction — vector handling, transit mechanics, return procedure — remains in working files. It is voluminous, it will be revised continuously, and it is of no use to anyone who is not already doing the work.*"

"Vector handling," Teva said. "Omya talks about vectors sometimes. I think that's the protocols."

"Yes."

"Then the working files are in the panels." She was looking at the lit wall.

Benal looked back through the door, the way they had come, at the benches in the outer rooms and the rotten blocks of stuck sheets lying on them.

"No," he said. "That's the working files."

Teva looked where he was looking, and her face closed.

He went on along the racks, because there were racks he had not read. Every plate he passed was stamped in one corner with the same few words and a date in the first months after the jump.

At the end of the last rack the plates changed. They were headed *Vector handling*, and the stamp in the corner was not like the others. He read it twice before he read it out.

"*Jump plus thirty-three months*," <!-- @WF-plates.stamp: +33mo --> he said. "*Deposited after the coalition left. For whoever comes back.*"

Teva came and stood beside him.

"Somebody did it anyway," Benal said. "Against the ruling, long after it."

"Who?"

"There's no name on it."

He drew the first of them out of its slot and gave it to her. She held it in both hands and read down it, slowly, her lips moving.

"Position one," she read. Then she was not reading any more. Her eyes had left the plate. She was saying it, position one and position two, vector into culture and culture into sequencing, and on. Benal followed her down the plate with his eyes and could not find her wrong. Omya had said these over the cultures for her whole working life, and now they were going out of her a piece at a time. Somebody had cut them into metal here before any of them was born.

"Twenty," she said, and stopped, and wiped the blur out of her eyes.

The plate went on: a twenty-first, in the same clean letters, and a twenty-second, and more.

"It's Omya's," she said.

"Yes," Benal said. "It is."

She did not turn away, or put her hand up. Her face was working.

Under the last position on it, at the foot of the plate, there was a list.

"Look," he said, and touched it. "That's every plate that comes after this one, by number."

She gave it back to him, and he counted the rack against it under his breath. Each plate had its number cut at the top corner. He went carefully.

Somewhere past twenty his skin prickled, and the hairs stood up along his arms and on the back of his neck. There was lightning in the air. The hum had gone from under everything.

Then it was back. His hand was on a plate, and the plate was three on from the last one he had counted. He had not counted the three.

He took his hand away and went back to the start of the rack, and began again from one.

"They're all here," he said.

Teva looked up. "All of them?"

"All of them."

Keo had come in from the doorway. He stood at the rack beside Benal and lifted one plate out of its slot in both hands. He felt the weight of it, and looked along the rack at all the others, then along the shelves of leaves and the racks behind.

"We can't carry all of that," he said.

Benal looked back down the length of the outer rooms. At their far end, the outer door had gone from white to gold.

They had what they had come for. They were on an island in the sea, with no boat, and the water in the hollows going down a finger in a night.

Across the room, at the far end of the racks, there was another door.
