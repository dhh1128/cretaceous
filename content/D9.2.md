The thicket stood at the last edge of the trees, young trunks grown so close that a hand would hardly go between them, open on one side only. They stopped there with an hour of light left, because Keo said so and because none of them could have walked another step.

They cut stakes for the open side and drove them in at an angle, points out, and laid thorn between them. They were all slow with it, slow and stupid with tiredness. Benal dropped the same stake twice, and the second time he stood looking down at it, betrayed. Teva picked it up and handed it to him, and he drove it in.

Keo made the fire on a patch of scraped ground, with dry wood from under a fallen trunk and the coal from the kindler. Then he went back out along the edge of the trees and came back with an armload of dead branches, and bound them to the outside of his pack with cord.

"For tomorrow," he said. "There won't be any out there."

Noli came in out of the ferns at dusk and went round the fence twice, and lay down close outside it, against the thorns.

Out past her the savanna went away into the last of the light, green going gray, flat to the edge of the sky.

Teva untied the cloth. There was a handful of pinyons in it and no more, and she counted them out into three heaps on the cloth and pushed one to each of them. They ate them one at a time, slowly, to make them last, and they did not last.

Then she uncorked the spare skin. There was honey in the bottom of it still, enough to cover a hand.

She fed her suit first, without thinking, a little into the feeding well at her belly, because it had been eating her for three days. "Yours," she said to Benal, and held the skin until he had opened his own well and she had given it some.

Then she held the skin out to Benal again, the neck of it tipped, and waited.

He looked at her, then put out a finger and took a little honey on it. It was what she did at home in the evenings: held out the cup and waited while Omya drank, and did not take it away until she had finished. Her mother had done it with a wooden spoon, a long time ago, held out a taste and waited. Teva thought of the spoon for a moment and let it go. She was not thinking about what her hands were doing at all.

"Thank you," Benal said gravely, the way he would have thanked someone for a meal.

She held it out to Keo last.

He looked at her. He looked at her a moment longer than a taste of honey needed. Then he took it and licked his finger and said, "Is that all I get?" It was meant as a joke. It came out careful, as if he had picked each word up and checked it before he put it down.

"You're tired," Teva said. "You sound strange."

He laughed, too quickly. "I'm tired," he agreed.

She corked the empty skin and put it away. Keo was still looking at the fire.

Benal was asleep almost before he had finished his honey, sitting up with his back against the trunks. Teva took the first watch. Keo was to have the second, and Benal the third, but Keo did not lie down. He sat with his knees up on the other side of the fire, feeding it a stick at a time, and they talked in low voices about the savanna.

"Faster walking," Keo said again. "No climbing. No trees."

"No shade."

"We'll go early. We'll be across it in two days."

Benal stirred against the trunks. "How far have we come?" he said, without opening his eyes.

Teva thought about it. "More than fifty since the tangle," she said. "Less than sixty."

Then, because it was true, and because it had been sitting in her all day, she said, "The river's behind us."

"Yes," Keo said.

That was all either of them said. Benal was asleep again. The fire ticked. Out past the stakes, Noli shifted and settled, and the savanna lay in the dark with nothing standing up in it anywhere.
