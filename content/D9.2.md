The cycads were old and close-grown, their spined fronds most of a fence already.

They staked the gaps, all three slow and stupid with tiredness, and Keo made a fire.

Noli came in out of the ferns at dusk, circled the fence twice, and lay down outside it, against the thorns.

Teva untied the cloth: a handful of pinyons and no more. She divided them on the cloth, and they were gone before the fire had settled.

Then she uncorked the spare skin. A little honey still clung to the bottom.

She fed her suit first, without thinking, a little into the feeding well at her belly; its film had gone three days without sugar. "Yours," she said to Benal, and held the skin until he had opened his own well and she had given it some.

Then she held the skin out to Benal again, the neck tipped, and waited.

He looked at her, then put out a finger and took a little honey on it. It was what she did at home in the evenings: held out a sip on her mother's wooden spoon and waited while Omya took it. Her mother had once held out tastes to Teva from that same spoon. Teva thought of the spoon and let it go. She wasn't thinking about what her hands were doing.

"Thank you," Benal said gravely, as if for a meal.

She held it out to Keo last.

He looked at her a moment longer than a taste of honey needed. Then he took it and licked his finger. "Is that all I get?" It sounded like a joke. It came out careful.

"You're tired," Teva said. "You sound weird."

He laughed, too quickly. "I'm tired," he agreed.

She corked the empty skin. Keo was looking at the fire.

Benal was asleep almost before he'd finished his honey, sitting up against a cycad's scaly trunk. Teva took the first watch, Keo the second, Benal the third, but Keo didn't lie down. He sat with his knees up across the fire, feeding it a stick at a time, and they talked low about the savanna.

"Faster walking," Keo said again. "No climbing. No trees."

"No shade."

"We'll go early. We'll be across it in two days."

Benal stirred. "How far have we come?" he said, without opening his eyes.

"Fifty-four since the tangle," Teva said.

Then she said, "The river's behind us."

"Yes," Keo said.

Benal was asleep again. Over the last trees, one star had come out. The fire ticked. Out past the stakes Noli shifted and settled, and the savanna lay in the dark with nothing standing up in it anywhere.
