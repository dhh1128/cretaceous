The sky came out all at once, with nothing in the air to stop it, and it was enormous. It went down to the trees on every side, so thick with stars that the sky seemed crowded, though the ground beneath it stayed dark. None of them looked up.

They had camped well back from the water, on a rise where the ginkgos grew so close that their trunks made most of a wall, and had put the fence across the gaps and gelled the stakes. Noli had gone out beyond the smell of it and settled in a hollow facing the river. Keo had the first watch. He would have had it anyway. He sat with his back to a stake and his spike across his knees and the river below him, flat and black, with the stars lying in it, and he did not look at the stars in the river either.

He reached up the bank instead.

The herd was there, in the dark above the narrows, where it had come down to drink at dusk. He could feel it: a great slow crowd of warm presences, close together, large, many. He could not pick one out of it. Somewhere in there was the one he would have to drive into the slow water tomorrow. He did not know which. He would not know until he had it.

He did not push. Pushing cost, and tomorrow he was going to need all of it. Tomorrow he had to drive one of them out into the slow water, deeper than it wanted to go, with its whole body telling it to get out and him telling it no, until the river had it.

He had pushed a wild one before, a little way, and it had cost him. He had never pushed one to its death.

The pressure was already there behind his eyes, as if he had been pushing all day. He knew it was not real yet. He rubbed his forehead with the heel of his hand and it did not go.

Teva was awake. He had known she would be. She sat a little way along the fence with her back to a ginkgo and her knees up, looking at the river.

Not at the sky. He watched her for a long time, and in all that time she did not once look up, not even when Benal shifted behind them to look up. She did not lift her head. She looked at the water below them, and at the line of the stones across it, and at nothing else.

She was afraid of it, he thought. Of course she was. She had been afraid of it all day; he had watched her stop dead thirty paces from the mud and turn around and come back and sit down. She was sitting there making herself look at the thing she was most afraid of, all night, so that tomorrow it would be a thing she had already looked at.

"The seventh's up," he said. "Your field. I think. Over the trees."

"I know," she said, and did not look.

*We'll be fine* came up in him out of habit and he let it go back down, because she would have heard it for what it was. "You should sleep," he said instead.

"So should you."

"Nobody should sleep," Benal said from behind them. "Statistically. Somebody has to watch the river not do anything."

"You sleep, Ben," Keo said. "If anything comes, it'll want the delicious one first."

"That's why I'm awake."

"Will there be a moon?" Keo said.

"Before dawn," Teva said, without looking up. "Thin."

"There's honey left," Benal said. "In the skin."

"In the morning," Keo said. "Before."

"Before," Benal agreed.

After a while he lay down, but his breathing stayed shallow under the bruise across his chest and did not slow into a sleeper's.

Out on the river, once, far off, something went into the water with a long heavy sound. Every one of them went still, and then slowly was not still again.

Last night he had slept the whole night through. Tonight his body kept waiting for the water to move.

When his three hours ended, Teva was already sitting up. He passed her the spike and lay down. Above the narrows a branch broke, and the herd shifted through his sense as one crowded presence; he waited for a single animal to come apart from it, and none did. He shut his eyes without sleeping.

The last time he had pushed at anything with everything he had, it had been a croc, in a flooded grove at home, with his sister beside him, and it had not turned its head. Tomorrow he would not be pushing at the crocs at all. He would be giving them something.

Through each change of watch he heard the others move, and none of them sounded asleep.

The sky went gray at the edges a long time before anything else did. Then the river came out of the dark, flat and pale and still, exactly as it had been, with the stones standing in it.

Today they had to go into it.
