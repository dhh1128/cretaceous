Noon came straight down through the windscreen. Keo sat forward in the left seat, his back held off the bare metal where the linings had been, too hot to lean on. Sweat ran down his sides. The cabin was as hot as the stones round a cook pit, and it stank of metal and sweat.

Teva sat beside him with her eyes shut. The wig rocked on its frame as Benal climbed in behind, chest heaving from the run, something gray under his arm that Keo had never seen before. He folded himself into the seat behind Keo's and laid it across his knees.

The number on the panel stood a little short of ninety.

"What's the charge?" Keo said.

"Range at current charge, about one hundred and eight kilometers."

Benal leaned forward between the seats. "Eighty-eight home." He breathed. "Then the lift off here, and the units once more at the wall. Twenty over, less those. Fifteen, roughly."

"That's more than at dawn," Keo said.

"It's more. It isn't much."

He could feel the heat coming off Teva's arm from a hand's width away. Her body could not shed heat, and nothing would move the air but going.

"Do we go?" he said to her.

"Go."

He looked round. "Ben?"

"Go," Benal said.

He did not ask the voice.

There was dirt in the seam round one end of the thing on Benal's knees.

"What's that?"

"Later," Benal said.

There was no room in Keo for it.

"Disconnect the charge lead," the voice said.

He climbed out into the glare, yanked the cord from the hull's side, and climbed back into the heat.

"Fasten harness."

"A qualified pilot is preferred."

"I know," Keo said. "There isn't one. The water's gone."

"Hold the stick at center. Lift units engaging."

All four came up at once, through the seat into his spine and his rib, and the whole hull shook. The floor went light. His stomach stayed on the concrete while the rest of him rose. Then the wig slid sideways, and the store swung into the windscreen, close enough to see the rubble in its banking.

"Correct left," the voice said.

He shoved the stick over, too hard and too late. The mound swung away and the cabin tipped his way.

"Less," the voice said. "Less."

He eased it, and the cabin came level. The ramp went under them, then the still water inside the reef, and the scream of the units began to drop.

"Nose down. Transition to cruise."

He pushed. The wig dipped and kept dipping. The water came up at the windscreen and the hull hit it once, a slap that snapped his jaw shut, and everything went white.

"Water contact. Logged."

The spray ran off. The hull held a meter over the streaming water. The units died, and in their place came a lower roar, steady, from something at the back he could not see.

"Lift units off. Main propulsor at cruise."

"Propulsor," Keo said. His knuckles, torn on the mounts that morning, had opened again on the stick.

The hull groaned once along its whole length, taking its own weight on the air.

Benal had his eyes on the panel over Keo's shoulder. "It's still going down. That's the reserve filling again, from the cells. That's what the lift cost. We need the units once more, at home. The tangle's as tall as this thing climbs."

"Then nobody touches them till the tangle," Keo said. "Keep the reserve full."

"Reserve charging," the voice said.

Under the propulsor, Benal was humming.

Keo knew the song before he knew he was listening: Liaso's, the long one, which had last come to him through the fever, a line at a time, from Benal. He came in on the next line with the words, through his teeth, because the shaking rattled his jaw when he opened it. Benal hummed under him, off the note. It was the worst either of them had ever sung it. They kept on.

Teva let them sing. Then she leaned forward against her straps and pointed. "The rock. There. At the end of the beach."

He found it, a notch where the sea met the land, and put the nose on it.

Now well past the reef, the sea came at the windscreen faster than anything he had ever seen move. The swells slid under the hull before he could see their shape. He held the stick in both hands and did not dare loosen his fingers.

Something rose into his sense from below, a mind as big as the hadrosaur he had pushed into the pool at the narrows. He reached to read its kind, and it was already behind them. Nothing had ever outrun his sense before.

They had crossed this water in a day and a night on a split deck. It went under them in minutes, and he could not slow it down to think.

The rock grew on the line ahead, a mark anyone could steer on. Beyond it there would be nothing to see but trees.

"Past the rock," Teva said, "put my north at our backs and keep it there. That's home."

It was not the way they had walked, round swamps and along banks and back, for days. It was the line all of that had kept coming back to, turned round.

On the boat, in the rain, he had asked her which way the station was, and she had said it was gone.

"You're sure?" he said.

"Yes."

She sat with one hand flat on her knee and her eyes on the coast, and let it stand.

He steered on her word.

The coast came up out of the glare, a line that thickened and broke into trees, palms first, standing up out of the shimmer. At the end of the beach the rock rose, higher every moment, where they had dragged the boat down to the water and put out to sea.
