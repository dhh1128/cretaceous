Noon came straight down through the windscreen. Keo sat forward in the left seat with his back held off the wall, because where the linings had been the bare metal was too hot to lean on. Sweat ran down his sides under his tunic. The cabin was as hot as the stones round a cook pit, and it smelled of metal and sweat.

Teva sat beside him with her eyes shut. The wig rocked on its frame as Benal climbed in behind, his chest heaving from the run, with something gray under his arm that Keo had never seen before. He folded himself into the seat behind Keo's and laid it across his knees.

The number on the panel stood a little short of ninety.

"What's the charge?" Keo said.

"Range at current charge, about one hundred and eight kilometers."

Benal leaned forward between the seats. "Eighty-eight home." He breathed. "Then the lift off here, and the units once more at the wall. Twenty over, less those. Fifteen, roughly."

"That's more than at dawn," Keo said.

"It's more. It isn't much."

Keo turned to Teva. "What was left in the hollows?"

"A mouthful each, at first light." She opened her eyes. "They're dry."

He could feel the heat coming off her arm from a hand's width away. Her body could not get rid of heat. Nothing in the cabin moved the air. Only going would do that.

"Do we go?" he said to her.

"Go."

He looked round. "Ben?"

"Go," Benal said.

The panel glowed and waited. He did not ask it.

His eyes went to the thing on Benal's knees. It was as long as a forearm, and there was dirt in the seam round one end.

"What's that?"

"Later," Benal said.

There was no room in Keo for it. He turned back to the panel.

"Disconnect the charge lead," the voice said.

He got out into the glare and pulled the cord from the hull's side. It dropped onto the concrete. He climbed back in and shut the door, and the heat closed round him again.

"Fasten harness."

He dragged the straps over his shoulders and pushed their ends together until they caught. Beside him Teva did hers.

"A qualified pilot is preferred."

"I know," Keo said. "There isn't one. The water's gone."

"Hold the stick at center. Lift units engaging."

All four came up at once. Together they went through the seat into his spine and found his rib, and the whole hull shook with it. The floor went light under him. His stomach stayed on the concrete while the rest of him rose. Then the wig was sliding sideways. The store swung into the edge of the windscreen, close enough to see the rubble in its banking.

"Correct left," the voice said.

He shoved the stick over, too hard and too late. The mound swung away and the whole cabin tipped his way.

"Less," the voice said. "Less."

He eased it, and the cabin came level. The ramp went under them, then the still water inside the reef, and the scream of the units began to drop.

"Nose down. Transition to cruise."

He pushed. The wig dipped and kept dipping. The water came up at the windscreen and the hull hit it once, a slap that snapped his jaw shut, and everything went white.

"Water contact. Logged."

The spray ran off. The water was a meter under them, going by, and the hull held there. The units died. In their place was a roar behind him, lower and steady, from something at the back he could not see.

"Lift units off. Main propulsor at cruise."

"Propulsor," Keo said. His knuckles, torn on the mounts that morning, had opened again on the stick.

The hull groaned once along its whole length, taking its own weight on the air.

Benal had his eyes on the panel over Keo's shoulder. "It's still going down. That's the reserve filling again, from the cells. That's what the lift cost, and it's still costing. It'll go on dropping for a few minutes. We need them once more. At home. The tangle's as tall as this thing climbs."

"Then nobody touches them till the tangle," Keo said. "Keep the reserve full."

"Reserve charging," the voice said.

Under the propulsor, Benal was humming.

Keo knew the song before he knew he was listening. It was Liaso's, the long one. It had last come to him through the fever, a line at a time, from Benal. He came in on the next line with the words. He sang them through his teeth, because when he opened his jaw the shaking rattled it. Benal hummed under him, off the note. It was the worst either of them had ever sung it. They kept on.

Teva let them sing. Then she leaned forward against her straps and pointed. "The rock. There. At the end of the beach."

He found it, a notch in the far line where the sea met the land, and put the nose on it.

The reef came at them as a line of surf and went under. Somewhere down there the boat had come apart under them five nights ago. Then it was behind, and he had not finished looking.

Past the reef the sea came at the windscreen faster than anything he had ever seen move. The swells slid under the hull before he could see their shape. He held the stick with both hands and did not dare loosen his fingers.

Something came up into his sense from below. It was a mind, as big as the hadrosaur he had pushed into the pool at the narrows. He reached for it to read what kind it was, and it was already behind them. Nothing had ever gone by him faster than his sense could follow.

It had taken them a day and a night to cross this water on a split deck. It was going under them in minutes, and he could not slow it down to think.

The rock grew on the line ahead. It was a mark anyone could steer on. Beyond it there would be nothing to see but trees.

"Past the rock," Teva said, "put my north at our backs and keep it there. That's home."

It was not the way they had walked. They had gone round swamps and along banks and back, for days. It was the line all of that had kept coming back to, turned round.

On the boat, in the rain, he had asked her which way the station was, and she had said it was gone.

"You're sure?" he said.

"Yes."

She sat with one hand flat on her knee and her eyes on the coast, and let it stand.

He steered on her word.

The coast came up out of the glare. A line along the water thickened and broke into trees, palms first, standing up out of the shimmer. At the end of the beach the rock rose, higher every moment, where they had dragged the boat down to the water and put out to sea.
