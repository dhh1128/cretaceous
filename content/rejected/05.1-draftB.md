Keo’s breath plumed, cold and fast, dissolving instantly into the ambient fog. Unsuited skin, already tacky with humidity, tightened against the pre-dawn chill. He rubbed his arms, feeling the abrasive grit of mud and the slick coating of the pheromone gel, the synthetic scent of wounded alpha, which clung to him like a second, rancid skin. The sick-sweet chemical cloy sat heavy in his throat, barely masking the cleaner, sharper smell of fear that radiated off Benal.

Benal stood rigid, his spine pressed against the moss-covered buttress root of a towering Araucaria. The razor-club felt impossibly light, useless in his hands—a weapon designed for swinging impact, not for silent, desperate defense. The living chromatophore skin of his suit had gone matte black, an absolute shadow against the low fog that smelled of slick mud and crushed pine needles.

Inside the helmet, Benal’s super-hearing felt like a magnified curse. The suit filtered the static insect drone, but the silence it created was worse: it magnified the minute, terrifying sounds of the world closing in. He could hear the drip of condensation off cycad leaves, the microscopic *thrum* of blood in his own ears, and the low-frequency acoustic shadow of the earth itself, all competing with the faint, dry click.

“Razortail,” Benal subvocalized. The name was a thread pulled too tight. “Fifty meters. Coming to the scent line. Two, maybe three. The click. Bone-dry. It’s a hunt code.”

Teva spun, croc-spike level. Her suit had shifted to a swirling, mottled green-brown. “Direction. Precise.”

“Shifts. Scent warps.” Benal tracked the rankness of their pheromone barrier blowing back from the north, then the raptors' hot musk from the east. “They are confused by the T-Rex scent. Wary, not scared. Weakly south-east.”

Keo funneled the energy of his own panic into a single, sharp current. *Noli. Down.* He sent the command: a crushing weight, a silent, absolute command against the bambiraptor’s instincts. Noli, terrified by the pheromone barrier, coiled into a statue of mute submission at his ankle. Keo focused hard, partitioning the corner of his mind that held the fear. Pressure flared behind his eyes, a spike of static, a psychic hammer slamming down on her will. *Hold. Hold.*

A twig snapped. A brittle, hard crack from the direction Benal had named. The sound was too close, too crisp. Silence followed, a suffocating vacuum. The air tasted of wet wool.

They held the position for what felt like an hour. Minutes stretched into wire. Keo counted the frantic rhythm of his pulse, his unsuited skin prickling with cold. He pressed back against the root, feeling the cold sap heat from his tunic. *This is the cost,* he thought, the truth cold and sharp as a chert knife. *I'm the weak point. I'm the vulnerability.* His decision to rely on his natural thermal regulation upgrade rather than the suit, born of pride and a desire to prove himself unreliant on technology, felt reckless and stupid. He could feel the cold radiating from the ground, the humidity seeping into his joints.

Inside his helmet, Benal fought paralysis. *Why are they hesitating?* He cycled the data, trying to override the physical terror that threatened to lock his muscles. The intellectual shame was a worse enemy than the raptors. His abstract work was useless here. His physical frailty was a liability. If they died, it would be because his body failed them, not his mind. He had to be useful. *The pheromone is working.* The raptors were drawn to the carrion smell, but repelled by the T-Rex alpha component. *They are circling, testing the boundaries of the threat.* They would take the easy damage. He was the most fragile, but Keo was the most exposed.

Benal mentally reviewed the contents of his field pack, the heavy, water-repellent polymer slung across his back. He had the chemical pods, small canisters of dried crawlers and pinyons. *Wasting a pod is worse than death. The risk of revealing our tech is too high.* He dismissed the idea with a shudder, his failure in abstract thought a familiar comfort: stick to the known variables.

Teva was the absolute anchor. Her spike held steady. She projected nothing but absolute mission resolve. Benal knew she was using her magnetic sense and proprioception to calculate the fastest escape route against the wind line. Her control was total, a cold rage focused entirely on the path ahead: *Monsters in the water*.

Click... shhh... click. The dry sound was definitely receding now.

Keo felt a thread of hope. The pheromone barrier was holding. He strained his Enhanced Vision, trying to pierce the fog, but the gloom was absolute.

Then the raptors' musky reek slammed into them again. It was stronger, a clear vector from the south-east.

“Wait,” Teva commanded, her voice flat.

But Benal knew. He tracked the secondary scent: freshly turned, cool soil. *They found our tracks. They are moving to cut us off.* “Back,” he hissed, the terror returning. “They circled. Found the line. South-east. We failed.”

Keo, who had been waiting for the signal, sprang into action. He needed an immediate, high-value diversion. He needed chaos. He locked eyes with Benal.

“Benal! South-west! Find a stone!”

Benal, fueled by the clarity of his purpose, darted two steps back, reaching the moss-gathered base of the laurel. He tore a sharp-edged piece of limestone from the bark.

Keo pointed, sending a mental command. *Hard. Behind the ferns!*

Benal threw the rock. His throw was clumsy, fueled by terror, but precise. It struck the ground behind a clump of waist-high ferns with a stomach-turning *thud-squelch* that perfectly mimicked prey being dragged into the wet earth.

Click-click-chitter! The three raptors pivoted, their attention fixed on the sound.

Keo used the pause. He slammed his mind outward, not at the raptors, but at a terrified, rabbit-sized **Grounder** he sensed lurking nearby. *RUN. PANIC! NOW!*

The Grounder shot out into the open, a blur of fur and terror.

The raptors’ instinct for easier, low-risk prey overrode the high-risk scent line. They vanished into the fog with a rush of iridescent feathers and a chorus of excited chitters.

The resulting silence was absolute.

Keo slumped against the root, the iron clutch of psychic pressure behind his eyes fading. His hands shook. He was alive.

Teva lowered her spike. Her mottled suit shifted to a steady, pale pink in the cold light.

Benal, wheezing and trembling, leaned against the tree. He retrieved his stone, clutching it like a trophy.

Teva turned to both of them, her voice calm on the external mic. “Your analysis of the pheromone as a tactical hesitation was correct, Benal. That was what bought us the time to deploy. Keo, the timing on the grounder was precise. It took all of us to sell the panic.” She paused, and the look she gave them—a decisive, almost proud nod—was the truest reward. **“I chose well. Both of you.”**

Keo, exhausted but grateful, put a hand on Benal’s still-shaking shoulder. “You saved us, Benal. I was just the megaphone for the plan you built.” He smiled, the bravado replaced by honest weariness.

Benal felt the shame of his earlier panic dissolve in the wave of relief and the uncomplicated pride of being needed. His body still shook, but his mind felt keen and purposeful. The abstract knowledge, the math that Marek dismissed, was the only thing that had saved their lives.

“Move south-east,” Benal said, his voice stronger now. “Against the wind. We follow the wind line back to the coast.”

Teva nodded, already turning to take point. Benal, shoulders back, silently followed. They stepped out of the fog, two shadows following a third, disappearing into the humid, dark thickness of the jungle. Their mission had begun in betrayal and guilt, but their journey had just found its purpose.
