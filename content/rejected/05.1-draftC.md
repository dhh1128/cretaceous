## 5.1

Benal did not hear the first sound, but he felt the subtraction of a sound.

The omnipresent thrum of the perimeter's sonic dampeners—a technology designed to calm the smaller herbivores around the Enclave—vanished. The silence was a crushing noise in his augmented ears, a void that screamed danger. The Acheroraptor pack had breached the outer defense.

“Run,” Teva’s voice hissed, a sheet of ice, from the collar of his helmet.

Benal’s body could not execute the command. His suit could, but the complex sensory input—the feel of the synth-fibers on his skin, the mechanical whir of the suit's joints, the rush of cool, filtered air—fought the panic. He was too slow.

Keo shoved him. Not with his hands, but with a furious wave of concentrated thought. Move.

Benal stumbled into motion. He crashed through the tangle of felled trees and armored cycads that marked the border, the razor-club scraping against the polymer of his suit.

The sound of their escape was deafening to him. Keo crashed through the brush with only his skin for defense. Noli, the companion bambiraptor, a blur of feather and muscle, darted ahead. And himself: every footfall a thump-squelch in the damp earth, every breath a metallic hiss-intake from the lightcell-powered suit.

The raptors. He listened past the noise of his own flight. Their bone-dry clicking was gone. Replaced by the shh-shh-shh of low-growth ferns being sliced apart by heavy movement. They were following the sound. He adjusted the suit’s internal sound filter, but it did little good. He heard the acrid scent of ozone from the activated defense grid on the perimeter, followed by a stronger, visceral smell: ammonia and wet musk, the territorial signature of the pack.

He tried to match Keo’s pace, but his suit wasn't a running machine; it was a pressurized, self-contained box. It gave him strength, but Keo had the metabolic optimization upgrade that Benal lacked. Keo moved like wind through the trees, a natural entity; Benal moved like a machine grinding the earth.

“We turn left,” Teva commanded, her voice steady and magnetic. Her internal compass, the magnetic sense upgrade, was already working. “South-west swell. Need high ground. Now.”

Benal forced his legs to turn, pushing through a curtain of palm fronds.

The Acheroraptors were smarter than mere instinct. Benal heard the click-click resume: two notes, then silence. They weren't crashing anymore. They were flanking, hunting.

Wait. Benal stopped dead, fighting the urge to vomit. He didn't just hear the raptors. He heard Keo’s mind.

It was not a thought, but a physical force, a brand of pressure that slammed into Benal’s own skull before it was immediately redirected. Keo was partitioning his mind, throwing the focus outward, trying to paint a target for the pack—a phantom scent, a sudden flash of fear—to buy them time. The pressure behind Keo's eyes must have been shredding.

“Climb!” Keo shouted, his voice cracking from the physical strain.

They had reached a limestone ridge, steep and covered in slick, wet moss. Keo scrambled up unsuited, hands tearing at the stone. Teva used the polymer grip-spikes on her suit’s boots and ascended with the controlled ease of a seasoned Explorer.

Benal was last. He fumbled with the magnetic ascenders on his gloves, cursing the old-tech design.

Below him, the lead raptor appeared. It was smaller than he expected, bird-like, feathered in shades of mud and moss, but its eyes were cold, like polished pinyons. The creature’s head cocked, studying the upward angle. It emitted a thin, rattling hiss.

Focus, Benal. He pushed his panic down and locked the ascenders. His suit’s enhanced strength engaged, hoisting his frail frame up the slick rock.

Then, the raptor did not follow. It stopped at the base of the ridge, clicking. Its movement was too cautious.

“Keo,” Teva whispered from above. “What did you do?”

Keo, hanging from the ridge crest, didn’t answer immediately, frozen. His chest heaved as he forced the mental pressure back inside his own skull.

Finally, he pulled himself over the crest. “I gave it something else to think about. We need to move. Now.”

Benal reached the crest and collapsed onto the flat, drier ground behind the ridge. He wrenched his helmet open and gasped. The air was thick, wet, and smelled of decaying ferns, but it was real. His lungs burned. The adrenaline, which had carried him, dumped violently, leaving him spent. His super-senses, unfiltered, slammed him with noise: the incessant whir of mosquitos, the damp breath of the jungle, the low, pulsing thump-thump of the Acheroraptor’s retreat below them.

"You used projection on a pack of razortails?" Teva was pale, her helmet open too. Her skin was already beginning to flush pink, a sure sign of her poor thermal regulation kicking in, despite the cool morning.

Keo was grinning, an arrogant display of teeth. “It worked. I told you, I’m good at it.” He patted Noli’s head. The small bambiraptor was already back to patiently hunting large crawlers.

Benal threw up, a thin, bitter spray of partially digested journey-pan onto the moss.

“No, Keo,” Teva said, stepping back from Benal’s mess. Her face was set, the color of wet stone. “It didn’t work. We broke the core mission’s rule one meter from the perimeter, you nearly blew out your own brain, and we got lucky because they couldn’t get a clear footing. You didn't control it. You shocked it.”

Keo’s smile died. “I got us out.”

“No,” Benal gasped, wiping his chin. He zipped his suit back up, needing the internal environmental controls to stop the shaking. His voice was a pathetic wheeze. “She’s right. I heard it. You only confused the one. The rest of the pack hesitated. If they had worked together, we’d be carne now”.

He finally looked at Keo, shame and terror twisting his stomach. “We’re out here. And we’re slow. And you are a reckless, unsuited idiot. We should have waited.”

Keo, still buzzing on his self-created adrenaline, took the hit, his expression hardening. His bravado, once armor, was now a thin, brittle shell. “We waited, Teva’s grandmother would be gone. And the cultures with her. Now we move. You can argue when the sun climbs higher.”

He turned and pushed off the ridge into the deeper jungle, not looking back. He had won the argument, but he had lost the camaraderie.

Teva looked at Keo's retreating back, then down at Benal, who was huddled and shaking. She zipped her helmet shut, the whir of the motor filling the air.

“Benal,” she said, her voice now flat and professional through the suit speakers. “If you need to throw up again, turn your head toward the wind. Don’t attract the flybeaks.”

She waited for a moment, then added, softer, through the external mic: “You were right to come. You are the only one who hears the truth out here.”

And then she followed Keo, leaving Benal alone to zip up his fieldpack and confront the terrible, noisy green wall of the jungle.
