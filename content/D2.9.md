"We need Ben," said Teva.

She said it to the dark past the groves, not to him. Keo heard it, and the answer was in his head before it reached his mouth. Ben could barely work a shift in the gardens. How could he last outside the Enclave?

"Teva, he'd —"

"You'll have me."

The voice came from behind them, unsteady. They both turned.

Benal unfolded himself from the dark against the rock beside the hide, all height and elbows, and came forward a step into what light there was. Keo had felt somebody standing there the whole time and had not cared who. Benal was shaking. The mild night could not have made him shake like that; he shook the way a yazhi does at the edge of the pool the first time.

"I — I heard you." He swallowed. "I was — waiting. To see if —"

He did not finish it. He looked past them both, out toward where the tangle was.

"Is Liaso on watch tonight?"

"Maybe," Keo said. He did not know. "Why?"

"He's my cousin." Benal's voice was hardly there. "He told me once — he said the ground outside breathes. He swears a T. rex stood across a clearing and looked at him for an hour. It didn't come. It didn't go. It just looked at him, and then it turned round and walked away, and he couldn't stop shaking until the next day." He made a sound that might have been a laugh. "He tells it as a funny story."

Nobody laughed at it now.

"Go home, Ben," Keo said.

He meant it kindly. He kept his voice low, but it came out as an order, and he said the rest anyway, because Ben's life might depend on it. "You're nervous around Noli. You once told me you can't sleep at night because the jungle's too loud. And your venom resistance didn't take. This isn't algebra."

"And at Genesis?" said Benal.

The shake had gone out of his voice, all at once.

"What will you do at Genesis, Keo? Hit it with your spear? Will you, Teva? Will you use your magnetic sense to navigate an old hard-tech interface?" He took a breath. "Genesis isn't a place you fight your way into. It's a place you read. It's old-tech, it's archives, it's data, it's the founders' own work, written in the founders' own symbols, and I'm possibly the only person alive who can make sense of what they left." He looked from one of them to the other. "You can get there. I believe that. You're the delivery system. I'm the key."

Teva had not taken her eyes off him.

"What does it do," she said. "Your work. What does it actually do?"

Keo looked at her. In sixteen years he had never heard anyone ask Ben that.

Benal stared at her before he answered. Then he told her, in sentences that went on and folded back on themselves: about the founders and the jump, about something he called an anchor and something he called drift, and about a term in his equation that would not come out right however many times he rubbed it out. "Their figures give a date," he said, "and the sky won't agree with it, and the part that would make them agree is the part I keep rubbing out." Keo understood perhaps one word in five. Teva did not interrupt him once.

Keo watched Benal while he talked. His hands still trembled, and once he wiped his face with his wrist. Every so often his eyes went past them to the dark where the tangle was, and came back. He had listened from beside the hide, frightened sick, and stepped out anyway.

Keo had known Ben all his life. He had carried Ben's share of a load more than once when they were small and walked slower for him without saying so. He had never once thought of him as anyone who would do a thing like this. He thought it now, and felt a strange emotion.

"— so the anchor," Benal was saying, "the place they tied the jump to. The place fits. The date doesn't. The anchor is — possibly — unmoored. And the Council is in there arguing about a way home that's possibly not even —"

"Possibly," said Teva.

She said it perfectly flat, exactly as he had, and Benal stopped with his mouth open.

Keo laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, the first time he had laughed since the morning. They all knew Benal's favorite word. Benal looked at him, and then, very slightly, the corner of Benal's mouth went too.

Maybe the word was worth hearing.

"Okay," Keo said. "Okay. Ben."

Then he was counting, the way his head had always worked. "But we can't go like this. Teva, you'll cook in the first open country, you know you will. And you —" He looked at Benal's thin arms and his hands. "A stingburn, a fall. We won't make it on what we are." It turned his stomach even as he said it. "We need suits."

Benal glanced back at the hide and dropped his voice. "Suits? The Repository? Keo, your parents are the custodians. If even one of them goes missing —"

"I know whose trust I'm breaking."

He said it more harshly than he meant to, and under his breath.

"Tonight," said Teva.

"Tonight," Keo said. "I'll get the suits. And food, from home." He thought it through as he said it, the way his parents planned a trip, one thing and then the next. Teva nodded. Benal nodded, slowly, as if the nod were a long way off and he had to go and fetch it.

"Bring what you can carry and nothing you can't," Keo said. "The place I take Noli in the mornings, by the rebuild. Before the fog lifts."
