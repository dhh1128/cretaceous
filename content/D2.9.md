"We need Ben," said Teva.

She said it to the dark past the groves. Keo had the answer before it reached his mouth. Ben could barely work a shift in the gardens. How could he last outside Enclave?

"Teva, he'd —"

"You'll have me."

The voice came from behind them, unsteady. They both turned.

Benal unfolded himself from the dark against the rock beside the hide and came forward a step, a long shape of elbows and height against the stars. Keo had felt somebody standing there the whole time and had not cared who. Benal was shaking like a yazhi at the edge of the pool the first time.

"I — I heard you." He swallowed. "I was — waiting. To see if —"

He looked past them both, toward the tangle.

"Is Liaso on watch tonight?"

"Maybe," Keo said. "Why?"

"He's my cousin." Benal's voice was hardly there. "He told me once the ground outside breathes. He swears a T. rex stood across a clearing and looked at him for an hour. It didn't come. It didn't go. It just looked at him, and then it turned around and walked away, and he couldn't stop shaking until the next day." He made a sound that might have been a laugh. "He tells it as a funny story."

"Go home, Ben," Keo said.

He meant it kindly. He kept his voice low, but it came out as an order, and he said the rest anyway, because Ben's life might depend on it. "You're nervous around Noli. You once told me you can't sleep at night because the jungle's too loud. And your venom resistance didn't take. This isn't algebra."

Benal flinched at *Noli*, which made Keo's point for him. "I know," he said. "I've done the odds. Unsuited, I'd say one in ten I reach Genesis. Suited, maybe one in two. Possibly."

Keo waited for him to turn and go home.

"But at Genesis?" Benal finally added.

The shake had gone out of his voice.

"What'll you do at Genesis, Keo? Hit it with your spike? Will you, Teva? Will you use your magnetic sense to navigate an old hard-tech interface? Genesis isn't a place you fight your way into. It's a place you read. It's archives, it's data, it's the founders' own work in the founders' own notation, and I might be the only one of us who can make sense of what they left. You can get there. I believe that. You're the delivery system. I'm the key."

"And it's on the water. Everybody knows the First Walk started on the turtle beach. What nobody remembers is that the ones who lost the argument made them put the boats up under the rock at the end of the beach, out of the sea's reach, so the way back would stay open. It's in the records." He swallowed. "From the rock they walked a day inland to a warm spring in the stone, and from there south to the narrows. Walk it backward and it ends at a boat."

Keo had heard the First Walk told a hundred times. Nobody had ever told it backward.

Teva hadn't taken her eyes off him.

"What does it do?" she said. "Your work. What does it actually do?"

Keo looked at her. He had never heard anyone ask Ben that, and want an answer.

Benal stared at her. Then he told her, in sentences that folded back on themselves: the founders and the jump, something he called an anchor and something he called drift, a term in his equation that would not come out right however many times he rubbed it out. Keo understood one word in five.

"Whose job is it to worry about all that stuff?" said Teva.

"Mine." Benal blinked at her. "The founders' half I could write in my sleep. It's my part that won't come right."

His hands were fidgeting, and once he wiped his face with his wrist. Every so often his eyes went past them to the dark where the tangle was, and came back.

He had listened to their plan, frightened sick, and stepped out anyway.

Keo had known Ben all his life. He had carried Ben's share of a load more than once when they were small, and walked slower for him without saying so. He had never once thought of him as anyone who would do a thing like this. He thought it now, and felt a strange emotion.

"— so the anchor," Benal was saying, "the place they tied the jump to. The place fits. The date doesn't. The anchor is — possibly — unmoored. And the Council is arguing about a way home that's maybe not even —"

"Possibly," said Teva.

She said it perfectly flat, exactly as he had, and Benal stopped with his mouth open.

Keo laughed, the first time since the morning. They all knew Benal's favorite word. Benal looked at him, and then, very slightly, the corner of Benal's mouth went too.

Maybe the word was worth hearing.

"Okay," Keo said. "Okay. Ben."

Then he was counting. "But we can't go like this. Teva, you'll cook in the first open country, you know you will. And you —" He looked at Benal's thin arms. "A stingburn, a fall. We won't make it on what we are." It turned his stomach to say it. "You two need suits."

Benal glanced back at the hide and dropped his voice. "Suits? The Repository? Keo, your parents are the custodians. If even one goes missing —"

"I know whose trust I'm breaking," Keo said under his breath, harder than he meant to.

"Tonight," said Teva. She looked at Benal. "Before the Council makes it harder. You can tell me the rest on the way."

"Tonight," Keo said. "I'll get the suits. And food, from home." Teva nodded. Benal nodded slowly, as if he had to go a long way to fetch it.

"Bring what you can carry and nothing you can't," Keo said. "The place I take Noli in the mornings, by the rebuild. Before the fog lifts."
