The sun had come round onto the concrete outside the long mound, and the glare off it filled the open doors white. Keo knelt on the boat's floor by the open lid with his rib held still. Under the lid every jar was fizzing at once, a fine sound that never got louder, like the first of a rain on the yazhi pool at home. They had not done that on the night he filled them. He leaned closer and something sharp and soapy caught at the back of his nose, the same thing that had bitten his fingers, and he sat back on his heels.

On the panel the number had climbed past half.

Benal and Teva stood in the doors against the glare. Neither of them had come in.

Benal came first. He walked the length of it slowly, from the tail up under the roof to the nose, with one hand held a little way off its side. At the stubby wing he bent to look under it, then up at a round empty mount, and went on. At the nose he stood with his head on one side. Benal had a word for most things. He opened his mouth and shut it again.

Teva did not walk round it. She came straight to the step, climbed in past Keo and sat down in the seat beside the pilot's. The cover crunched under her. She looked out through the curved clear sheet at the front, past the doors and down the ramp to the still water inside the reef, and she stayed like that.

He had thought he would have to talk her in.

Keo got up and leaned between the seats to the panel. "What's the charge?" he said, loud enough for the doors.

The panel lit. "Main cell bank charging. Charge level fifty-three percent."

Benal stopped dead at the nose. Then he came round under the wing, ducking, and put his head in at the door.

"Th-that's—" He looked from the panel to Keo. "That's it. Talking. That's what you meant, at the fire? A voice that answers? Why didn't you say so properly?"

"I said," Keo mumbled. "I tried."

"You said there was a voice. You didn't say it was a voice like that."

Keo looked at the floor of the bay. Benal let it go. He climbed in and folded himself into the seat behind Teva's, and leaned forward between the two of them to the panel.

"What is this?" he said. "This craft. What is it called?"

"This craft is a wing-in-ground-effect craft. Common term: wig."

"A wig," Teva said.

"Wig," Keo said. It was nothing he had ever heard.

"What's a wig?"

"This is," Keo said. "It says."

Benal said the word over once, under his breath. Then he climbed out again and went back past the tail to the doors. He stood in the glare with his hand up over his eyes and looked at the sky.

"Ben?"

"Something I read." He came back in out of the sun. "Possibly nothing."

He leaned between the seats again. "Show me what's wrong with it."

"Operator command required."

Benal looked at Keo.

"Show the craft's faults," Keo said, the long way, which was how it liked things.

"Displaying logged faults."

Words ran up the panel, too fast for Keo, and Benal leaned in after them with his lips moving. So it took its orders from Keo, and Benal was the one who could read what it said back. Teva looked from one of them to the other, and one corner of her mouth went up.

Then Benal began to ask it things, which Keo knew was what he had come for.

"Why did the founders come here?"

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"What was the personnel modification for?"

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"You ask it," Benal said. "You're the one it listens to."

"Why did the founders come here?" Keo said.

"Operator access is maintenance only. That information is restricted to mission personnel."

"What would make me mission personnel?" Benal said.

"State your personnel identifier."

"I don't have one."

"Identifier not recognized."

"It did that to me all night," Keo said.

Benal sat back and thought, and then came at it from the side. "When did you last fly? In the station's years."

"Last flight: river survey, station year three. After landing, this craft was powered down for storage."

"And the station. When they put you to sleep. What was its water?"

"In the last station records before power-down, water plant output was below station consumption."

"Below what they drank," Benal said. He looked at Teva.

"That's why they walked," she said.

"It's a number, here. At home it's a story." He turned back to the panel. "And after that?"

"I do not know. This craft was powered down."

Teva had her eyes on the water down the ramp. "How does it get off the island?"

"On the lift units. Vertical lift from the concrete, then down onto the water to cruise. A planing run from the water is outside this hull's limits at its age."

"Lift units," she said.

"Lift units: fitted."

"They're not," Keo said. "There's nothing in the mounts. I told you."

"Logging a sensor fault."

"There's long boxes in the store," he said. "Along the back. Shut tight."

"Open one and describe the contents."

Teva's face had gone gray under the sweat, and she was breathing through her mouth. The air in the cabin had not moved since they came in.

"Go and sit in the doors," he said. "There's air there."

She looked at him, and went. She sat down against the doorframe in the shade, where the glare stopped, and put her head back against the metal.

The long boxes lay along the back wall of the store, four of them. They would have to come to the door, or the voice would never hear him. Benal went to one end of the first.

"I'll take this end," he said.

"That's the light end."

"Yes."

They dragged it on its edge along the floor. The rib found every pull and the bad ankle every step backward. At the door Keo stood with his hand flat against his side until he could breathe. The catches on the lid were stiff. He worked the first with both thumbs until it gave all at once and took skin with it. The lid came up heavy. Inside, whatever it had been packed in had gone to yellow crumbs, and it came away in their hands when they dug.

Under it was a ring, as wide as his arms could have gone round, with blades inside it set to a heavy middle, and a cord coiled at its side.

"There's a ring," he called toward the long mound. "Wide as I can reach. Blades inside it. Something heavy in the middle, and a cord."

"Lift unit," the voice said, faint through the doors. "Inside the crate is a humidity card. State its color."

There was a card tucked against the ring's side, a little one behind a clear cover, with spots on it. He held it up into the light from the door.

"Blue. Blue spots."

"Blue indicates dry. Turn the blades by hand. Report any binding."

At home a color told a Keeper whether a culture was alive. Here a color said a thing was dry, and the thing had never been alive at all.

He put one finger to a blade. It was hard and smooth, lighter than it looked, and warmer than stone. Nobody had carved it and nothing had grown it. It turned under his finger with a long whisper. Nothing caught.

"Nothing catches," he called.

"Read the serial plate on the unit housing."

There was a little plate set into the rim with marks cut in it. He bent to it. The first mark was a letter he knew. Then the next one turned itself round on him, as letters always had in lessons, and he started it again.

Benal, at his shoulder, read it out, letters and then a long run of numbers, quick and clear. He was looking at the plate.

"Unit confirmed," the voice said.

They did the other three the same, and every card was blue. At the last one the voice said, "Unit four confirmed. Lift units serviceable."

"You haven't run them," Keo said.

"Lift units serviceable."

He knew by now what its word was worth. It had told him the units were fitted, and that the boat out in the water was moored. He got his hands under one edge of the last ring and lifted, to see. The rib told him at once. It was a load for two.

"We'll leave them here," he said. "By the door. Where we can get at them tomorrow."

Back in the cabin he sat in the pilot's seat. What was under the cover crunched to grit, and under that it was hard and warm through his tunic.

"With lift units serviceable, this craft can be flown by a qualified pilot," the voice said. "A qualified pilot is preferred. I can train the operator."

"Train me," Keo said.

The panel went dark. Then it was water.

It was water seen from a meter up, sliding toward him and under him, with a dark line of ground beyond it. It was a picture, and it moved. A stick stood up from the floor between his knees. When he pushed it the water on the panel tipped, and when he let it go the water came level again.

"Hold the waterline at the lower third of the windscreen," the voice said.

"The what?"

"The windscreen. The clear panel in front of you."

He looked up at the curved clear sheet. "Windscreen."

"At one meter, ground effect carries most of the craft's weight. Cruise at one meter. Each meter higher costs charge. The lift units cost most. Use them to lift off. Do not use them otherwise."

"How high can it go?"

"Ceiling, about fifteen meters."

He eased it up, and the water on the panel dropped away.

"Climbing. Charge cost rising," the voice said.

He brought it back down. Then he let the water come up too fast, and the panel went white.

"Water contact. Logged. A qualified pilot is preferred."

The second time he knew it was coming and could not stop it.

"Water contact. Logged. A qualified pilot is preferred."

Behind him Benal made a sound that was nearly a laugh, and turned it into a cough.

"State destination," the voice said.

"Home."

"Destination not recognized."

He had known it would say that. He tried it another way. "The river. The one we crossed at the narrows. Do you know it?"

"The river was surveyed by this craft. The survey marked three sites along it as candidates for settlement. Describe the destination."

"Teva," he called. "It wants to know where home is."

She got up from the doorframe and came. She climbed in and sat down beside him again, in the warm air shut up in the cabin. Her lips were cracked white.

"There's a bend in it," she said to the panel. "A big one. The ground inside the bend is higher than the ground round it. It's about eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from the coast, walking." She stopped. "We walked it."

"Site two. Distance from this hangar, about eighty-eight kilometers."

"There's a leaf in the archive with the river on it," Benal said from behind. "A survey. I passed it on the racks, the first day. It has the bend marked, more or less there."

"So it's the same," Keo said.

"It's the same."

Teva had not taken her eyes off the panel. "Who flew it? The survey."

The voice gave a name for the pilot, a long founder name, and Keo lost the front of it again before it reached the end. Then it said, "Survey scientist: Cecilia Arroyo, lead geneticist. Survey objective: fresh water."

Keo knew that name. He had seen her face yesterday in the register, looking straight out of the metal.

"Cecilia Arroyo," Teva said, slowly, the whole of it, the long way.

Then she said nothing for a while. Her hand went out and lay flat on the edge of the panel, and stayed there. Behind them Benal had gone still. Keo waited. She did not go on, and he did not ask her to.

When she took her hand back, Benal cleared his throat. "How far does it go with three of us?"

"On a full charge, at rated load, about one hundred and twenty kilometers <!-- @WF-wig.range.claimed: 120km -->. Range falls as load rises. Three crew and cargo exceed rated load."

"What can come out?"

"Seat four. Seat four harness and stowage locker. Cabin lining panels."

Teva got out again, without being told, and went back to the shade by the doors.

Seat four was the one Benal had sat in, behind Teva's. Keo got the tool out of the locker in the store, the long one with the jaw at its end, and lay half under the seat on the cabin floor to get at its feet. There was a nut under each, rusted at its edges, and they came the hard way, a little at a time. Each turn went into the rib. He passed them back over his shoulder one by one.

Benal took one and turned it in his fingers. "What are you taking off?"

"Bolts," Keo said. "And nuts. The long one's a bolt. The round one goes on it."

"Bolt," Benal said, and held one up against the light from the doors, and looked at the grooves running round it. "Bolt. Nut."

The seat came free with a jerk. They carried it out between them and set it on the concrete just outside the doors, in the sun. Teva reached up from where she sat and took the nuts from Benal's hand.

"Nut," she said, and looked at it. "Like a pinyon?"

"That's what I said to it."

She almost smiled, and put them down on the seat in a little pile.

The straps came off after the seat, and then the little locker that had sat behind it. The lining panels came away from the cabin walls in long curved sheets, gone brittle at the edges, and under them were the bare ribs of the wig, close together and shining dully. The pile outside the doors grew.

"What can we carry," Benal said, "after all that?"

"Cargo allowance, about forty kilograms."

Benal went quiet. He stood in the doors and looked back up toward the crown, where the archive was, with all its shelves.

Keo went to the lid and looked in again at the fizzing. Then he looked at the number.

"The jars are taking it," he said. "Look. It's gone up again."

"Cells," the voice said.

"They're doing well, the jars."

"Cells," Benal said, from the doors.

"Jars," Keo said.

Benal shook his head at him, and then turned back to the panel. "When will it be full?"

"Full charge, about the middle of tomorrow."

Benal looked at the floor for a moment. "That's when the water's gone."

Teva got up. She came to the cabin door and leaned in at it, so that the air at her back was the air of the doors.

"Then we go at first light," she said. "With what it's got."

Keo turned to the panel. "How far does it go on what it'll have at first light?"

"Projected range at first light, about ninety-five kilometers." <!-- @WF-wig.range.firstlight: 95km -->

"Home's eighty," Benal said. "And the sea. Call it eighty-eight."

"That's over," Keo said.

"A few kilometers over. On its figure." Benal had both hands on the seat back. "If anything costs more than it thinks, a few kilometers is nothing. And if it comes up short, under us is the flood. On foot. With whatever's in it."

"And waiting is the water," Teva said. She looked at Keo.

"Keo?"

Yesterday, in the room by the inner door, he had been on her side in all of it.

"We wait," he said. "For more. More is the one thing it can give us."

She did not look away from him. "How much more is enough?"

"I don't know."

"Nobody does," Benal said.

Teva straightened up out of the door. "I'll count the water again at first light."

The light was going. The glare had gone off the concrete, and down the ramp the water had turned pink. Teva went out past the pile and up the slope toward the hollows to count.

Benal stood a moment in the doors.

"I'm going to find somewhere quiet," he said, and went the other way, up over the top of the crown, toward the sea side.

Keo sat in the pilot's seat. The picture had gone from the panel. Through the windscreen and the open doors a half moon stood high over the water, and the jars fizzed under the lid behind him. The number on the panel had climbed since the afternoon. It was a long way from full.
