The sun had come round onto the concrete outside the long mound, and its glare filled the open doors. Keo knelt on the boat's floor by the open lid, holding his rib still. Below him every jar fizzed at once, like the first of a rain on the yazhi pool at home. They hadn't done that the night he filled them. He leaned closer, and something sharp and soapy caught at the back of his nose, the same thing that had bitten his fingers.

Benal and Teva stood in the doors.

Benal walked its length, tail to nose, one hand held a little off its side. At the stubby wing he bent to look up at a round empty mount. Benal had a word for most things. He opened his mouth and shut it again.

Teva went straight to the step, climbed in past Keo and sat in the seat beside the pilot's. The cover crunched under her. She looked out through the windscreen, past the doors and down the ramp to the still water inside the reef.

He had thought he would have to talk her in.

Keo leaned between the seats to the panel. "What's the charge?" he said, loud enough for the doors.

The panel lit. "Main cell bank charging. Charge level fifty-eight percent."

Benal froze at the nose. Then he ducked under the wing and put his head in at the door.

"Th-that's—" He looked from the panel to Keo. "That's what you meant, at the fire? A voice that answers? Why didn't you say so properly?"

"I said," Keo mumbled. "I tried."

Benal folded himself into the seat behind Teva's and leaned forward between them.

"What is this?" he asked the panel. "What's it called?"

"This craft is a wing-in-ground-effect craft. Common term: wig."

"A wig," Teva said.

"What's a wig?" Benal said.

"This is," Keo said. "It says."

Benal said the word again under his breath. Then he climbed out and stood in the glare of the doors with a hand over his eyes, looking at the sky.

He came back and leaned between the seats. "Show me what's wrong with it."

"Operator command required."

Benal looked at Keo.

"Show the craft's faults," Keo said, the long way, as it liked things.

Words ran up the panel, too fast for Keo. Benal leaned in after them with his lips moving. Teva looked from one of them to the other, and one corner of her mouth went up.

"Why did the founders come here?" Benal said.

"That information is restricted to mission personnel."

Benal came at it from the side. "When did you last fly? In the station's years."

"Last flight: river survey, station year three."

"And the station's water, when they put you to sleep?"

"In the last station records before power-down, water building output was below station consumption."

"Below what they drank," Benal said. He looked at Teva.

"So they left because they were thirsty," she said.

Teva had her eyes on the water down the ramp. "How does it get off the island?"

"On the lift units. Vertical lift from the concrete, then down onto the water to cruise. A planing run from the water is outside this hull's limits at its age. Lift units: fitted."

"They're not," Keo said. "The mounts are empty. I told you."

"Logging a sensor fault."

"There's long boxes in the store," he said. "Along the back."

"Open one and describe the contents."

It was hot. Teva's face had gone gray under the sweat, and she was breathing through her mouth. The air in the cabin had not moved since they came in.

"Go sit in the doors," Keo said. "There's air there."

She sat against the doorframe in the shade, her head back against the metal.

Four long boxes lay along the store's back wall, too far back for the voice to hear. Benal went to one end of the first.

"I'll take this end," he said.

"That's the light end."

"Yes."

They dragged it on its edge to the door, where Keo stood with a hand pressed to his side until he could breathe. He worked the stiff catches with both thumbs. The first gave all at once and took skin with it. The lid came up heavy. Inside, the packing had gone to yellow crumbs that came away in their hands.

Under it lay a ring as wide as his arms could have gone round, blades set inside it to a heavy middle, and a cord coiled at its side.

He called it out toward the long mound, and the voice answered faint through the doors. "Lift unit. Inside the crate is a humidity card. You are standing between it and my camera. State its color."

A little card was tucked against the ring, spots behind a clear cover. He held it up to the light.

"Blue spots."

"Blue indicates dry. Turn the blades by hand. Report any binding."

At home a color told a Keeper whether a culture was alive. Here it said a thing was dry, and the thing had never been alive.

He touched a blade. It was hard and smooth, and warmer than stone. Nobody had carved it and nothing had grown it. It turned under his finger with a long whisper.

"Nothing catches," he called.

They did the other three the same. Every card was blue. "Lift units serviceable," the voice said.

"You haven't run them," Keo said.

"Lift units serviceable."

He knew by now what its word was worth. He got his hands under the edge of the last ring and lifted. The rib told him at once: a load for two.

"We'll leave them by the door," he said. "For tomorrow."

Back in the cabin he sat in the pilot's seat. It was warm through his tunic.

"With lift units serviceable, this craft can be flown by a qualified pilot," the voice said. "A qualified pilot is preferred. I can train the operator."

"Train me," Keo said.

The panel went dark. Then it was water seen from a meter up, sliding toward him and under him, a low line of ground beyond. A picture, and it moved. A stick stood up from the floor between his knees. When he pushed it the water tipped, and when he let go it came level.

"Hold the waterline at the lower third of the windscreen," the voice said.

"At one meter, ground effect carries most of the craft's weight. Cruise at one meter. Each meter higher costs charge. The lift units cost most. Use them to lift off. Do not use them otherwise."

"How high can it go?"

"Ceiling, about fifteen meters."

He eased it up, and the water dropped away. He brought it down too fast. The panel went white.

"Water contact. Logged. A qualified pilot is preferred."

The second time he knew it was coming and could not stop it.

"Water contact. Logged. A qualified pilot is preferred."

Behind him Benal nearly laughed, and turned it into a cough.

"State destination," the voice said.

"Home."

"Destination not recognized."

"The river. The one we crossed at the narrows."

"The river was surveyed by this craft. The survey marked three sites along it as candidates for settlement. Describe the destination."

"Teva," he called. "It wants to know where home is."

She came from the doorframe and climbed in beside him, into the shut-up air. Her lips were cracked white.

"There's a bend in it," she said to the panel. "A big one. The ground inside the bend is higher than the ground round it. It's about eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> from the coast, walking." She stopped. "We walked it."

"Site two. Distance from this hangar, about eighty-eight kilometers."

"There's a leaf in the archive with the river on it," Benal said from behind. "A survey. It has the bend marked, more or less there."

Teva's eyes stayed on the panel. "Who flew it? The survey."

The voice gave a name for the pilot, a long founder name, and Keo lost the front of it again before it reached the end. Then it said, "Survey scientist: Cecilia Arroyo, lead geneticist. Survey objective: fresh water."

Keo knew the name. Teva had said it at the hollows that morning.

"Cecilia Arroyo," Teva said, the whole of it.

Her hand went out and lay flat on the edge of the panel. Behind them Benal had gone still. Keo didn't ask.

When she took her hand back, Benal cleared his throat. "How far does it go with three of us?"

"On a full charge, at rated load, about one hundred and twenty kilometers <!-- @WF-wig.range.claimed: 120km -->. Range falls as load rises. Three crew and cargo exceed rated load."

"What can come out?"

"Seat four. Seat four harness and stowage locker. Cabin lining panels."

Teva climbed out and went back to the shade by the doors.

Seat four was the one Benal had sat in. Keo got the tool with the jaw from the locker in the store and lay half under the seat. There was a nut under each foot, and they came a little at a time, every turn in the rib. He passed them back over his shoulder.

Benal turned one in his fingers. "What are these?"

"Bolts," Keo said. "And nuts. The long one's a bolt. The round one goes on it."

Benal held one up to the light and looked at the grooves running round it. "Bolt. Nut."

The seat came free with a jerk, and they carried it out onto the concrete. Teva reached up from where she sat and took the nuts from Benal's hand.

"Nut," she said. "Like a pinyon?"

"That's what I said to it."

She almost smiled, and piled them on the seat.

The straps came off, and the little locker behind the seat. The lining panels came away from the cabin walls in long curved sheets, brittle at the edges, and left the wig's ribs bare. All of it went out onto the concrete.

"What can we carry," Benal said, "after all that?"

The voice gave him a weight. Benal stood in the doors and looked up toward the crown, where the archive was.

Keo looked in at the fizzing, and at the number. "The jars are taking it. It's gone up again."

"Cells," the voice said.

"Cells," Benal said, from the doors.

"Jars," Keo said.

Benal shook his head at him. "When will it be full?" he called to the panel.

"Full charge, tomorrow afternoon."

Benal looked at the floor. "The water's gone by the middle of the day."

Teva got up and came to the cabin door.

"Then we go at first light," she said. "With what it's got."

"How far on what it'll have at first light?" Keo asked the panel.

"Projected range at first light, about ninety-five kilometers." <!-- @WF-wig.range.firstlight: 95km -->

"Home's eighty," Benal said. "And the sea. Call it eighty-eight."

"That's over," Keo said.

"A few kilometers over, on its figure," Benal said. "If anything costs more than it thinks, that's nothing. And if it comes up short, under us is the flood. On foot. With whatever's in it."

"And waiting is the water," Teva said. She looked at Keo. "Keo?"

Yesterday, in the room by the inner door, he had been on her side.

"We wait," he said. "For more. More is the one thing it can give us."

She held his eyes. "How much more is enough?"

"I don't know."

"Nobody does," Benal said.

Teva straightened. "I'll count the water again at first light."

The glare had gone off the concrete. Down the ramp the water had turned pink. Teva went out past the pile and up the slope toward the hollows.

Benal stood a moment in the doors. "I'm going to find somewhere quiet," he said, and went the other way, over the top of the crown toward the sea.

Keo sat on in the pilot's seat. Out past the doors the water held the half moon's light. Behind him the jars fizzed. The number had climbed since the afternoon. It was a long way from full.
