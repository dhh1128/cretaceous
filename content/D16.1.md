Sun came in through the broken end of the roof and lay across Benal's face. He kept his eyes shut and listened. The wind had gone. Below the walkway slaps were gentler; mostly there was just water running off concrete into water, and out at the broken end the birds beginning their racket.

Keo was sitting up beside him, working the bad ankle round in both hands. Teva stood at the edge of the walkway, looking down.

"It's gone," she said.

The crusted backs lay in their rows on the concrete with weed draped over them, and the water lay only in the low places between.

They came down the stair and out over the crust on the sill. Between the shore and the crown, where all yesterday the flood had ruffled in the wind, the low ground had drained to bare rock, pitted and shining. Pools stood in its hollows. The crown rose beyond it, every edge of it sharp against an empty sky.

Benal went to the hollows along the shore first. They were dry. Where the rain had stood at dusk there was a damp stain in the bottom of each.

"The rain's gone," he said. "All of it."

"Gone where?" Keo said.

"Up. Or down. Through." He laid his palm on the shell. It was all holes, and the holes went down. "This ground keeps nothing. If any of it's held, it'll be up on the crown, in the hard rock."

"We're going up anyway." Teva looked at Keo's foot. "Can you climb on that?"

"If it isn't steep."

"Some way won't be."

They went out across the low ground. The rock was slick in places with a green skin of weed, and the sun was already hot on it. Some of the pools were no bigger than a hand and some as long as a nest's floor, shallow and clear to the bottom. Keo stopped at the edge of one and looked down at the shell and sand in it.

"That's the sea's," Benal said. "The surge left it."

"I know."

Keo went round it the long way, hopping on the ankle. It was a step across. Benal went round it too, and found that Teva had gone round the other side. Habit. Water was death.

They kept to the high side, well back from the edge of the water inside the ring. A gully wound up the crown's slope from there, and they climbed it at Keo's pace, Keo with a hand on Benal's shoulder wherever it steepened.

On the climb the morning came to Benal unfiltered. His suit had stopped taking the edge off what he heard days ago, and he had not told them. Up here the surf on the reef was a long way off and the birds were few. He could stand it. He had only his ears and his nose now.

The gully went over the brow, and the top of the crown opened in front of them.

Arches stood across it, row behind row: bare frames of metal, pitted and chalky, each a curve from the rock up and over to the rock again, with nothing stretched across them, like the ribs of a carcass the scavengers had finished with. Beyond them, along the crown's spine, long shapes lay end to end in a row. They were heaped with rubble and sand to their eaves, so that only their roofs showed above it.

"What do you think?" Keo said.

Teva did not answer. She stood on the brow with the sun on her face and looked.

Benal had already started counting. The numbers came, and he heard himself saying them aloud. "Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve."

"Ben."

"Thirteen. There are more behind those. Fourteen."

Keo laughed.

"They lived here," Teva said. She was smiling. "Don't stop, Ben. I want to know how many."

"Twenty," Benal said. "Twenty-one." Two hundred people <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> had lived up here, for three years <!-- @WF-genesis.occupancy: 3y -->, and they had needed all of these.

"What's that?" Keo said.

Past the end of the row the crown fell away at its far shoulder. There seemed to be a pool of water there, and out of the shallows there a tall fin of metal stood up, crusted white, catching the sun.

"I don't know," Benal said, and stopped counting.

Teva shook her head. Keo was grinning at it. Benal found he was grinning too, his face stiff with salt.

Nearby some rock was hard and close-grained, not full of holes, and a run of hollows along it still held the storm's rain. They knelt and guzzled. The water tasted of stone and nothing else, with none of the grit of shell that had lain in the bottom of the shore's hollows at dusk.

Benal sat back and looked along the puddles, at their size and the sun already on them.

"Two or three days, maybe" he said. "If the sun's kind." He pointed at the smallest. A dark line ran round the rock a finger above its water, where the water had stood in the night.

"So we drink it before the sun does," Keo said.

Teva had gone to the nearest of the long shapes. Close up, it was a mound of coral rubble and sand, packed and settled, with the edge of a roof showing along the top. She walked round its near end and came back.

"There's no door," she said. "Not on this side and not on this end. It's buried to the roof." She looked along the row of them, at the rubble. "Records like the protocols will be wherever it's still dry, if it's dry anywhere. Not under a heap of rubble. We go back down and look along the low ground past the foot of the track, now the water's off it."

Keo looked back at the slope he had just come up. "Down," he said.

"I know." She looked at the ankle. "Take my shoulder this time. Ben's had you all the way up."

He got his weight onto the good foot, and his hand onto her shoulder.

Morning sounds sorted themselves into a neat inventory in Benal's ears: the far boom of the surf on the reef, the birds, the scrape of Keo's foot on the rock. Under all of it was one note. It held. He waited for it to rise or fall or break. Everything alive that he had ever heard pulsed, or breathed, or stopped to listen. This did not. And with it, faint, there was the tang of the air after lightning.

For a moment he was at the foot of the rock on the coast again, and he waited for the silence and the prickle.

His skin lay quiet on him. The surf went on breaking, and the birds went on. The note was not the coast's, which had risen and fallen. But it was as even as that had been.

The lightning was in his nose. It was really there.

It did not stop. He turned his head until he had it. It came from the mound beside them.

"Wait," he said.

Teva turned back. "What is it?"

"There's something in there. I can hear it. And I can smell it."

Keo had gone still, with his eyes on nothing, feeling for minds. "There's nothing alive in there," he said.

"No," Benal said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's one note, and it doesn't move."

"Can you tell where it's coming from?" Teva said.

"Along there. It's louder toward the end."

She did not go back down. She went along the mound where he pointed, and Keo went after her.

The note grew as they went. At the far end of the long shape the rock dropped into a cut, a step down to a floor of rock between two walls of it, and there, under the lip of the rubble heaped above, the end of the thing stood clear. It was a wall of pitted metal. In it, a step up from the rock, a seam ran round the shape of a door.

Benal went down into the cut. At the seam, air was breathing out of the wall, a thread of it, onto his face and hands. It was warmer than the morning, with no sun on it and no fire behind it. It smelled of hot dust.

"There's warm air coming out," he said. "It smells like after lightning."

Keo came down into the cut on his good foot, put his face to the seam and breathed in.

"The suit room smells like this sometimes. But this is stronger and drier." He straightened.

Beside the door a square panel was set into the wall, and it was lit from inside.

"Lit like the suits," Teva said.

"Then something's been making power here for two hundred and six years <!-- @WF-transit.elapsed: 206y -->," Benal said. He tried to think of anything at home that had kept going that long without someone feeding it, and all he could think of was the rock and the river.

Teva put her finger on the panel. Words came up on it, in the founders' plain letters, and she read them out.

"Authorized personnel only. Identify."

"Tell it who you are," Keo said.

Teva looked at him. Then, to the panel, she said, "Teva."

The words stayed as they were.

"I don't think it wants our names," Benal said. "I think it wants a name it already has."

Keo was running his hand over the wall below the panel. He lifted a flap of the same metal.

"There's a handle under here." He looked round at Benal with his hand on it. "Do I pull it?"

"Pull it," Benal said.

Keo pulled. The handle came down stiffly and then gave all at once, and the seal broke with a breath of air outward, past Benal's face and on up the cut.

The air that came out of the door was dry.
