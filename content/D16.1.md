Sun came in level through the broken end of the roof and lay across Benal's face, and it was warm. He kept his eyes shut and listened. The wind had gone in the night. Below the walkway the surge no longer slapped on the rust; there was only water running off concrete into water, and out at the broken end the birds beginning their racket.

Keo was sitting up beside him, working the bad ankle round in both hands. Teva stood at the edge of the walkway, looking down.

"It's gone," she said.

The floor of the house was bare. The lumps of rust stood in their rows on wet concrete with weed draped over them, and the water lay only in the low places between.

They came down the stair and out over the crust on the sill. The morning had been washed. Between the bank and the crown, where all yesterday a sheet of brown water had ruffled in the wind, the low ground had drained to gray rock, pitted and shining, with pools standing in its hollows. The crown rose beyond it, every edge of it sharp against a sky with nothing in it.

Benal went to the hollows along the bank first. They were dry. Where the rain had stood at dusk there was a damp stain in the bottom of each, and in one a crab, which went sideways into a crack as his shadow fell over it.

"The rain's gone," he said. "All of it."

"Gone where?" Keo said.

"Down. Through." He put his palm flat on the shell. It was all holes, and the holes went down. "This ground keeps nothing. If any of it's held, it'll be up on the crown, in the hard rock."

"Then we go up," Teva said.

They went out across the low ground toward the landing. The rock was slick in places with a green skin of weed, and the sun was already hot on it. Pools lay everywhere, some no bigger than a hand and some as long as a nest's floor, shallow and clear to the bottom. Keo stopped at the edge of one and looked down at the shell and sand in it.

"That's the sea's," Benal said. "The surge left it."

"I know."

Keo went round it the long way, hopping on the ankle. It was a step across, and he could see every shell on the bottom. Benal went round it too, and found that Teva had gone round the other side. Water was death. Clear water two fingers deep was still water.

Where the low ground met the lagoon, the rock was stained red in long streaks running down under the surface, as though something of the founders' had stood there once and rusted into the water. They kept to the high side, well back from the edge. A track went up the crown's slope from there, smoother than the rock on either side of it, and they climbed it at Keo's pace, Keo with a hand on Benal's shoulder wherever it steepened.

On the climb the morning came to Benal whole. His suit had stopped taking the edge off sound days ago, in the rain, and he had not told them. Up here the surf on the reef was a long way off and the birds were few, and he could stand it. He had his ears and his nose and nothing else now. On the bank yesterday they had told him the station was dead, and he had believed them.

The track went over the brow, and the top of the crown opened in front of them.

Arches stood across it, row behind row: bare frames of metal, gray and pitted, each one a curve from the rock up and over to the rock again, with nothing stretched across them, like the ribs of a carcass the scavengers had finished with. Beyond them, along the crown's spine, long shapes lay end to end in a row, heaped with rubble and sand to their eaves so that only their roofs showed above it, flat and pale.

Every one of those frames had had something over it once, and people under it.

"Are you seeing this?" Keo said.

Teva did not answer. She stood on the brow with the sun on her face and looked.

Benal had already started counting. He did not decide to. The numbers came, and he heard himself saying them aloud. "Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve."

"Ben."

"Thirteen. There are more behind those. Fourteen."

Keo laughed.

"They lived here," Teva said. She was smiling.

"Twenty," Benal said. "Twenty-one." Not two or three people, and not for a season. Two hundred people <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> had lived up here, for three years <!-- @WF-genesis.occupancy: 3y -->, and they had needed all of these.

"What's that?" Keo said.

Past the end of the row the crown fell away at its far shoulder to the lagoon. Out of the shallows there, beyond it, a tall fin of metal stood up out of the water, crusted white, catching the sun.

"I don't know," Benal said, and stopped counting.

Teva shook her head. Keo was grinning at it. Benal found he was grinning too, with his face stiff with salt, at a thing none of them could name.

The hollows were where he had hoped. Here the rock was hard and gray, not full of holes, and a run of hollows lay along it below the row, each still holding the storm's rain. They went down on their knees at the first. It was clean, with none of the grit of shell that had lain in the bottom of the bank's hollows at dusk.

Benal sat back and looked along the run of them, at their size and the sun already on them.

"About three days," he said. "If the sun's kind. Possibly less." He pointed at the smallest. A dark line ran round the rock a finger above its water, where the water had stood in the night. "That one's down a finger since last night."

"Then drink it before the sun does," Keo said.

Teva had gone to the nearest of the long shapes. Close to, it was a bank of coral rubble and sand, packed and settled, with the edge of a roof showing along the top. She walked the length of it and round its end and came back.

"There's no door," she said. "Not on this side and not on the end. It's buried to the roof." She looked along the row of them, at the rubble. "The protocols will be wherever it's still dry, if it's dry anywhere. Not under a heap of rubble. We go back down and look along the low ground past the landing, now the water's off it."

Keo looked back at the slope he had just come up. "Down," he said.

"Down."

He got his weight onto the good foot.

Benal had been half listening to them. The morning laid itself out for him sound by sound: the surf, the birds, the water still draining off the low ground in a hundred trickles. Under all of it there was something else. It was one note, and it held. He waited for it to rise or fall or break. Everything alive that he had ever heard pulsed, or breathed, or stopped to listen. This did not. And with it, faint, there was the smell of the air after lightning.

For a moment he was at the foot of the rock on the coast again, with the swell gone silent, and he waited for the silence and the prickle.

His skin lay quiet on him. The surf went on breaking, and the birds went on. The sound was not the coast's, which had risen and fallen; this one only held. But it was as even as that had been, and nothing alive was that even.

The smell was there. It was really there.

It did not stop. He turned his head, and it moved as he turned, and it came from the bank beside them.

"Wait," he said.

Teva turned back. "What?"

"There's something in there. I can hear it. And I can smell it."

Keo had gone still, with his eyes on nothing, feeling for minds. "There's nothing alive in there," he said.

"No," Benal said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's one note, and it doesn't move."

"Where?" Teva said.

"Along there. It's louder toward the end."

She did not go back down. She went along the bank the way he pointed, and Keo went after her.

The note grew as they went. At the far end of the long shape the rock dropped into a cut, a step down to a floor of rock between two walls of it, and there, under the lip of the rubble heaped above, the end of the thing stood clear. It was a wall of metal, dull and pitted. In it, a step up from the rock, a seam ran round the shape of a door.

Benal went down into the cut. At the seam, warm air was coming out of the wall, a thread of it, onto his wet face and hands. The morning was warm, and this was warmer, with no sun on it and no fire behind it. It smelled of lightning and warm dust.

"There's warm air coming out," he said. "It smells like after lightning."

Keo came down into the cut on his good foot, put his face to the seam and breathed in.

"That's the Repository," he said. "That's what old-tech smells like." He straightened. "And it's running."

Beside the door a square panel was set into the wall, and it was lit from inside.

"It's lit," Teva said. "Lit means power."

"Then something's been making power here for two hundred and six years <!-- @WF-transit.elapsed: 206y -->," Benal said. He tried to think of anything at home that had kept doing what it did for two hundred and six years <!-- @WF-transit.elapsed: 206y --> without someone feeding it, and all he could think of was the rock and the river.

Teva put her finger on the panel. Words came up on it, in the founders' plain letters, and she read them out.

"Authorized personnel only. Identify."

"Tell it who you are," Keo said.

Teva looked at him. Then, to the panel, she said, "Teva."

The words stayed as they were.

"I don't think it wants our names," Benal said. "I think it wants a name it already has."

Keo was running his hand over the wall below the panel. There was a flap there of the same metal, and when he lifted it there was a handle under it.

"There's a handle under here." He looked round at Benal with his hand on it. "Do I pull it?"

"Pull it," Benal said.

Keo pulled. The handle came down stiffly and then gave all at once, and the seal broke with a breath of air outward, past Benal's face and on up the cut.

The air that came out of the door was dry.
