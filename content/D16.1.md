Sun came through the broken end of the roof and lay across Benal's face. He kept his eyes shut and listened. The wind had gone. Below the walkway the slaps had gentled to water trickling off concrete into water, and out at the broken end the birds were starting their racket.

Keo was sitting up beside him, working the bad ankle round in both hands. Teva stood at the edge of the walkway, looking down.

"It's gone," she said.

The crusted backs lay in their rows on the concrete with weed draped over them, and water stood only in the low places between.

They came down the stair and out over the crust on the sill. Between the shore and the crown, where all yesterday the flood had ruffled in the wind, the low ground had drained to bare rock, pitted and shining, with pools in its hollows. Beyond it the crown rose sharp against an empty sky.

Benal checked the hollows along the shore first. Where the rain had stood at dusk, each held only a damp stain.

"The rain's gone," he said. "All of it."

"Gone where?" Keo said.

"Up. Or down. Through." He laid his palm on the shell. It was all holes, and the holes went down. "This ground keeps nothing. If any of it's held, it'll be up on the crown, in the hard rock."

"We're going up anyway." Teva looked at Keo's foot. "Can you climb on that?"

"If it isn't steep."

"Some way won't be."

They set out across the low ground. The rock was slick in places with green weed, and the sun was already hot on it. The pools ran from a hand's width to a nest floor's length, shallow and clear to the bottom. Keo stopped at one and looked down at the shell and sand in it.

"That's the sea's," Benal said. "The surge left it."

"I know."

Keo went round it the long way, hopping on the ankle. It was a step across. Benal went round it too, and found that Teva had gone round the other side. Habit. Water was death.

They kept to the high side, well back from the still water inside the reef. A gully wound up the crown's slope from there, and they climbed it at Keo's pace, his hand on Benal's shoulder wherever it steepened.

The morning came to Benal unfiltered. His suit had stopped dulling what he heard days ago, and he hadn't told them. Up here the surf was far off and the birds few. He could stand it.

The gully went over the brow, and the top of the crown opened in front of them.

Arches stood across it, row behind row, bare frames of metal, pitted and chalky, curving up out of the rock and down into it again, like the ribs of a carcass the scavengers had finished with. Beyond them, along the crown's spine, long shapes lay end to end, heaped with rubble and sand to their eaves so that only their roofs showed.

"What do you think?" Keo said.

Teva stood on the brow with the sun on her face and looked.

Benal was already counting, and heard himself doing it aloud. "Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve."

"Ben."

"Thirteen. There are more behind those. Fourteen."

Keo laughed.

"They lived here," Teva said. She was smiling. "Don't stop, Ben. I want to know how many."

"Twenty," Benal said. "Twenty-one." Two hundred people <!-- @WF-firstwalk.departed: 200 --> had lived up here, for three years <!-- @WF-genesis.occupancy: 3y -->, and they had needed all of these.

"What's that?" Keo said.

Past the end of the row the crown fell away at its far shoulder to what looked like a pool, and out of its shallows stood a tall fin of metal, crusted white, catching the sun.

"I don't know," Benal said, and stopped counting.

Teva shook her head. Keo was grinning at it. Benal found he was grinning too, his face stiff with salt.

Nearby the rock turned hard and close-grained, and a run of hollows in it still held the storm's rain. They knelt and guzzled. It tasted of stone and nothing else, without the shell grit of the shore's hollows at dusk.

Benal sat back and looked along the puddles, the sun already on them.

"Two or three days, maybe," he said. "If the sun's kind." He pointed at the smallest. A dark line ran round the rock a finger above its water, where the water had stood in the night.

"So we drink it before the sun does," Keo said.

Teva had gone to the nearest of the long shapes: coral rubble and sand, packed and settled, the edge of a roof showing along the top.

"There's no door," she said. "Not on this side and not on this end. It's buried to the roof." She looked along the row. "They wouldn't bury their own doors. There's a way in along here somewhere, and records like the protocols will be wherever it's still dry, if it's dry anywhere."

Keo looked along the mounds, and then at his ankle. "All of them?"

"Till we find it. Take my shoulder this time. Ben's had you all the way up."

He got his weight onto the good foot, and his hand onto her shoulder.

Benal's ears sorted the morning: the far boom of the surf on the reef, the birds, the scrape of Keo's foot on the rock. Under all of it ran one note. It held. He waited for it to rise or fall or break. Everything alive he had ever heard pulsed, or breathed, or stopped to listen. This didn't. And with it, faint, came the tang of the air after lightning.

For a moment he was at the rock on the coast again, waiting for the silence and the prickle.

His skin lay quiet on him. The surf went on breaking, and the birds went on. The note wasn't the coast's, which had risen and fallen. But it was as even.

The lightning was in his nose. It was really there.

It didn't stop. He turned his head until he had it. It came from the mound beside them.

"Wait," he said.

Teva turned back. "What is it?"

"There's something in there. I can hear it. And I can smell it."

Keo had gone still, his eyes on nothing, feeling for minds. "There's nothing alive in there," he said.

"No," Benal said. "That's what's wrong with it. It's one note, and it doesn't move."

"Can you tell where it's coming from?" Teva said.

"Along there. It's louder toward the end."

She went along the mound where he pointed, and Keo went with her.

At the far end the rock dropped into a cut, a step down between two walls of it, and there, under the lip of the heaped rubble, the end of the thing stood clear: a wall of pitted metal. A step up from the floor of the cut, a seam ran round the shape of a door.

Benal went down into the cut. At the seam a thread of air breathed out of the wall onto his face and hands. It was warmer than the morning, with no sun on it and no fire behind it. It smelled of hot dust.

"There's warm air coming out," he said. "It smells like after lightning."

Keo came down into the cut on his good foot, put his face to the seam and sniffed. "The suit room smells like this sometimes. Stronger, though. Drier."

Beside the door a square panel was set into the wall, lit from inside.

"Lit like the suits," Teva said.

"Then something's been feeding it for two hundred and six years <!-- @WF-transit.elapsed: 206y -->," Benal said. He tried to think of anything at home that had kept going that long without someone feeding it, and all he could think of was the rock and the river.

Teva put her finger on the panel. Words came up in the founders' plain letters, and she read them out.

"Authorized personnel only. Identify."

"Tell it who you are," Keo said.

Teva looked at him. Then, to the panel, she said, "Teva."

The words stayed as they were.

"I don't think it wants our names," Benal said. "I think it wants a name it already has."

Keo was running his hand over the wall below the panel. He lifted a flap of the same metal.

"There's a handle under here." He looked round at Benal with his hand on it. "Do I pull it?"

"Pull it," Benal said.

Keo pulled. The handle came down stiffly and then gave all at once, and the seal broke with a breath of air outward, past Benal's face and on up the cut.

The air that came out of the door was dry.
