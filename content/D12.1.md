The stone came down on the core and split it along the grain. Teva turned the wet strings of it over with her fingers and brought the stone down again.

She knelt at a shelf of limestone with the rest of the cores in a heap by her knee. Keo had cut them on the march all morning with the tooth-knife, from wild stone-cycad growing out of the cracks in the rock, and they were thin and stringy, nothing like the cores at home.

Her hands were cold. All of her was. Where the suit's skin was alive it had gone the color of the wet limestone. Down both forearms and across one knee it had died in patches the size of her palm. Anything looking down out of the rain would see pieces of a girl.

On the savanna she had hidden the heat from them all day, with the suit pulsing at her wrists. Hiding the cold was easier. She only had to keep her hands busy.

A few steps below her the spring came out of a crack in the rock and ran off down the scree in a channel. Steam lifted off it into the rain, and it stank of sulfur. When she first put her hand in, the water had been the heat of her own blood, or a little more, and she had wanted to leave the hand there.

Benal had gone up the slope as far as he could go and still be with them, and sat with his back to the spring. Keo sat on the scree below her with his bad leg out straight, shaking.

The bag was the spare water skin. She had slit it down one side and pricked it all over with her knife. Now she scooped the first of the pulp into it and packed two stones in on top, and tied the neck with cord. The other end of the cord went round a knob of rock at the edge of the channel. She lowered the bag into the flow, and cloudiness began out through the holes.

She wanted it in the water before anyone said anything about it, and it was.

Then she went back to the stone. Each time she had a double handful of pulp she drew the bag up, fed it in, and let it down again.

"Seven hours of light," Benal said, "and four of them we sit here. Thirteen kilometers left to walk. It's about forty hours since any of us ate." He looked at the heap by her knee. "And what we cut this morning came out at about half what I'd hoped. Two days for three of us, if we eat thin."

Teva brought the stone down.

"How long can it stay in before we walk?"

"Four hours. Then we go, if we want a fence up before dark." He turned his face a little further from the spring. "I'd rather not be here at dusk anyway. Things come to drink at a spring."

"At home it's one to three days in the river," Teva said. "This is pounded fine, and the water's hotter than blood. That goes faster. Half a day, maybe. A day." She swept the pulp off the rock into her palm. "We have four hours."

Keo was watching the water go into the bag and out of it.

"It needs another day," he said. "Hang it in the runoff tonight, and we eat it in the morning."

"That would be better," Teva said. "It's also another day with nothing in any of us, and tomorrow is the longest walk we have left. No."

"The trouble with pulp," Benal said, "is it lets you feel fine for hours first. Everybody's told that. So we wouldn't know whether it had worked until long after it was in us." He considered the rain. "Possibly not until morning. Possibly it's fine."

Teva drew the bag up, fed in the handful, and let it down.

Keo got his good leg under him and stood. "Let me do some."

She gave him the stone. He went down on his good knee and tried to bring the other one down beside it, and the leg would not bend that far. He sat down on the rock before she could tell him to, and held the stone up to her.

She took it.

When the last of the pulp was in, she unwound his dressing. The strips were wet through and pink in the middle. Under them the cut had drawn its edges together along most of its length, and only the center, where the stone had gone in deepest, still showed raw.

"How does it look?" Keo said.

"Closing."

She wrung the strips out and wound them on again. The leg was hot under her hands. He had the spring's heat in him and nothing to make it from.

Afterward she woke the display on her forearm. The skin was dying faster than it grew back. Growing back took sugar, and there was none.

Then the spikes. She ran her palm down each shaft to wipe the wet off it and rewound the binding at the head, tight. The wood had gone spongy under her thumb where the tooth was seated. When she laid them down they were as wet as when she had picked them up.

On the scree below the spring a scatter of red berries had dropped among the stones. She did not know them. She looked at them anyway, more than once, and they stayed where they were.

Twice on the walk, Noli had trotted into camp with a grounder in her jaws and dropped it at their feet. Nobody would catch one for them now.

At four hours by Benal's count she hauled the bag up. Water poured out through the holes and ran off her wrists. She opened the neck. The pulp inside was packed down gray under the stones, and warm from the spring. She took a pinch of it and put it on her tongue.

It was bitter, faintly, at the back of her tongue after the first moment, under the sulfur. Pan at home tasted of nothing. This tasted of something. Was it a little, or a lot? Her tongue could not tell her. She had swallowed it before she thought not to.

She took another pinch and held it to the intake at her collar and waited. The suit named nothing. It knew the smells of things that hunted, and it knew the gel. Did it know anything else? She wiped her fingers on her knee.

It was not clean. That was not the question. The question was how much was left in it, and whether that was less than a body could handle. In the stories it was never a mouthful that killed. It was a big dose, eaten as meals and kept down. One meal was not meals.

And against it there was tomorrow. There was Keo on the rock, shaking, with nothing in him to heal on, and Benal, who had been thin before any of this.

What was left in it she could not know. What a day with nothing in it would cost, she knew.

"I'll eat first," she said. "Keo, you're fevered. You can't take a second thing on top of that. Ben, your resistance is the worst of the three of us."

Keo looked at her, and then at the bag. Benal folded his arms over his knees.

She cut the pulp in the bag across twice with her knife and lifted one share of it out onto a flat stone. It was the share she would have given either of them.

"Nobody else eats until I've been well for hours."

She took the first mouthful off the flat of her knife and blew on it, though it was only warm. At home she blew on the spoon for Omya. Then she put it in her mouth.

She ate it all, a knifeful at a time, while the two of them watched her. Nobody spoke.
