The suit had stopped taking her heat an hour ago.

She felt when it happened, in late morning. Her skin grew warm, and the suit had begun pulsing at her wrists. The display on her forearm would say what the pulses meant, in words and numbers. She did not look at it.

Ahead, Keo went through another verse of Liaso's song without stopping for breath. His voice went on over the fern, easy and loud. Benal came in on the chorus, wheezing.

Teva's heart was going fast and light. Sweat ran into her eyes. The sun sat on the top of her helmet like a hand pressing down.

To take her mind off the heat, she recited the star fields that Omya had taught her when she was small. Partway through she lost track and started over.

Something darted into underbrush ahead of them; Keo stopped, laughed, mumbled. She did not look up. Looking up meant lifting her head into the sun.

Far off, out over the fern, something bugled. The sound throbbed behind her eyes. The edges of the world had started to go dark; it felt like she was walking down a green tube with the sun at the end of it.

"We should lie up," Benal said.

She wanted it so badly. She wanted to get down in the bit of shade the fern made and lie still and let her heart slow.

Then Keo said the bugle meant a herd going to drink. There'd be water...

If she said stop, they would ask her why. If they asked her why, she would have to say, or they would see.

She looked at the ground and kept her mouth shut. Keo turned to the bugles. They went on. And on.

Benal smelled it first, a long way off: wet earth, and dung, and something sour. And still they walked and walked. Then they came to where the herd had drunk, a hollow in the fern trodden to mud, with water lying in the prints, warm as the air and thick with dung. Nothing in it was fit to drink. Teva stood at the edge and wanted to lie down in the mud.

Benal took the three water skins and weighed them in his hands and admitted there wasn't much left. When he gave hers back, he held onto it a moment, and whispered, "You're sweating more than I am. With your regulation, you need the water—"

"I know," Teva said. "Walk."

She drank a little, fast, so that nobody would see how much she wanted it, and hung the skin on her belt and walked. And walked. And sweated.

Keo didn't stop until the sun was low. There was no thicket, no rock, and the cycads stood too far apart to close anything, so their fence had to go all the way round. She cut and carried, swaying a little, swallowing often.

The sun went down into the fern, red, and the heat did not come out of the suit.
