The suit had stopped taking her heat an hour ago.

She knew the moment it happened, as the late-morning heat built. Before that she had been hot and the suit had been drawing it out of her, steadily, into the reservoirs, as it was meant to. Then the reservoirs were full. The suit went on lying against her everywhere, close as a skin, and held her own heat in.

It had been pulsing at her wrists since then. Small, even pulses through the weave: a warning she understood without looking. The display on her forearm would say what the pulses meant, in words and numbers. She kept her eye shield up and her arm down at her side, and she did not look at it.

She did not want to see what it said. She knew it was bad. Looking would not make it less bad, and looking was a thing somebody could turn around and see her do.

Ahead of her, four steps, Keo was singing.

He went through another verse of Liaso's song without stopping for breath. Behind her Benal came in on the chorus, wheezing, a breath late. Keo's voice went on over the fern, easy and loud. Inside the suit Teva's heart was going fast and light. Sweat ran into her eyes, and the sun sat on the top of her helmet like a hand pressing down.

She put one foot in front of the other.

To keep doing it, she said the fields under her breath. Omya had given them to her one star at a time, on clear nights, when she was small. The Organs first, as Omya always began. Then the next field, and the next, each with its count, down to the faintest. Her lips moved. It was the only thing in her head that was cool.

Somewhere past the sixth field it broke, where it always broke. She started again from the beginning.

Something crossed the way ahead of them, fast; she heard Keo stop and laugh and say a word she did not catch. She did not look up. Looking up meant lifting her head into the sun.

Far off, out over the fern, something was calling, long and low. Every call landed behind her eyes like a slow thick drum. The edges of the world had started to go dark and close in, so that she seemed to be walking down a green tube with the sun at the end of it.

"We should lie up," Benal said.

She wanted it more than she had ever wanted anything. She wanted to get down in the bit of shade the fern made and lie still and let her heart slow.

"In the fern," Benal said. "It's shade, if you crouch. It's the hottest part of the day."

Then Keo was talking about the calling and water and a herd going to drink. She heard in his voice that he was sure. His feet stopped in the trodden way ahead of her and turned toward her.

If she said yes, they would ask her why. If they asked her why, she would have to say, or they would see.

She looked at the ground and kept her mouth shut. Keo turned away, and they went on.

In the afternoon Benal smelled it first, a long way off: wet earth, and dung, and something sour. They came to it much later. It was where the herd had drunk, a hollow in the fern trodden to mud, with brown water lying in the prints, warm as the air and thick with dung. Nothing in it could be drunk. The herd had gone on, and its calls with it. Teva stood at the edge and wanted to lie down in the mud, and did not.

Benal took the three belt skins and weighed them in his hands and said half a canteen, possibly less. When he gave hers back, he held onto it a moment, and said, low, with Keo still walking ahead, "You're sweating more than I am. Possibly a lot more. With your regulation, you need the water—"

"I know," Teva said. "Walk."

He let go of the skin. She drank her share fast, two swallows, so that nobody would see how much she wanted it, and hung the skin on her belt and walked.

She did not fall. She thought about falling all afternoon: down onto her knees in the trodden way, in front of Keo, with the suit pulsing at her wrists. She thought about it the way she thought about the river, and kept walking toward it and past it.

When the sun was low, Keo stopped them while there was still light to build. There was nothing out here to build against, no thicket, no rock, and the cycads stood too far apart to close anything, so the fence had to go all the way round, cycad fronds laid points outward and the few stakes they could cut from the frond stalks. She cut and carried with the others, keeping the load off her left shoulder. Her hands knew what to do.

The sun went down into the fern, red, and the heat did not come out of the suit.

She had not said anything about it all day.
