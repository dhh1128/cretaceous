The rock at the hollows was already warm under Keo's knees. Teva had come up to count the water. The biggest hollow was dry below the stain where the water had stood, and so was the next. In the last, a skin of water lay over the stone.

Benal crouched over it. "That's less than I left for this morning," he said. "The sun took more than I allowed."

"Ben first," Keo said. "Then Teva."

"You first, Keo. I'm watching."

So he took one mouthful, where she could see. It was warm as blood and tasted of the birds that had roosted on the crown all night, and he held it in his mouth anyway. Benal drank, then Teva. After hers the hollow was dry stone.

"Then it's today," Keo said.

"It's today," Teva said.

They went along the crown with the sun on their backs.

"Lift units on first," he said. "All four. Then the records in. It says."

"So we're not going at first light," Teva said.

"It's been first light for a while."

She almost smiled. "What do I do?"

"Lift. They're a load for two, and Ben's arm won't." He looked at her. "How's your head?"

"Pain's nearly gone."

Birds sat in a row along the top of the long mound and on the tail of the sunk boat, crying the same few rasping cries. Beside the store's door stood a rack of bars as long as his arm, flattened at one end. He had one in his hand before he thought about it. He leaned it against the first box and went into the long mound, under the wing, to the cabin. Under the floor the jars fizzed. On the panel the number had climbed in the night.

"Fit the wing-root units first," the voice said.

He and Teva walked the first unit in from the store, a ring on its edge between them. The rib found it on the first step. At the wing they heaved it chest high, level with the mount.

"There's something on it," she said. "Like old fat."

It was grease gone to gum.

"Knives," he said.

They scraped till the metal showed. Teva held the ring while he fed the bolts through, ran each nut down by hand and turned it with the tool with the jaw until it clicked. Then the plug on the unit's cord, which went in only one way round.

"It's in. They all clicked. Is it on?"

"Lift unit one: no response. Check orientation. Align the arrow on the housing with the mark on the mount rim."

"What mark?"

Teva put her knife to the rim where they hadn't scraped. The gum came away in a curl.

"Here." Under it, a little arrowhead cut into the metal pointed the wrong way. "We put it in backward."

They took off every nut and turned the ring. Getting it back on, his hand slipped, and the mount's edge took the skin off three knuckles, where the burns from the jars had only just closed.

"Wrap those," Teva said.

"Later."

The bleeding stopped before the last nut was on. He felt the skin closing, a tight itch across the backs of his fingers.

"Lift unit one: responding."

"Same weight as yesterday," Benal said from the cabin door, where he had been getting it from the voice again. "Every plate past it is a piece of the way home we don't get to fly."

"I have an idea," Keo said. "Maybe add one more thing to what we take."

"The skin that made power out of light," he said to the panel. "You said it's dead."

"Surface power membrane: not responding."

"Could it be grown again?"

"A membrane regrowth kit is listed in this craft's maintenance stores. Starter cysts in sealed ampoules, and an etched procedure plate. Kit locker."

"Where?"

The voice gave directions.

It was a flat case on the bottom shelf of a cupboard in the store. In it lay a plate cut close with writing and a row of small sealed tubes of glass in a tray, each with something brown and dry in the bottom of it, like seed. He took a tube out and turned it to the light from the door, and he could see his own fingers through it. It was made from the thing the founders had made to last. He put it back and closed the case with both hands.

"It's alive, then," he said to Teva. "Or it was. You Keepers grow things."

"If it's a culture, the Vitarium can grow it."

"It weighs next to nothing," Benal said. "It's in."

Benal went up the slope alone toward the archive.

The second wing unit went on the right way round and answered the first time.

The back mounts were out of reach from the floor.

"Dorsal mounts," the voice said. "Use the maintenance hoist."

"There isn't one," Keo said.

"Logged."

Teva was at the doors, looking out at the empty long boxes by the store. "Would those hold?"

They dragged two in and stacked them against the hull, the top one set back from the bottom. They held.

"One end each," Keo said, and put the bar under his edge of the ring. "Up a box, rest, up the next."

Every box was a lift, and the rib complained each time. Up on the wig's back, under the roof, the air hung dead. Sweat ran into his eyes.

Halfway through the next lift Teva stopped, put a hand flat on the top box and stayed there, head down. Under the sweat she had gone pale.

"Go and stand in the doors," he said.

She went without arguing, and that told him more than the hand had.

She came back when her color did. They got the first back unit onto its mount, and it answered. Then she sat beside him on the bottom box.

"Do you trust it?" she said.

"It's been wrong. It said the mounts were full. It said the other boat was moored." He turned the bar over. "Every time I showed it, it said so."

"And when you don't show it?"

"I check it."

She looked at the lit panel through the cabin door a while. Then she got up for the last one.

The last unit went up the boxes the same way. His arms had gone heavier than the lifting accounted for, and his hands shook on the bar by the time it was level with the mount. He ran the nuts down and put the tool to each until it clicked.

"Lift unit four: secure," the voice said.

He put his hand flat on the ring and pushed. It rocked.

"It isn't," he said.

One nut turned stiff and gritty under the tool, and he backed it off. It had gone on crooked and jammed short of tight, and the tool had clicked as if it were done. The voice had counted the clicks. The bolt's grooves were chewed flat halfway down.

"Logging a fault," the voice said.

The box had held exactly enough bolts, and every one was in a mount. The hole was sound.

"Teva," he called down. "A bolt, and a nut that goes on it. Off the seat outside. Your pile."

She brought them up the boxes in her palm.

The bolt went through clean. He ran the nut down, turned it until it clicked, then took the ring in both hands and leaned his whole weight on it. It did not move.

He left the plug for when his hands would hold still.

Teva took one of the emptied boxes up the slope for the register. She and Benal came back down with a long box between them, Benal at the light end, and he had already chosen; Keo saw it in how fast he moved. He set the box down at the cabin door, gave Teva the few loose leaves off the top to hold, and passed the rest in to Keo one at a time.

"Every protocol plate. I counted them against the first plate's list again. The tablet with the impact on it. That's the one they have to see."

He handed up the register. "The faces, and the roll of who walked."

He looked at the leaves in Teva's hands. "Those are yours. The first leaves of the arrival log. Where they came, and what they were told to do, and why. For the Council."

She held them against her chest.

The last thing he lifted out was the plate with his equation on it. He held it in both hands, flat.

"For your father," Keo said.

Benal set it in beside the case of tubes and wedged it upright. He did not answer.

"What did you leave?" Keo said.

"The rest of the log. Huge amounts of it. The rest of the plates. The lab's leaves."

One leaf was left in the bottom of the box, ruled close in the founders' writing. Benal laid it on the concrete beside the box. He picked it up again and looked at it. He laid it down again, square to the box's edge, and took his hand off it.

"The soft sheets," he said. "The last leaves of the ones who stayed. The panels, which nobody can read anyway."

He stowed the records where the fourth seat had been, behind Teva's, her leaves on top, and tied them down with that seat's straps. Then he put his hand on the seat beside them.

"Mine," he said.

He sat down on the step with his weak arm in his lap.

"With all of it in, and the three of us, it's inside what it gave me," he said. "Just. The records cost us under a kilometer against flying it empty."

A while later Keo found him on the concrete in the sun, looking up over the long mound's roof toward the sea side of the crown. He looked away, and then up there again. His lips had split.

"Ben, drink some—" Keo said, and stopped. There was nothing to drink.

Keo went up the boxes with the last plug. His hands held still enough now, and it went in.

"All four," he said. "Read them back."

The voice went through them, one to four. Each was responding.

"Four of four," he said down to them, and sat back on his heels.

"Lift unit test requires open sky," the voice said. "Release the cradle brakes. A lever at each wheel."

He and Teva went round the frame hauling up the stiff levers until the last gave with a knock. The three of them put their shoulders to the back and shoved. The wheels squealed and the frame rolled. The nose went out through the doors into the sun, the cord paying out of the wall behind it, till the whole wig stood on the open concrete with the birds shouting at it from the mound's top.

"Stand clear. Testing lift unit one."

They went back to the store's door.

The first unit screamed up to a pitch he felt in his teeth, a sound with no breath in it, the loudest thing he had ever stood near. It came through the concrete into the soles of his feet and up into the rib, and died away. Then the second, and the third, and the fourth.

The birds went up off the long mound and the sunk boat's tail all at once, circled once, high, and went away over the crown. They did not come back.

In the quiet his ears rang.

Benal had gone to the cabin door, and Keo looked in at the panel over his shoulder. While they watched, it went down by one.

"Filling the reserve," the voice said.

"That's a kilometer," Benal said. "About."

He looked at the four rings, still now, on the wig's wings and back.

"And we were standing still," he said. "Lifting off here is all four at once, with us in it."
