The sun was not yet clear of the sea, and already the rock at the hollows was warm under Keo's knees. Teva had come up to count the water. The biggest hollow was dry below its dark line, and so was the next. In the last one a skin of water lay over the stone, enough to wet a finger.

Benal crouched over it and looked a long time.

"That's less than I left for this morning," he said. "The sun took more than I allowed."

"Ben first," Keo said. "Then Teva."

"You first, Keo." Teva did not move. "I'm watching."

So he went down first and took one mouthful and lifted his face out of it, where she could see. It was warm as blood. It tasted of the birds that had sat on the crown all night, of the white they left on the rock, and he held it in his mouth a moment anyway before he swallowed. Benal drank after him, then Teva. After hers the hollow showed dry stone.

There was no more water on the island.

"Then it's today," Keo said.

"It's today," Teva said.

They went along the crown with the sun on their backs. Past the banked row the slope fell away toward the concrete.

"Lift units on first," he said. "All four. Then the records in. It says."

"So we're not going at first light," Teva said.

"It's been first light for a while."

She almost smiled. "What do I do?"

"Lift. They're a load for two, and Ben's arm won't."

Benal was walking with his weak arm held against his side. "How much can it carry? I want it from the voice again, not from my head."

Keo glanced at Teva. Yesterday her head had still been aching from the sea.

"How's your head?"

"Nearly gone."

The birds had the concrete. They sat in a row along the top of the long mound and stood thick on the tail of the sunk boat out in the shallows, and the noise of them came up the slope to meet them, the same few cries over and over. His sense was crowded with them, and in the still water below the ramp it found nothing big. The long boxes left by the store's door last night had gone white along their lids. The smell came off them sharp in the heat, and under it the weed on the bared edge down the ramp, drying.

"Off!" Keo waved both arms. "Go on! Get off it!"

They went up all together off the mound with a clatter of wings and came down again a body's length along it, and went on shouting.

"It's theirs, Keo," Teva said. "They've had it two hundred years."

"They can have it back at noon."

A rack stood beside the store's door with bars on it as long as his arm, flattened at one end. He had one in his hand before he had thought about it. He leaned it against the first box and went in under the wing to the cabin door. Under the floor the jars were fizzing. On the panel the number had climbed in the night.

"Which ones first?" he said.

"Fit the wing-root units first," the voice said. "Then the dorsal units."

"The ones on its back."

"Confirmed."

He and Teva walked the first unit in from the store's door, a few paces at a time, with the ring between them on its edge. The rib found it on the first step and stayed with it. At the wing he got his shoulder under the ring's rim and they lifted it level with the mount, which was as high as his chest. Teva looked over the ring at the mount.

"There's something on it," she said. "Like old fat."

The mount's face was coated in old grease gone hard as gum.

"Knives," he said.

They scraped till the metal showed. The bolts had come packed in the box with the ring, wrapped and dry. He fed one through and Teva held the ring steady while he ran the nut down by hand and then put the tool with the jaw to it, and turned it until it clicked. He did them all the same way. Then there was the cord on the unit's side, with a plug that went into its place in the mount one way round and not the other.

"It's in," he said, loud enough for the cabin. "It clicked, all of them. Is it on?"

"Lift unit one: no response. Check orientation. Align the arrow on the housing with the mark on the mount rim."

"What mark?"

Teva had her knife at the rim, where they had not scraped. The gum came away in a curl.

"Here," she said. Under it a little arrowhead had been cut into the metal, and it pointed the wrong way. "We put it in backward."

They took off every nut again and turned the ring between them. Getting it back onto the mount, his hand slipped and the mount's edge went across his knuckles. It was cut sharp, and it took the skin off three of them. His hands had been burned raw by what was in the drums on the first night, and they had only just closed.

"Wrap those," Teva said.

"Later."

The blood had stopped before he had the last nut on. He felt it closing, a tight itch across the backs of his fingers, and paid it no attention.

"Lift unit one: responding."

Benal had been at the cabin door with the voice all this while, getting his figure again. Now he stood in the doors, out of the glare.

"Before I go up," he said. "It gave me the same figure as yesterday. So not a kilo past it. Past that, every plate is a piece of the way home we don't get to fly."

"Count one thing in first," Keo said.

He climbed into the pilot's seat. The grit under its cover crunched. On the first night he had asked the voice what used to make the wig go, and it had told him about a skin over the whole of it, living, that made power out of light. Out of the door the wing was the gray of dead bark.

"The skin that made power out of light," he said. "You said it's dead."

"Surface power membrane: not responding."

"Could it be grown again?"

"A membrane regrowth kit is listed in this craft's maintenance stores. Starter cysts in sealed ampoules, and an etched procedure plate. Kit locker."

The kit was a flat case on the cupboard's bottom shelf. In it lay a plate cut close with writing and a row of small sealed tubes of glass in a tray, each with something brown and dry in the bottom of it, like seed. Keo had watched Benal light the fire with a piece of glass two days ago and had not held it. He took a tube out and turned it to the light from the door. It was lighter than it looked, cool, and he could see his own fingers through it. It was the thing the founders had made to last. He put it back as it had lain and closed the case with both hands.

"It's alive, then," he said to Teva. "Or it was. Your lot grow things."

"If it's a culture, the Vitarium can grow it."

"It's light," Benal said. "It's in."

He went up the slope alone toward the archive.

"I'll come up for the register when these are on," Teva called after him.

"I'll have chosen by then."

The second wing unit went on the right way round and answered first time.

The mounts along the back were higher than either of them could reach from the floor.

"Dorsal mounts," the voice said. "Use the maintenance hoist."

"The what?"

"The maintenance hoist."

Keo looked round the long mound, at the bare walls and the roof. "There isn't one. There's nothing in here to lift with."

"Logged."

Teva had gone to the doors. She was looking at the empty long boxes, lying where they had dragged them out.

"Would those hold?" she said. "The empty ones."

They dragged three in and stood them against the hull's side, one on the floor, one on top of that and set back, then the third, and climbed them to see. They held. The unit had to go up them a box at a time, with the bar under one edge of the ring and Teva's hands under the other.

"One end each," Keo said. "Up a box, rest, up the next."

Each box was a lift, and every lift went into the rib. Up on the wig's back, under the roof, the air did not move at all. Sweat ran into his eyes and stung. The fizz of the jars came up through the hull into his knees.

When he looked round for the next lift, Teva's face had gone gray under the wet. She was breathing through her mouth.

"Go and stand in the doors," he said. "There's air there."

She went, and did not argue. That told him more than her face had. He watched her go down the boxes one at a time with a hand on the hull. Then he sat on the top box with the bar across his knees and waited for her.

She came back when her color did. They got the first back unit up the last box and onto its mount, and it answered. Then she sat beside him on the bottom box. Through the open cabin door they could see the panel's light.

"Do you trust it?" she said.

"It's been wrong. It said the mounts were full. It said the other boat was moored." He turned the bar over. "Every time I showed it, it said so."

"And when you don't show it?"

"I check it."

She looked at the panel's light a while longer. Then she got up for the last one.

The last unit went up the boxes the same way. His arms had gone heavy, heavier than the lifting accounted for, and his hands shook on the bar by the time it was level with the mount. He ran the nuts down and put the tool to each until it clicked.

"Lift unit four: secure," the voice said.

He put his hand flat on the ring and pushed. It rocked.

"It isn't," he said.

He put the tool to each nut in turn. One of them turned stiff and gritty, and he backed it off a little at a time. It had gone on crooked under his hands and jammed short of tight, and the tool had clicked on it as if it were done. The voice had counted the clicks. The grooves on the bolt were chewed flat halfway down.

"Logging a fault," the voice said.

There were no more bolts in the box. Every one had gone into a mount. The hole through the mount was still sound, though.

"Teva," he called down. "A bolt, and a nut that goes on it. Off the seat outside. Your pile."

She came back with them in her palm and climbed the boxes and held them up to him.

"This one?"

"That one."

It went through clean. He ran the nut down, turned it until it clicked, then took hold of the ring with both hands and leaned his whole weight on it. It did not move.

He left the plug for when his hands would hold still.

Teva went up the slope for the register. When the two of them came back down, they had one of the long boxes between them, Benal at the light end, and Benal had already chosen. Keo saw it in how fast he moved. He set the box down at the cabin door and took a few loose leaves off the top, held together at one edge, and gave them to Teva to hold. Then he lifted the rest out one at a time and passed them in to Keo, and he talked while he did it.

"Every protocol plate. I counted them against the first plate's list again. Twice." He passed in the next. "The tablet with the impact on it. That's the one they have to see."

He handed up the register, the heavy stack of faces. "The faces, and the roll of who walked."

He looked at the leaves in Teva's hands. "Those are yours. The first leaves of the arrival log. Where they came, and what they were told to do, and why. For the Council."

She held them against her chest.

The last thing he lifted out was the plate from the lab. He held it in both hands, flat, and the lines on it caught the white light.

"For your father," Keo said.

Benal set it in beside the case of tubes and wedged it upright. He did not answer.

"What did you leave?" Keo said.

"The rest of the log. Two kilometers of it, roughly, possibly three. The rest of the plates, about the same. The lab's leaves."

There was one more thing in the bottom of the box, a leaf ruled close from edge to edge in the founders' writing. Benal took it out. He laid it on the concrete beside the box. He picked it up again and looked at it, and laid it down again, square to the box's edge, and took his hand off it.

"The soft sheets," he said. "The last leaves of the ones who stayed. The panels, which nobody can read anyway."

He climbed in after the records and fitted them into the space behind Teva's seat, where the fourth seat had been, plates edge to edge so nothing would knock, and Teva's leaves on top. He tied it all down with the straps they had taken off that seat yesterday and pulled each one twice. Then he put his hand on the back of the seat beside it, the one left in the back.

"Mine," he said.

He sat down on the step with his weak arm in his lap.

"With all of it in, and the three of us, it's inside what it gave me," he said. "Just. The box costs us under a kilometer against flying it empty. I think under."

Keo had it the first time, and so did Teva.

A while later Keo found him outside, standing in the sun on the concrete. He was looking back up over the long mound's roof at the crown, toward the far side of it, where the sea was. He looked away, and then he looked up there again. His lips had split.

"Ben, drink some—" Keo said, and stopped. There was nothing to drink.

Keo went up the boxes again with the last plug. His hands held still enough now, and it went into its place the one way it would go.

"All four," he said. "Read them back."

"Lift unit one: responding. Lift unit two: responding. Lift unit three: responding. Lift unit four: responding."

"Lift units responding," he said down to them. "Four of four." He sat back on his heels on the wig's back. "They're on. All of them."

"Lift unit test requires open sky," the voice said. "Move the craft clear of the hangar."

"How?" Keo said. "It's on a frame."

"Release the cradle brakes. A lever at each wheel. Raise each lever."

There was a lever beside each wheel of the frame, stiff, and he and Teva went round and pulled them up one after another until the last gave with a knock. Then the three of them put their shoulders to the back of the frame and pushed. For a moment nothing moved. Then the frame rolled, slowly, and the nose went out through the doors into the sun. The cord came out of the wall behind it, more and more of it, till the whole wig stood on the open concrete with the birds shouting at it from the mound's top.

"Stand clear. Testing lift unit one."

They went back to the store's door.

The first unit screamed. It came up out of nothing to a pitch he felt in his teeth, a sound with no breath in it, the loudest thing he had ever stood near. It came through the concrete into the soles of his feet and up into the rib. Then it died away. Then the second went, and the third, and the fourth.

The birds went up off the long mound and off the sunk boat's tail all at once. They circled once, high, and went away over the crown. They did not come back.

In the quiet afterward his ears rang. Under it he could hear the jars fizzing again, and the water at the ramp's foot.

Benal had gone to the cabin door. He stood looking in at the panel, and Keo came and looked over his shoulder. All night the number had climbed. Now, while they watched, it went back down by one.

"Filling the reserve," the voice said.

"That's a kilometer," Benal said. "About."

He looked out at the four rings, still now, on the wig's wings and back.

"And we were standing still," he said. "Lifting off here is all four at once, with us in it."
