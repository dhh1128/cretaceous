The sun was not yet clear of the sea, and already the rock at the hollows was warm under Keo's knees. Teva had come up to count the water. The biggest hollow was dry below its dark line, and so was the next. In the last one a skin of water lay over the stone.

Benal crouched over it and looked a long time.

"That's less than I left for this morning," he said. "The sun took more than I allowed."

"Ben first," Keo said. "Then Teva."

"You first, Keo." Teva did not move. "I'm watching."

So he went down first and took one mouthful, where she could see. It was warm as blood and tasted of the birds that had sat on the crown all night, and he held it in his mouth a moment anyway. Benal drank after him, then Teva. After hers the hollow was dry stone.

There was no more water on the island.

"Then it's today," Keo said.

"It's today," Teva said.

They went along the crown with the sun on their backs.

"Lift units on first," he said. "All four. Then the records in. It says."

"So we're not going at first light," Teva said.

"It's been first light for a while."

She almost smiled. "What do I do?"

"Lift. They're a load for two, and Ben's arm won't."

"How much can it carry?" Benal said. "I want it from the voice again, not from my head."

"How's your head?" Keo said to Teva.

"Nearly gone."

The birds had the concrete. They sat in a row along the top of the long mound and stood thick on the tail of the sunk boat out in the shallows, crying the same few cries over and over. In the water below the ramp his sense found nothing big. The long boxes left by the store's door last night had gone white along their lids, and the smell came off them sharp in the heat.

"Off!" Keo waved both arms. "Go on! Get off it!"

They went up all together off the mound with a clatter of wings and came down again a body's length along it, and went on shouting.

"It's theirs, Keo," Teva said. "They've had it two hundred years."

"They can have it back at noon."

A rack stood beside the store's door with bars on it as long as his arm, flattened at one end. He had one in his hand before he had thought about it. He leaned it against the first box and went into the long mound, under the wing, to the cabin door. Under the floor the jars were fizzing. On the panel the number had climbed in the night.

"Which ones first?" he said.

"Fit the wing-root units first," the voice said. "Then the dorsal units."

"The ones on its back."

"Confirmed."

He and Teva walked the first unit in from the store's door, a ring on its edge between them, a few paces at a time. The rib found it on the first step. At the wing they lifted it level with the mount, chest high.

"There's something on it," she said. "Like old fat."

It was old grease, gone to gum.

"Knives," he said.

They scraped till the metal showed. The bolts had come packed in the box with the ring. Teva held the ring steady while he fed each one through, ran the nut down by hand and turned it with the tool with the jaw until it clicked. Then the plug on the unit's cord, which went into the mount only one way round.

"It's in," he said. "It clicked, all of them. Is it on?"

"Lift unit one: no response. Check orientation. Align the arrow on the housing with the mark on the mount rim."

"What mark?"

Teva had her knife at the rim, where they had not scraped. The gum came away in a curl.

"Here," she said. Under it a little arrowhead had been cut into the metal, and it pointed the wrong way. "We put it in backward."

They took off every nut and turned the ring. Getting it back on, his hand slipped, and the mount's sharp edge took the skin off three knuckles. What was in the drums had burned his hands raw on the first night, and they had only just closed.

"Wrap those," Teva said.

"Later."

The bleeding had stopped before the last nut was on. He felt the skin closing, a tight itch across the backs of his fingers.

"Lift unit one: responding."

Benal had been at the cabin door all this while, getting his figure from the voice again.

"It gave me the same figure as yesterday," he said. "Every plate past it is a piece of the way home we don't get to fly."

"Count one thing in first," Keo said.

He climbed into the pilot's seat.

"The skin that made power out of light," he said. "You said it's dead."

"Surface power membrane: not responding."

"Could it be grown again?"

"A membrane regrowth kit is listed in this craft's maintenance stores. Starter cysts in sealed ampoules, and an etched procedure plate. Kit locker."

The kit was a flat case on the bottom shelf of a cupboard in the store. In it lay a plate cut close with writing and a row of small sealed tubes of glass in a tray, each with something brown and dry in the bottom of it, like seed. He had watched Benal light the fire with glass, and had not held any. He took a tube out and turned it to the light from the door, and he could see his own fingers through it. It was the thing the founders had made to last. He put it back and closed the case with both hands.

"It's alive, then," he said to Teva. "Or it was. Your lot grow things."

"If it's a culture, the Vitarium can grow it."

"It weighs next to nothing," Benal said. "It's in."

He went up the slope alone toward the archive.

"I'll come up for the register when these are on," Teva called after him.

"I'll have chosen by then."

The second wing unit went on the right way round and answered first time.

The mounts along the back were higher than either of them could reach from the floor.

"Dorsal mounts," the voice said. "Use the maintenance hoist."

"The what?"

"The maintenance hoist."

Keo looked round the long mound. "There isn't one. There's nothing in here to lift with."

"Logged."

Teva was at the doors, looking out at the empty long boxes by the store.

"Would those hold?" she said.

They dragged three in and stacked them against the hull's side, each set back from the one below, and climbed them to see. They held.

"One end each," Keo said, and put the bar under his edge of the ring. "Up a box, rest, up the next."

Every box was a lift, and the rib found each one. Up on the wig's back, under the roof, the air did not move at all. Sweat ran into his eyes.

When he looked round for the next lift, Teva's face had gone gray under the wet. She was breathing through her mouth.

"Go and stand in the doors," he said. "There's air there."

She went without arguing, and that told him more than her face had.

She came back when her color did. They got the first back unit up the last box and onto its mount, and it answered. Then she sat beside him on the bottom box.

"Do you trust it?" she said.

"It's been wrong. It said the mounts were full. It said the other boat was moored." He turned the bar over. "Every time I showed it, it said so."

"And when you don't show it?"

"I check it."

She looked at the lit panel through the cabin door a while. Then she got up for the last one.

The last unit went up the boxes the same way. His arms had gone heavy, heavier than the lifting accounted for, and his hands shook on the bar by the time it was level with the mount. He ran the nuts down and put the tool to each until it clicked.

"Lift unit four: secure," the voice said.

He put his hand flat on the ring and pushed. It rocked.

"It isn't," he said.

One nut turned stiff and gritty under the tool, and he backed it off a little at a time. It had gone on crooked and jammed short of tight, and the tool had clicked on it as if it were done. The voice had counted the clicks. The grooves on the bolt were chewed flat halfway down.

"Logging a fault," the voice said.

There were no more bolts in the box; every one had gone into a mount. The hole was still sound.

"Teva," he called down. "A bolt, and a nut that goes on it. Off the seat outside. Your pile."

She came back with them in her palm and climbed the boxes and held them up to him.

"This one?"

"That one."

It went through clean. He ran the nut down, turned it until it clicked, then took hold of the ring with both hands and leaned his whole weight on it. It did not move.

He left the plug for when his hands would hold still.

Teva went up the slope for the register. She and Benal came back down with one of the long boxes between them, Benal at the light end, and he had already chosen. Keo saw it in how fast he moved. He set the box down at the cabin door, gave Teva the few loose leaves off the top to hold, and passed the rest in to Keo one at a time, talking as he did.

"Every protocol plate. I counted them against the first plate's list again." He passed in the next. "The tablet with the impact on it. That's the one they have to see."

He handed up the register. "The faces, and the roll of who walked."

He looked at the leaves in Teva's hands. "Those are yours. The first leaves of the arrival log. Where they came, and what they were told to do, and why. For the Council."

She held them against her chest.

The last thing he lifted out was the plate with his equation on it. He held it in both hands, flat.

"For your father," Keo said.

Benal set it in beside the case of tubes and wedged it upright. He did not answer.

"What did you leave?" Keo said.

"The rest of the log. Two or three kilometers of it. The rest of the plates, about the same. The lab's leaves."

One leaf was left in the bottom of the box, ruled close from edge to edge in the founders' writing. Benal laid it on the concrete beside the box. He picked it up again and looked at it, and laid it down again, square to the box's edge, and took his hand off it.

"The soft sheets," he said. "The last leaves of the ones who stayed. The panels, which nobody can read anyway."

He climbed in and fitted the records into the space behind Teva's seat, where the fourth seat had been, plates edge to edge and Teva's leaves on top, and tied it all down with the straps off that seat. Then he put his hand on the back of the seat left beside it.

"Mine," he said.

He sat down on the step with his weak arm in his lap.

"With all of it in, and the three of us, it's inside what it gave me," he said. "Just. The box costs us under a kilometer against flying it empty."

Keo had it the first time, and so did Teva.

A while later Keo found him out on the concrete in the sun, looking up over the long mound's roof toward the sea side of the crown. He looked away, and then up there again. His lips had split.

"Ben, drink some—" Keo said, and stopped. There was nothing to drink.

Keo went up the boxes again with the last plug. His hands held still enough now, and it went in.

"All four," he said. "Read them back."

"Lift unit one: responding. Lift unit two: responding. Lift unit three: responding. Lift unit four: responding."

"Lift units responding," he said down to them. "Four of four." He sat back on his heels. "They're on. All of them."

"Lift unit test requires open sky," the voice said. "Move the craft clear of the hangar."

"How?" Keo said. "It's on a frame."

"Release the cradle brakes. A lever at each wheel. Raise each lever."

He and Teva went round the frame pulling up the stiff levers until the last gave with a knock. Then the three of them put their shoulders to the back of it and pushed. For a moment nothing moved. Then the frame rolled, and the nose went out through the doors into the sun, the cord paying out of the wall behind it, till the whole wig stood on the open concrete with the birds shouting at it from the mound's top.

"Stand clear. Testing lift unit one."

They went back to the store's door.

The first unit screamed up to a pitch he felt in his teeth, a sound with no breath in it, the loudest thing he had ever stood near. It came through the concrete into the soles of his feet and up into the rib, and died away. Then the second, and the third, and the fourth.

The birds went up off the long mound and the sunk boat's tail all at once, circled once, high, and went away over the crown. They did not come back.

In the quiet his ears rang.

Benal had gone to the cabin door, and Keo looked in at the panel over his shoulder. The number had climbed all night. While they watched, it went back down by one.

"Filling the reserve," the voice said.

"That's a kilometer," Benal said. "About."

He looked at the four rings, still now, on the wig's wings and back.

"And we were standing still," he said. "Lifting off here is all four at once, with us in it."
