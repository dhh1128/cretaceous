The rock at the hollows that held their puddles was already warm under Keo's knees. They had come up to measure the water.

The widest hollow was dry below the stain where the water had stood, and so was the next. In the last, a skin of liquid lay over the stone.

Benal crouched over it. "Less than I was hoping," he said. "Almost nothing."

"Ben first," Keo said. "Then Teva."

"You first, Keo. I'm watching."

So he slurped one mouthful, where she could see. It was warm as blood and tasted of the birds that had roosted on the crown all night, and he held it in his mouth anyway. Benal also slurped, then Teva. After hers the hollow was just damp.

"We definitely have to go today," Keo said.

"Soon," Teva agreed.

They went along the crown with the sun on their backs.

"First we have to put the lift units on the wig," he said. "All four. Then we load the records."

"So much for first light," Teva said. She pushed a tangle of hair back from her face. "What can I do?"

"Lift with me. They're a load for two, and Ben's arm won't." Keo looked at her. "How's your head?"

She sighed. "The headache from that bad mash is finally gone."

Birds sat in a row along the top of the long mound and on the tail of the sunk boat, crying the same few rasping cries. Beside the store's door stood a rack of bars as long as his arm, flattened at one end. He had one in his hand before he thought about it. He leaned it against the first box and went into the long mound, under the wing, to the cabin. Under the floor the jars fizzed. On the panel the number had climbed in the night.

"We're back," Keo told the voice.

"Fit the wing-root units first," it replied.

He and Teva walked the first unit in from the store, a ring on its edge between them. His rib twinged on the first step. He grunted. At the wing they heaved it chest high, level with the mount.

"There's something on it," she said. "Like old fat." Grease gone to gum.

"Use knives," he said.

They scraped till the metal showed. Teva held the ring while he fed the bolts through, ran each nut down by hand and turned it with the tool with the jaw until it clicked. Then the plug on the unit's cord, which went in only one way round.

"It's in. They all clicked. Is it on?"

"Lift unit one: no response. Check orientation. Align the arrow on the housing with the mark on the mount rim."

"What mark?"

Teva put her knife to the rim where they hadn't scraped. The gum came away in a curl.

"Here." Under it, a little arrowhead cut into the metal pointed the wrong way. She grimaced. "I think we put it in backward."

They took off every nut and turned the ring. Getting it back on, Keo's hand slipped, and the mount's edge took the skin off three knuckles, where the burns from the jars had only just closed.

"Wrap those," Teva said.

"Later."

The bleeding stopped before the last nut was on. He felt the skin closing, a tight itch across the backs of his fingers.

"Lift unit one: responding."

"We still have to be careful about weight," Benal said from the cabin door, where he had been calculating with the voice again. "Anything past the bare minimum could be part of the way home we don't get to fly."

"I have an idea," Keo said. "Even if it adds a tiny bit of risk, it still might be worth it to add one more thing." He turned to the panel. "The skin that made power out of light," he said to the panel. "You said it's dead."

"Surface power membrane: not responding."

"Could it be grown again?"

He saw Benal's eyes widen.

"A membrane regrowth kit is listed in this craft's maintenance stores. Starter cysts in sealed ampoules, and an etched procedure plate. It is kept in the kit locker."

"Where?"

The voice gave directions.

What they were looking for turned out to be a flat case on the bottom shelf of a cupboard in the store. In it lay a plate cut close with writing and a row of small sealed tubes of glass in a tray, each with something brown and dry in the bottom of it, like seed. He took a tube out and turned it to the light from the door, and he could see his own fingers through it. It was made from the thing the founders had made to last. He put it back and closed the case with both hands.

"It's alive, then," he said to Teva. "Or it was. You keepers grow things."

"If it's a culture, the Vitarium can grow it."

"It weighs next to nothing," Benal said. "And if this lets us charge the wig without Genesis, we want it. It's in. I'm getting the rest of the stuff from the archive." He disappeared out the doors of the hangar.

The second wing unit went on the right way round and answered the first time. But then they had a problem. The back mounts were out of reach from the floor.

"Now fit the dorsal mounts," the voice said. "Use the maintenance hoist."

"What's that?" Keo asked.

"A hydraulic platform that telescopes up from the floor on a crank."

Keo had no idea what most of those words meant, but he didn't see anything big enough to be helpful. "There isn't one," he said.

"Logged."

Teva was at the doors, looking out at the empty long boxes by the store. "Would those hold?"

They dragged two in and stacked them against the hull, the top one set back from the bottom.

"One end each," Keo said, and put the bar under his edge of the ring. "Up a box, rest, up the next."

Every box was a lift, and his rib hurt each time. Up on the wig's back, under the roof, the air hung dead. Sweat ran into his eyes.

Halfway through the next lift Teva stopped, put a hand flat on the top box and stayed there, head down. Under the sweat she had gone pale. Heat again.

"Go and stand in the doors," he said.

She went without arguing, and that told him more than the hand had.

She came back when her color did. They got the first back unit onto its mount, and it answered. Then she sat beside him on the bottom box.

"Do you trust the voice?" she said.

"It's been wrong. It said the mounts were full. It said the other boat was moored."

"So how do we catch the mistakes?"

Keo shrugged. "Check what we can. Hope it's right about the rest. What else can we do?"

She looked at the lit panel through the cabin door a while. Then she got up for the last lift unit.

It went up the boxes the same way. His arms had gone heavier than the lifting accounted for, and his hands shook on the bar by the time it was level with the mount. He ran the nuts down and put the tool to each until it clicked.

"Lift unit four: secure," the voice said.

He put his hand flat on the ring and pushed. It rocked.

"It isn't," he said.

One nut turned stiff and gritty under the tool, and he backed it off. It had gone on crooked and jammed short of tight, and the tool had clicked as if it were done. The voice had counted the clicks. The bolt's grooves were chewed flat halfway down.

"Logging a fault," the voice said.

The box had held exactly enough bolts, and every one was in a mount. The hole was sound.

"Teva," he called down. "A bolt, and a nut that goes on it. Off the seat outside. Your pile."

She brought the boxes in her palm.

The replacement bolt was smaller than the original, but it went through clean. He ran the nut down, turned it until it clicked, then took the ring in both hands and leaned his whole weight on it. It did not move.

He sat in the driver's seat to rest. His hands and arms were weary and unsteady.

Teva disappeared up the slope with one of the emptied boxes. After a while she and Benal came back, a half-full box between them. They set it down and Benal began handing the sheets of metal to Keo.

"Every protocol plate. I counted them against the first plate's list again. The tablet with the impact on it. That's the one they have to see." He passed the register. "The faces, and the roll of who walked."

Teva was clutching some leaves against her chest. Benal looked at her for a moment. "Those are also important. The first leaves of the arrival log. Where they came, and what they were told to do, and why. For the Council."

She handed them over.

The last thing Benal lifted out was the plate with his equation on it. He held it in both hands, flat.

"For your father," Keo said. He set it in beside the case of tubes and wedged it upright. 

Benal did not answer.

"What are we leaving?" Keo said.

"The rest of the log. Huge amounts of it. The rest of the plates. The lab's leaves."

Benal sat down on the step with his weak arm in his lap. He looked as exhausted as Teva.

"With all of that, and the three of us, the weight's probably under the limit," he said. "Just. The records probably cost us a kilometer or two against flying empty."

They sat for a while, waiting for energy to come back.

Keo noticed that Benal's lips had split. "Ben, drink some—" Keo said, and stopped. There was nothing to drink. After a minute of silence, he stretched and stood.

"I think we're ready," he said. He turned to where the invisible voice spoke from. "Are we ready?"

"All the lift units appear healthy, but they require a test," the voice said. "That requires open sky. Release the cradle brakes. A lever at each wheel. Don't unplug the cable yet."

They went round the frame hauling up the stiff levers until the last gave with a knock. The three of them put their shoulders to the back and shoved. The wheels squealed and the frame rolled. The nose went out through the doors into the sun, the cord paying out of the wall behind it, till the whole wig stood on the open concrete with the birds shouting at it from the mound's top.

"Stand clear. Testing lift unit one."

They went back to the store's door.

The first unit screamed up to a pitch he felt in his teeth, a sound with no breath in it, the loudest thing he had ever stood near. It came through the concrete into the soles of his feet and up into the rib, and died away. Then the second, and the third, and the fourth.

The birds went up off the long mound and the sunk boat's tail all at once, circled once, high, and went away over the crown. They did not come back.

In the quiet his ears rang.

Benal had gone to the cabin door, and Keo looked in at the panel over his shoulder. While they watched, it went down by one.

"Filling the reserve," the voice said.

"That's a kilometer," Benal said. "About."

He looked at the four rings, still now, on the wig's wings and back.

"And we were standing still," he said. "Lifting off here is all four at once, with us in it."
