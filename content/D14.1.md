The palms ended at first light. Past the last of them the salt scrub thinned to bare ground, and the bare ground ran out level to a line that had to be sand.

Benal stopped at the edge. His legs had done the last of the dark without much help from him.

The air was wrong. A breeze had come in off the sea all night, and now there was none. The heat sat on his face though the sun was not up, and the fronds behind him hung still. The insect wall had thinned with the palms, and every sound on the coast came to him whole, each in its place: the surf beyond the sand, birds he could not see, a tick and a pop out on the mud.

"There's no wind," he said.

Keo came up beside him, his weight on the spike. "Then nothing smells us coming."

"There was wind all night."

Teva had her own pack again, and she held her head carefully under the helmet, as though it might spill. She looked up. "The sky's clear."

"I know."

The flat looked like ground. Low ridges of broken shell ran across it, and between them the silt lay smooth and shining. Channels wound through it, all running the same way, out.

They went out along the ridges, Keo first, testing each step with the spike. With no wind, the sandflies found them.

At the first channel Benal stopped. The water ran shallow and quick over the silt.

"It's all running out," he said. "That must be the tide. When it turns, these fill first, and then they join. In a few hours, I think."

"Then we don't stop," Keo said.

At a gap between ridges Keo's bad leg came down short, and the silt took it to the knee.

"Don't pull straight up," Teva said behind him.

"I know. Rock it." He worked his hip, and the silt sucked at him. "Ben, take the arm."

Benal stepped past him onto the far ridge and took Keo's forearm with his right hand. His left was not worth anything any more, and Keo would have felt it. Teva had Keo by the belt.

"If the tide came in now," Benal said, "you'd drown standing up."

"Pull, Ben," Teva said.

The leg came up black, stinking of the springs, with a sound like a mouth letting go.

They went on slower, with the water already creeping back into the creeks below them. At the last gap Keo stepped across and held his hand back. Teva took it and came across on it. He let go on the other side.

The barrier beach was a long low hump of sand. From the top of it Benal looked back over the flat and the palms, at everything they had walked.

"We couldn't walk back now," he said. "Eighty kilometers, on what we've got? We'd be dead before the river." <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->

"Nobody's walking back," Keo said. "It's the boat or nothing."

Teva had already turned to the sea.

Each swell broke with a boom Benal felt in the sand before he heard it, and a long hiss going back. The booms came slow, and he counted the gaps. The sea smelled sharp and green, like nothing at home, and the air left salt on his lips.

The beach ran off into haze both ways, and along the whole of it turtles were heaving up out of the surf by the dozen. Crocs lay at the waterline where the turtles had to cross, and the smell of blood came up the beach. The three kept to the dry sand above the tide line, where every step slid back. Over the boom Benal could hear every turtle's long hard sigh at once, and it hurt.

After a while Teva stopped and shaded her eyes. The shimmer hid everything farther down the beach than a shout would carry.

"I can't see it," she said.

Benal stood still and let the beach come in. Far down to his right, beyond where he could see, the swell was breaking another way.

"It's that way," he said. "It's breaking on stone down there. Not sand."

He started walking. Behind him he heard Keo's spike go into the sand, then Teva's steps.

Some way on, a turtle had dug in on their line. She lay with her head out and her eyes running, and when they came near she did not stop.

Keo let himself down on his good knee behind her and reached into the hole under her tail. "Don't touch her. Just catch them."

"She doesn't mind?" Benal said.

"She can't stop now."

They came one at a time, then two, soft and white, and Keo handed them back. The shell gave under Benal's thumb like a skin. He tore the first one open with his teeth and sucked it out, warm and slick and salty, the best thing he had eaten since the honey. He ate four before he thought about anything else. Teva ate beside him with her eyes shut.

"Put some in the fieldpack," she said.

He packed them in around the board until it would hold no more.

A shadow ran over the sand at his feet.

Benal was down in a crouch with his arm over his head and his other hand on his forearm, for the flash that was not there any more. It was a flybeak, a small one. Keo was watching him. Benal got up and brushed the sand off.

Out past the surf a bird ducked under the swell, and Benal counted. He was past forty before it came up, far out, with something in its beak.

They walked. The sun climbed, and the stone got louder.

"There," Keo said.

It came up out of the shimmer where the beach ended: a long spine of rock, ten or fifteen meters high, <!-- @WF-firstwalk.rock.height: 10-15m --> the one high thing on the whole coast.

"How did you know?" Teva said.

"Stone sounds different," he said. "Sand takes a wave and hisses. Stone throws it back, sharp, with a slap at the end, and then the suck going out. With no wind you can hear it a long way."

"All the way from back there?"

"From back there."

She nodded. For once somebody had wanted to hear it.

At the foot of the rock the swell went silent. He could see it still breaking white on the stone, and he could not hear it. His skin prickled, and the hairs stood up on his arms and the back of his neck, the way they did when a storm was right overhead.

Then came a sound he had no name for, high and hard, rising and falling and rising again, every rise the same as the one before, to a hair. Nothing alive made the same sound twice. With it came the smell of the air after lightning.

Then there was only the swell, breaking on stone.

Keo and Teva were already going round the end of the rock. Benal stood above the wash and went back over the sound and the smell until he had the shape of both. Then he followed.

On the landward side the rock leaned out overhead, and under the overhang, four or five meters up, <!-- @WF-firstwalk.ledge.height: 4-5m --> ran a ledge, with a slope of drift sand climbing to it. Stones lay heaped along the ledge, and under them something too straight to be natural.

They went up the slope on their hands, Keo last and slowest, and rolled the stones off the ledge one at a time, each as much as one of them could lift. Then they scooped the sand away. The decking was split along its length. At the stern a hunk of rusty metal hung off the frame by one corner. Benal ran his hand down the nearest float and stopped.

"One of these is cracked," he said.

"The other one will hold it," Keo said.

It moved on the third heave, tipped over the lip and slid down the slope faster than they wanted. They dragged it round the end of the rock by its rail and down to the water, a body's length at a time.

"What do we paddle with?" Teva said.

Keo put his hand on the rail, a row of flat blades set close along the frame. "The rail. Ben, the other end."

They worked two of the blades back and forth between them until they came away.

Then they climbed the rock from its end, Keo last, on his hands and his good knee.

From the top, above the shimmer, the sea lay flat and shining. Far out on it there was a low line, too straight to be a wave. Something stood up off one end of it with square edges, catching the sun.

"That's Genesis," Benal said. "Five kilometers. Possibly a little more." <!-- @WF-genesis.offshore.seen: 5km -->

Beyond it, along the edge of the sky, lay a band of black that had not been there at dawn.

Teva stood with her eyes shut. Then she pointed. "It's on my north. A little left of it."

"We can see it, Teva," Keo said.

"Once the sea's up we won't."

They went down. The boat floated low, its cracked float lower, and the water was warm around Benal's legs. The deck flexed where it was split. Keo came aboard last on his good knee and leaned on the spike, the boat slid off the sand, and Teva poled with the bare shaft over the other side. Benal knelt in the middle with a blade in his hands and the fieldpack against his chest, and the rock went slowly back behind them.

A drop off the blade had landed on his forearm. The hairs under it stood thick and dark, bigger than the hairs around it, as if he were looking at them from closer. The records said a curved clear thing bent light. He watched it until it dried.

When he looked up, the black was nearer.
