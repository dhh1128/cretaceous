The palms ended at first light. Past the last of them the salt scrub thinned to bare ground, and the bare ground ran out level to a line that had to be sand.

Benal stopped at the edge of it. His legs had done the last of the dark without much help from him.

The air was wrong. All night a breeze had come in off the sea. Now there was none. The heat sat on his face already, though the sun was not up, and the fronds behind him hung still. The insect wall had thinned with the palms, and every sound on the coast came to him whole, each in its place: the surf beyond the sand, birds he could not see, a tick and a pop somewhere out on the mud.

"There's no wind," he said.

Keo had come up beside him with his weight on the spike. "Then nothing smells us coming."

"There was wind all night."

Teva looked up. She had her own pack again, and she held her head carefully under the helmet, as though it might spill.

"The sky's clear," she said.

"I know."

The flat looked like ground. Low ridges of broken shell ran across it, and between them the silt lay smooth and shining. Channels wound through it, all running the same way, out. That was the tide.

They went out along the ridges, Keo first, testing each step with the spike. The shell shifted under Benal's boots, but it held. With no wind, sandflies found them.

At the first channel he stopped and listened. The water ran shallow and quick over the silt.

"It's all running out," he said. "When it turns, these channels fill first, and then they join. Three hours, I think."

"Then we don't stop," Keo said.

Where one ridge ended a long stride short of the next, Keo went first. His good leg found the far shell. His bad one came down short, in the silt, and the silt took it to the knee.

"It's got me," Keo said.

Teva was behind him on the ridge. "Don't pull straight up."

"I know. Rock it." He worked his hip, and the silt sucked at him. "Ben, take the arm."

Benal stepped past him onto the far ridge and reached back with his right hand. His left was not worth anything any more, and Keo would have felt it. He took Keo by the forearm. Teva had him by the belt.

"If the tide came in now," Benal said, "you'd drown standing up."

"Pull, Ben," Teva said.

He pulled while Keo rocked and Teva hauled, and the leg came up black to the knee, stinking of the springs, with a sound like a mouth letting go. The boot came with it. Keo sat down hard on the shell.

At the last gap Keo stepped across and turned and held his hand back. Teva took it and came across on it. He let go on the other side.

The barrier beach was a long low hump of sand, no higher than a man. From the top of it Benal turned around. Beyond the flat and the palms was everything they had walked.

"We couldn't walk back now," he said. "Eighty kilometers, on what we've got? We'd be dead before the river." <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->

"Nobody's walking back," Keo said. "It's the boat or nothing."

Teva had already turned to the sea.

Each swell broke with a boom Benal felt in the sand before he heard it, and a long hiss going back. The booms came slow, and he counted the gaps. The sea smelled of something he had no name for, sharp and green, like nothing at home, and the air left salt on his lips.

The beach ran off into haze in both directions, and along the whole length of it turtles were heaving up out of the surf by the dozen. Higher up others were dug in, throwing sand behind them in spurts. Crocs lay at the waterline where the turtles had to cross them, and the smell of blood came up the beach from where they were working. The three kept to the soft dry sand above the tide line, where every step slid back, a long throw from the water.

Over the surf's boom he could hear every one of the turtles, their flippers throwing sand and their breath going out of them in long hard sighs. With nothing to take the edge off it, it came into his head all at once, and it hurt.

After a while Teva stopped and shaded her eyes. The shimmer had come up off the sand and hid everything farther down the beach than a shout would carry.

"I can't see it," she said.

Benal stood still and let the beach come in. He listened past the near waves. Far down to his right, beyond where he could see, the swell was breaking another way.

"It's that way," he said. "It's breaking on stone down there. Not sand."

He started walking. Behind him he heard Keo's spike go into the sand, then Teva's steps.

Some way on, a turtle had dug in on their line. She lay with her head out and her eyes running. When they came near she did not stop.

Keo let himself down on his good knee behind her and reached into the hole under her tail.

"Don't touch her," he said. "Just catch them."

"She doesn't mind?" Benal said.

"She can't stop now. Not till she's done."

They came one at a time, and then two, soft and white. Keo caught them and handed them back. The shell gave under Benal's thumb like a skin. He tore the first one open with his teeth and sucked it out. It was warm and slick and tasted of salt, and it was the best thing he had eaten since the honey. He ate four before he thought about anything else. Teva was eating beside him with her eyes shut.

"Put some in the fieldpack," she said.

He packed them in around the board until it would hold no more. The turtle went on laying.

A shadow ran over the sand at his feet.

Benal was down in a crouch with his arm over his head. His other hand had gone to his forearm, for the flash that was not there any more. It was a flybeak, a small one, no wider across the wings than his arms. Keo was watching him. Benal got up and brushed the sand off.

Out past the surf a bird sat low in the swell. It ducked its head and went under, and Benal counted. He was past forty before it came up again, far out, with something in its beak.

They walked, and the sun climbed. Benal listened to the stone the whole way, and it got louder.

"There," Keo said.

It came up out of the shimmer where the beach ended: a long spine of rock, ten or fifteen meters high, <!-- @WF-firstwalk.rock.height: 10-15m --> the one high thing on the whole coast. The swell broke against its foot.

"How did you know?" Teva said.

"Stone sounds different," he said. "Sand takes a wave and hisses. Stone throws it back, sharp, with a slap at the end, and then the suck going out. With no wind you can hear it a long way."

"All the way from back there?"

"From back there."

She nodded. For once somebody had wanted to hear it.

At the foot of the rock the sound of the swell went flat.

Then something else came, a sound he did not have a name for, high and hard, rising and falling and rising again, and every rise was the same as the one before, to a hair. Nothing alive made the same sound twice. With it came the smell of the air after lightning, sharp in his nose.

Then there was only the swell, breaking on stone.

Keo and Teva were already going around the end of it, to the landward side. Benal stood with the water running over his boots and went back over the sound and the smell until he had the shape of both. He did not tell them. Then he followed.

On the landward side the rock leaned out overhead, and under the overhang, four or five meters up, <!-- @WF-firstwalk.ledge.height: 4-5m --> ran a ledge. A slope of drift sand climbed to it from the end of the rock. Stones lay heaped along the ledge, and from under the stones and the sand the end of a float stuck out, chalky and smooth.

They went up the slope on their hands, the sand running away under them, Keo last and slowest. The stones came off one at a time. Each was as much as one of them could lift and no more, and they rolled them off the lip of the ledge. Then they knelt and scooped the sand from around the floats with their hands, Keo on his good knee with the other leg out straight. Under the sand the decking was split along its length. At the stern a motor hung off the frame by one corner, and where its bolts had been there was only rust. Benal ran his hand down the nearest float and stopped.

"One of the floats is cracked," he said.

"The rest will hold it," Keo said.

They got behind it and pushed. It moved on the third heave, tipped over the lip and went down the slope on its floats, faster than they wanted, and stopped at the bottom in a spray of sand. Keo came down after it and his knee went out under him at the foot of the slope. They dragged it round the end of the rock by its rail and down to the water, a body's length at a time, resting between. It was heavier than three of them should have had to drag, and they dragged it.

"What do we paddle with?" Teva said.

Keo put his hand on the rail. It was made of flat blades, set close along the edge of the frame. "The rail. Ben, the other end."

They bent two of the blades back and forth between them until they came away.

Then they climbed the rock from its end, where it was lowest, Keo last, on his hands and his good knee.

From the top, above the shimmer, the sea lay glassy. Far out on it there was a low line, too straight to be a wave. Something stood up off one end of it with square edges, catching the sun.

"That's Genesis," Benal said. "Five kilometers. Possibly a little more." <!-- @WF-genesis.offshore.seen: 5km -->

Beyond it, along the edge of the sky, lay a band of black that had not been there at dawn.

Teva stood with her eyes shut. Then she lifted her arm and pointed. "It's on my north. A little left of it."

"We can see it, Teva," Keo said.

"Once the sea's up we won't."

They went down. The boat floated low with its cracked float lower, and the water was warm around his legs. Keo held it steady while Teva climbed on, then Benal. The deck flexed where it was split.

"Push," Keo said. He came aboard on his good knee and leaned on the spike, and the boat slid out off the sand. Teva had the bare shaft down over the other side, poling. Benal knelt in the middle with a blade in his hands and the fieldpack against his chest, and the rock went slowly back behind them.

When he looked up again, the black was nearer.
