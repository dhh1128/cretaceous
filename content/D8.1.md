They ate at the fence in the first gray light, sitting on the ground with their backs to the stakes, and nobody said it was good. Teva shared out the pinyons. Benal squeezed the honey skin and held out the end of it to each of them in turn, and they took it on their fingers and licked them clean.

When there was a handful of pinyons left, Teva tied them back into the cloth, and Benal stoppered the honey skin with a little still in it.

"If it goes badly," Benal said, "we'll have saved those for nothing. Possibly the crocs like pinyons."

"Eat," Keo said.

"I'm making a point."

"Make it with your mouth full."

Benal ate. Keo's head had started before the light did. It sat behind his eyes like a hand pressing, and he had not pushed at anything yet.

The herd was where it had been at dusk, along the slow pool above the narrows. Keo went up onto the cut bank over it, high enough that nothing in the river could reach him, and lay on his stomach in the ferns and looked down. Eleven of them, he thought, maybe twelve. Big gray-green backs in the shade of the trees along the water, the calves underneath, tails swinging. They had eaten the bank bare in a long strip and stood now in the shade with their heads low, as hadrosaurs did on any morning that promised heat. The heat would hold them. That was the whole of his luck.

At home he did this every few days, walking the work herd out to the river to drink and back to the basin, with a stick he never needed and his sense out ahead of him. He knew what a hadrosaur thought about water, which was mostly that it wanted some. At home there were gratings across the river at both ends and a watcher on the bank, and still no one let one stand in deep.

Twice in the morning a young male wandered off along the bank after better grazing. Twice Keo reached and pushed, *good here, cool here, stay*, and felt the animal's small dull wish turn over and go back the other way. Both times the pressure behind his eyes went to an ache, and the ache did not go away when he stopped.

When he came back down to the rise, Benal looked at his face and did not ask.

The morning went on. They checked the bindings on the packs, and then Benal checked them again. They tied the spears and Teva's spike flat along the packs so nothing would trail in the water. Then they untied them and tied them shorter. Keo poured his own water skin and the spare into the other two. He blew the empty ones full and tied off the necks, and showed them how.

"You put your arm through here," he said. "Like that. Then it doesn't matter if your hands slip."

"Do your hands slip?" Teva said.

"Not yet."

Teva sat at the edge of the rise for a long time looking down at the narrows. From up here they were plain enough. The big rounded stones stood across the river in a ragged line that stepped downstream as it went, with low quick water between most of them, rocky and pale. In two places the water ran dark and fast and nothing showed underneath it. Old water marks stood on the stones high above today's river, a long way above her head if she had been standing at their feet.

"Say it back," she said.

"Teva."

"Say it back. You first."

So he said it back. He would push one of them out into the pool, deeper than it wanted to go. When it started to scream, they would go in at the first stone and cross where the water was quick, while everything in the river went to the noise. Benal said his part, which was to hold the cord and keep still. Teva said hers, which was to go first.

It had sounded better last night. Said in daylight, by three people sitting on their packs, it sounded like something children would make up.

"From the bank or from the stone?" Benal said. "The pushing."

"The stone," Keo said. "Then nobody waits for anybody."

"Can you do it from the stone? It's farther."

"I can do it from the stone."

Teva looked at him then. He did not know what she saw.

Around midday Noli went down to the water.

She had been lying in the shade at the edge of the rise all morning with her head on her forelegs, watching the far bank. Now she got up and went down the slope by herself and stood by the first stone with her tail out stiff behind her and her head low, looking across. Keo felt her fear through the bond, the long sour fear she had of water. Then he felt it go still.

"Noli," he said, and stood up, and then he did not push at her. He was saving it, and if he reached for her now she would only look back.

She went.

She went up onto the first stone in one jump and from it to the next in a long leap, her arms out, the feathers along them spread and stiff, so that she came down onto the far rock lightly, steadied, nothing like flying. Then the next. Where the stones were close she took them one after another like a game. Where the shallow water ran between them she waded, high-stepping, furious, with her head up. At the first dark gap she stood on the edge of the stone for a moment, and Keo's whole chest went tight. Then she went in and swam low and fast and badly, her head stretched out flat on the water. She came out on the stone below scrabbling with all four feet.

Nothing in the river moved.

She did the second gap the same way. Then she was over the last stones and up the mud and up the far bank. She stood on the top of it in the sun and shook herself all over, and Keo let his breath go.

"Well," Benal said, very quietly. "That's settled, then."

"It isn't," Teva said. "She's small, and she's fast. And she's lucky."

"I know," Keo said. He did know. It proved nothing. His hands were shaking anyway.

On the far bank Noli lifted her head and called. It was the call she gave at home when he was late to the edge of the buffer, high and ragged, and it carried over the water like a thrown stone. She called again. She did not stop.

The lay-up came on, and the heat with it. They lay in the shade on the rise, well back from the water, and none of them slept. The sun did not seem to move at all.

"The chute water," Benal said. "If a leaf takes about two breaths to go through it, and the chute is about four body lengths—"

"Ben."

"I'm getting there." He got there. He gave them a speed, and then he lay back and looked at the leaves overhead. "It doesn't help. I don't know why I did that."

"What's the herd doing?" Teva said.

Keo reached up the bank. "Standing in the shade. Two of them lying down. The big one at the far end keeps going to the water and not drinking."

"Why?"

"Doesn't like the smell. They know something's in it. They're not stupid." He shut his eyes. "They'll go down anyway when it's hot enough. Thirst wins over knowing."

Noli called again, across the water.

"She's showing off," Benal said.

"She's not showing off. She's frightened. She wants me over there."

"Your animal is braver than you," Teva said, without opening her eyes.

"She's braver than all of us."

"I said you."

He laughed before he could stop it, and then lay there with his arm over his face. After a while, without thinking about it, he was humming. It was the song Liaso hummed on the watch, the long one, and Keo did not know he knew all of it until he was halfway through.

Benal hummed the next line with him, off key.

Teva did not tell them to stop.

Noli called and called. She had been calling for most of an hour, and every time she did it, all three of them flinched. Keo wanted to shout at her to be quiet. He could not shout at her across a river full of crocs. He pushed *quiet, stay, good* across the water instead, and felt it land, and felt her not care.

She called again, the worst one yet, long and cracked at the top.

Down at the pool, one of the herd turned out of the shade and walked down to the water.

Keo was up on his elbows before he had decided anything. It was the big one from the far end, a cow with a scar along her flank, the one that had kept going to the water all morning and not drinking. He knew her kind. At home there was one just like her in the work herd, an old cow who always drank first and walked in deepest, who would stand up to her chest in the river with her eyes half shut. She wanted the water. She wanted it more than she was afraid of it.

He could push that.

"Now?" Teva said.

"Now."

They went down off the rise together, Keo carrying the pot and the cord, Teva and Benal with the packs strapped tight. The pot stayed shut. The herd would smell it. At the first stone Keo uncoiled the cord and laid the blown-up skins at his feet.

The light had come all the way into the water. He could see the stones under the shallows, gold and brown and pale, every one, and the dark of the chutes where it could not reach. The river lay as flat as they had needed it to be.
