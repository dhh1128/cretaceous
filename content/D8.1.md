They ate at the fence in first light, their backs to the stakes. Teva shared out the pinyons. Benal squeezed the honey skin and held out the end of it to each of them in turn, and they took it on their fingers and licked them clean.

When a handful of pinyons was left, Teva tied them back into the cloth, and Benal stoppered the skin with a little honey still in it.

"If it goes badly," Benal said, "we'll have saved those for nothing. Possibly the crocs like pinyons."

"Eat," Keo said.

"I'm making a point."

"Make it with your mouth full."

Benal ate. The ache sat behind Keo's eyes like a hand pressing, and he hadn't pushed at anything yet.

The herd was still along the slow pool above the narrows. Keo climbed the cut bank over it, out of the river's reach, and lay on his stomach in the ferns. Eleven, maybe twelve: gray-green backs under the trees along the water, calves beneath, tails swinging. They had stripped the bank bare in a long swath and stood now in the shade with their heads low. The heat would hold them. That was the whole of his luck.

From the work herd at home he knew what a hadrosaur thought about water, which was mostly that it wanted some. But at home there were gratings across the river at both ends and a watcher on the bank, and still nobody let one stand in deep.

Twice a young male wandered off along the bank after better grazing. Twice Keo reached and pushed, *good here, cool here, stay*, and felt the animal's small dull wish turn over. Each time the ache behind his eyes dug deeper, and stayed.

When he came back down to the rise, Benal looked at his face and did not ask.

The morning dragged. They checked the pack bindings, and Benal checked them again. They lashed the spikes flat to the packs, then untied them and lashed them shorter. Keo poured his water and Benal's into Teva's skin, leaving the spare with its honey, and blew the two empties full and tied off the necks.

"You put your arm through here," he said. "Like that. Then it doesn't matter if your hands slip."

"Do your hands slip?" Teva said.

"Not yet."

Teva sat a long time at the edge of the rise, looking down at the narrows. The big stones stepped across the river in a ragged line, slanting downstream, with low quick water between most of them. In two places the water ran dark and fast with nothing showing under it. Old water marks stood on the stones high above today's river.

"Say it back," she said.

"Teva."

"Say it back. You first."

So he did. He would push one out into the pool, deeper than it wanted to go. When it screamed, they would go in at the first stone and cross the quick water while everything in the river went to the screaming. Benal's part was to hold the cord and keep still. Teva's was to go first.

In daylight, from three people sitting on their packs, it sounded like something children would make up.

"From the bank or from the stone?" Benal said. "The pushing."

"The stone," Keo said. "Then nobody waits for anybody."

"Can you do it from the stone? It's farther."

"I can do it from the stone."

Teva looked at him then. He did not know what she saw.

Noli had lain all morning in the shade at the edge of the rise, watching the far bank. Around midday she got up, went down the slope alone and stood by the first stone, tail stiff, head low, looking across. Through the bond Keo felt her old sour fear of water. Then he felt it go still.

"Noli," he said, and stood. He did not push at her. If he reached for her now she would only look back.

She went.

She took the first stone in one jump and the second in a long leap, arms out, feathers spread stiff, and came down light and steadied, nothing like flying. Then the next. Where the stones stood close she hopped them like a game. Where shallow water ran between, she waded, high-stepping, furious, head up. At the first deep gap she stopped on the lip of the stone, and Keo's chest clenched. Then she went in and swam low and fast and badly, head stretched flat on the water, and came out scrabbling on the stone below.

Nothing in the river moved.

The second gap the same. Then she was over the last stones and up the mud to the top of the far bank, where she stood in the sun and shook herself all over. Keo let his breath go.

"Well," Benal whispered. "That's settled, then."

"It isn't," Teva said. "She's small, and she's fast. And she's lucky."

"I know," Keo said. His hands were shaking anyway.

On the far bank Noli lifted her head and called, high and ragged, the call she gave at home when he was late to the edge of the buffer. It carried over the water like a thrown stone. She called again, and kept on.

The heat came on. They lay in the shade on the rise. The sun hung where it was.

"The chute water," Benal said. "If a leaf takes about two breaths to go through it, and the chute is about four body lengths—"

"Ben."

"I'm getting there." He got there. He gave them a speed, and then he lay back and looked at the leaves overhead. "It doesn't help. I don't know why I did that."

"What's the herd doing?" Teva said.

Keo looked up the bank. "Standing in the shade. Two lying down. The big one at the far end keeps going to the water and not drinking."

"Why?"

"Doesn't like something about it. She keeps coming back, though." He shut his eyes. "They'll go down anyway when it's hot enough. Thirst wins over knowing."

Noli called again.

"She's showing off," Benal said.

"She's not showing off. She's scared. She wants me over there."

"Your animal is braver than you," Teva said, without opening her eyes.

"She's braver than all of us."

"I said you."

He laughed before he could stop it, and lay with his arm over his face. After a while he was humming without meaning to: Liaso's song, the long one from the watch. He didn't know he knew all of it until he was halfway through.

Benal hummed the next line with him, off key.

"Wrong verse," Teva said, and hummed the right one.

When the song ran out, Benal sat up. Where the ground broke at the edge of the rise there were flat stones, and without seeming to notice he set one on another, and another, turning each until it sat: five, in a crooked column no higher than his hand. Keo couldn't have said what it was for.

Noli called and called, for most of an hour, and every time, all three of them flinched. Keo wanted to shout at her to be quiet. Across a river full of crocs. He lay with his teeth together and let her call.

She called again, the worst one yet, long and cracked at the top.

Down at the pool, one of the herd turned out of the shade and walked down to the water.

Keo was up on his elbows before he'd decided anything. It was the big one from the far end, a cow with a scar along her flank. He knew her kind: the work herd at home had an old cow who always drank first and waded deepest, and stood chest-deep in the river with her eyes half shut. This one wanted the water more than she was afraid of it.

He could push that.

"Now?" Teva said.

"Now."

Benal got up, and on his way past knocked the stones over without looking. Keo did not ask.

They went down off the rise together, Keo with the pot and the cord, Teva and Benal with the packs strapped tight. The pot stayed shut; the herd would smell it. At the first stone Keo uncoiled the cord and laid the blown-up skins at his feet.

The light had come all the way into the water. He could see every stone under the shallows, gold where the sun lay on them, and the dark of the chutes where it couldn't reach. The river lay as flat as they had needed it.
