They lay up back in the trees, where the ground rose a little and they could see the river between the trunks.

Teva sat with her back to a trunk. She had tried, once, to walk out to where Keo was, and her legs had simply stopped. Finally she had turned around and come back.

A little upstream a steep shelf of stone stood out over the current, twice a man's height above the surface. Keo had climbed up onto it. He slouched now with his spike grounded, unsuited, easy, looking down at the water. Every so often he picked a broken ginkgo leaf off the stone and turned it in his fingers and let it drop.

Teva watched him do it and felt her jaw set. Maybe a croc could not lunge that high. But given what had happened to his sister, how could he just stand there? He was going to stumble and get himself killed. It was stupid, being easy like that. Stupid.

Benal started it, because somebody had to.

"A raft," he said. He was sitting with his knees up and his elbows on them, the way he sat over a board. "Logs. Bound together. We'd sit on it instead of being in it—" He stopped.

She watched him get there.

"It's the loudest thing on the river," he said, in a different voice. "Isn't it. Paddling. For the whole width. In full view of the bank." He looked across at the long shapes in the far mud. "And it'd take days to find logs or cut stuff with knives. Sorry."

"Still worth saying," Keo murmured.

Benal rubbed his face with both hands.

"The saropo, then. The ones in the lake. They walked through it as if it wasn't there." He spread his hands. "We find a herd going this way and we ride one across. Up on its back. Nothing in the river's going to bother something that size."

Keo turned around and spoke quietly.

"No," he said. "Nobody can steer one. I tried."

"You did?" Benal said.

"Yesterday. On the ridge. I pushed at one." He was turning a leaf in his fingers again. "It was like pushing a wall. It didn't even know I was there. If you're on its back you go where it goes, and if it stops in the middle of the river you're standing in the middle of the river, and if it shakes you off—" He put the leaf down. "I can't move one. I can't move it at all."

Teva realized that never before had she heard him say he couldn't do something.

"How hard did you push?" she said.

He did not look back at her. "As hard as I've ever pushed anything."

They sat in silence for a long time. Out on the water something rolled and went under, and the rings spread and spread and were gone.

"We could go home," Benal said.

He said it quietly, to his knees, and Teva saw Keo's head turn. Benal did not look at either of them. Yesterday's ease had gone out of his face, and what was there now was hard to watch.

"Is it your chest?" Keo said. "Ben. If it's your chest, nobody'd think less of you."

"It isn't my chest," Benal said. "I meant all of us." He looked at Keo then, and Teva saw him hear it: what Keo had thought he meant.

"Not back," Teva said.

The rest was behind her teeth. She could not face Elana with nothing done. She could not sit in that chamber again and watch it get up to go home and consider. The three of them had walked out through the tangle before dawn because no one else would. They were the only answer anybody had given. She would rather be in the river.

Benal nodded slowly.

So there was only one thing left. They had all known it from the start.

"So we find the narrows," she said.

Every child knew the First Walk had crossed at a place where the river ran tight between some stones. And that spot was roughly on a line between the Enclave and Genesis.

She stood up. Her legs let her, this time, because she was not going toward the water.

She shut her eyes and found the pull in her bones, the north that was always there, and laid the old heading against it. Then she laid their own walk against that: the suit's distance on her arm since the tangle, and the run in the fog on the first morning, the turns she had felt with her whole body and rebuilt afterward, leg by leg. They had drifted in the fog, downstream of the First Walk's line, and she wasn't sure how far.

She opened her eyes and pointed upstream along the bank.

"That way," she said. "Probably not far. A few kilometers. Maybe less." She began walking.

Keo looked at her, then at Benal. He picked up his spike. Benal followed. Noli trotted out ahead.

They walked the mud and fern and leaf litter under the ginkgos for an hour, then stopped where the trees thinned. A herd of grazing hadrosaurs lifted their heads, chewing as they assessed the humans. Seeing no threat, they lowered their heads and went back to eating ferns.

Keo studied them. Teva wondered why. They weren't that interesting.

Eventually they pressed on.

When they reached the narrows, Teva knew it by the sound. The river had been silent; ahead, it was talking, low and steady. Then they rounded a bend and saw great rounded stones standing out of a surface that was reflecting the sunset's red and gold. There was a line of the rocks, dark with old water marks, with the channel squeezed between them. Between them the water ran quick and creased and glittering. Upstream, the flow opened out again into a wide slow pool with a gravelly bank. More hadrosaurs milled about, apparently working up courage to approach for a drink.

Noli ran ahead to the nearest stone, wedged against the bank, jumped up, and looked back at the trio quizzically.

Keo spoke first, looking upstream at the herd.

"One of those," he said. "Not a saropo. But I can push a hadrosaur." He touched his forehead without seeming to notice he had done it. "Not far, but enough. One of them approaches up there to drink, and I push it deeper than it wants to go. Then everything in the river comes to it."

"Yes," Benal said. "But far enough upstream. Not close to the rocks." He pointed at the quick water between the stones. "And then we go in here, in the fast part. The current kills our ripples and covers the noise." He frowned. "As long as nobody splashes. We can't splash."

"Nobody splashes," Teva said.

"And not now." Benal looked at the light on the water, which was going gold and hard to see into. "In the middle of the afternoon. When the sun's high enough to see down into it, and everything on the bank is lying up in the heat."

They looked at Teva. She knew what they were waiting for.

"They find you by the surface," she said. "Whatever moves the water, they feel from a long way off. So we make one place on the river the loudest thing in the world, and we make ourselves the quietest."

Upstream, a pair of hadrosaurs drank quickly, then retreated to higher ground.

The river ran talking between the stones, exactly as wide as it was going to be tomorrow.

They had a plan now. That was worse, Teva thought, than not having one. Now it was going to happen.
