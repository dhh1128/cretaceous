They lay up on the bank because there was nowhere else to go, in the thin shade of the ginkgos, and none of them slept.

Teva sat with her back to a trunk, ten paces from the mud, and found that her body would not let her any closer. She had not decided that. She had tried, once, to walk down to where Keo was. Her legs had simply stopped, the way they had stopped at the backwater on the first morning, and she had stood there in front of the others and then turned round and come back and sat down as if that had been her plan. Now she sat and looked at the river because she could not stop looking at it, and she hated that most of all.

Keo was down at the edge. He crouched on the black mud with his spear across his knees and his bare forearms resting on it, unsuited, easy, a body's length from the water, looking out at it the way he might have looked at a field. Every so often he picked up a broken ginkgo leaf and turned it in his fingers and put it down again. Teva watched him do it and felt her jaw set. He was going to get himself killed, being easy like that. He was going to get one of them killed.

It was Benal who started it, because somebody had to.

"A raft," he said. He was sitting with his knees up and his elbows on them, the way he sat over a board. "Logs. Bound together. We'd sit on it instead of being in it, which seems to me to be the essential point—" He stopped.

She watched him get there. It took him about as long as it took her.

"It's the loudest thing on the river," he said, in a different voice. "Isn't it. Paddling. For the whole width. In full view of the bank." He looked across at the far mud, at the long dark shapes lying on it. "And it would take days to cut the logs with knives. Possibly more. I'm sorry. I knew that before I'd finished saying it. I said it anyway."

"It was worth saying," Keo said, from the water's edge, without turning round.

Benal did not look as if he believed that. He rubbed his face with both hands and then, after a while, said something else, half laughing, because he could not help himself.

"The saropo, then. The ones in the lake. They walked through it as if it wasn't there." He spread his hands. "We find a herd going this way and we ride one across. Up on its back. Nothing in the river is going to bother something that size."

Keo turned round.

He did not get up. He looked at Benal, then at her, then down at the ground between his boots. When he spoke it was quieter than he usually spoke, as if he would rather not be heard.

"No," he said. "Nobody can steer one. I tried."

"You tried," Benal said.

"Yesterday. On the ridge. I pushed at one." He was turning a leaf in his fingers again. "It was like pushing a wall. It didn't even know I was there. If you're on its back you go where it goes, and if it stops in the middle of the river you're standing in the middle of the river, and if it shakes you off—" He put the leaf down. "I can't move one. I can't move it at all."

Teva had never heard him say that he could not do something. She looked at him for a long moment. He did not look back at her, and something shifted in her that she did not have a name for and did not want one.

"Then we walk," Keo said, getting up, brushing the mud off his knees, and his voice was his own again. "Upstream. It has to narrow somewhere. Or round. Rivers start somewhere. We go round the end of it."

"Where?" Teva said.

He looked at her.

"Where does it narrow?" she said. "How far? Do you know?"

"We'd find it."

"In how many days?" She did not raise her voice. She did not have to. "We have the pinyons and the honey. Benal counted them. That's three days if we're careful, with foraging. Nobody knows how far upstream it goes before it narrows on its own, or if it does. And round the end of it is the other way from where we're going, and nobody's ever been."

Keo opened his mouth and shut it again. She watched it cost him. Walking was the only thing he had been sure of since the tangle, and she had taken it away from him in four sentences. She was not sorry. She was right, and that was what mattered.

It was very quiet after that. A long way out on the water something rolled and went under, and the rings spread and spread and were gone.

"We could go home," Benal said.

He said it quietly, to his knees, and Teva saw Keo's head come round. Benal did not look at either of them. His face was the face from the tree yesterday, the one that had said *this is the best day I've ever had*, and it was doing something now that was hard to watch.

"Not back," Teva said.

That was all she said, and she did not let any of the rest out of her mouth. But it was all there behind her teeth. The chamber, and Elana on the stone floor with the small body. The next night, the Council on its dais with its hands open on its knees, talking and talking, and getting up at the end to go home and consider, without a vote. The three of them walking out through the tangle before dawn because no one else would. They were the only answer anybody had given. She would rather be in the river.

Benal nodded slowly, as if she had said all of it aloud. Perhaps, she thought, she had.

So there was only one thing left. They had all known it from the start, and none of them had wanted to be the one to say it.

"The narrows," she said.

Every child knew that much. The First Walk had crossed the river at the narrows, where it runs between the stones; two hundred people had left Genesis and ninety-four had come out the other side, every one of them suited and armed, and the narrows were the one fixed point on the whole route. Where exactly, no one knew. A heading from the Enclave, and a distance, roughly. That was all that had come down.

She stood up. Her legs let her, this time, because she was not going toward the water.

She shut her eyes and found the pull in her bones, the north that was always there, and laid the old heading against it. Then she laid their own walk against that: the suit's distance on her arm since the tangle, and the run in the fog on the first morning, the turns she had felt with her whole body and rebuilt afterward, leg by leg, as well as anyone could. She had known then that she had not got it all back. They had drifted, in the fog, downstream of the line the First Walk took, and she had never been able to say exactly how far.

She opened her eyes and pointed upstream along the bank.

"That way," she said. "Not far. A few kilometers. Maybe less."

Keo looked at her. Then he picked up his spear and went where she pointed, without a word. Benal got up with a hand on the ginkgo trunk and followed, and Teva went after them both with the river on her left the whole way and did not once look at it.

The bank went on, mud and fern and leaf litter, under the ginkgos. Halfway along it they came on hadrosaurs.

There was a herd of them grazing the bank, twenty or so, big and heavy and slow, tearing at the ferns at the water's edge and moving on, their broad tails swinging. The three of them stopped well back. Noli, who had been ranging ahead, came back fast and pressed against Keo's leg. The hadrosaurs lifted their heads and looked at them, chewing. Then they decided the three of them did not matter, and went back to the ferns.

Keo watched them for a long time. Teva saw him watch them. He did not say anything, and he did not have to.

They found the narrows with the light already going off the water.

She knew it before she saw it, from the sound. The river had been silent all day; here, ahead, it was talking, low and steady. Then the bank came round, and there they were: great rounded stones standing out of the river from one side to the other, a line of them, gray and dark with old water marks, with the channel squeezed between them. Between the stones the water ran quick and creased and glittering. Above them, upstream, it opened out again into a wide slow pool, flat as the rest, where the hadrosaurs were coming down to drink.

Noli went straight to the nearest stone and jumped up onto it and looked across at the next one, her head on one side.

For a while none of them spoke. Then they all did.

It came together the way things did when all three of them were working on the same thing, without anybody deciding who went first. Keo said it before anyone, looking upstream at the herd in the shallows of the slow pool.

"One of those," he said. "Not a saropo. One of those. I can hold one of those. Not for long." He touched his forehead without seeming to notice he had done it. "Long enough. If I drive one into the slow water up there and hold it there, everything in the river comes to it."

"Fifty paces up," Benal said. "More. Where it's slow, so it stays put. Not here." He was looking at the quick water between the stones the way he looked at numbers. "And then we go in here, in the fast part. The current takes our ripples down, away from it. Small ones can't go upstream against water this quick; they're too slow. They just get swept down." He frowned. "As long as nobody splashes. A splash is a big wave. A big wave might get up."

"Nobody splashes," Teva said.

"And not now." Benal looked at the light on the water, which was going gold and flat and hard to see into. "In the middle of the afternoon. When the sun's high enough to see down into it, and everything on the bank is lying up in the heat."

They looked at Teva. She knew what they were waiting for. It was the one thing she had been sure of before any of this began, the thing she had stood in the yazhi pool and said with her own mouth to a ring of small serious faces.

"They hear the surface," she said. "That's all they hear. So we make one place on the river the loudest thing in the world, and we make ourselves the quietest."

None of them said it was a good plan, or thought it was.

The hadrosaurs drank in the slow water upstream. Noli was still on the first stone, looking at the second. The river ran between the stones, quick and quiet, exactly as wide as it was going to be tomorrow.

They had a plan now. That was worse, Teva thought, than not having one. Now it was going to happen.
