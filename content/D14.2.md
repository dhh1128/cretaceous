The reef reached Benal's ears in the afternoon heat, long before it showed itself. Then a white line stood up out of the flat water under the station: surf, breaking along the coral as far as he could see both ways.

Behind it lay Genesis, low ground under a pale crown of rock. Up close, the station had walls, square-cornered, and after all the days of fern and mud and palm his eyes kept going back to the corners.

His left arm had stopped answering an hour ago. He paddled on the right and let the left lie across his knee. The spray dried on his face as fast as it landed, crusting his lips and lashes and pulling the skin tight, and the corners of his mouth had split. Salt had found every cut on him and stayed. In front of him Keo pulled at his blade, his breath snagging at the top of each stroke. Beyond Keo, Teva sat with sweat running off her jaw; her suit cooled her only in patches now. Every so often she scooped a hand over the side, quick, without looking at the water, and wet her face and neck.

The surf came to him whole: one long roar that never stopped to breathe, the slap of each wave inside it and the hiss of each one dragging back over the coral.

"Ben," Teva said. "Where's the way in?"

"Wait."

He laid the blade across his knees and listened.

"A channel's deeper," he said, "so the swell shouldn't stand up there the same. I'd hear a gap in the noise."

"Do you?" Keo said.

"No. It's the same all along. Either there's no gap on this side, or it's buried under the rest."

"Then we go along till you hear one."

She pointed, and they turned the boat and worked along outside the break. The current wanted them nearer. Every few strokes Keo dug in on his side and pulled them off again, and the walls slid by behind the surf.

That morning he had heard stone through the haze, and the other two had walked where he said. Here every wave was stone.

"Anything?" Keo said.

"No."

He said it again, later, to Teva. Then he said, "If we go through here, we'll get there. In more pieces than we left in, possibly."

Teva pressed the back of her wrist hard against her forehead and held it there.

"Keep listening," she said.

Air moved on his neck after a day of none. The water round the boat lost its shine and turned to scales. The black that had sat on the horizon ahead of them all afternoon was over them, and the sun was gone.

The wind came off the land, at their backs, smelling of the plain, mud and palm. The rail's struts began to sing. The note climbed, and the boat slid toward the break.

"It's taking us in," Teva said.

Keo threw his blade down on the deck. "Down. Flat, both of you. Hands through the rail."

"The paddles—"

"Leave them. Hands through, Ben."

Benal lay on his face with the fieldpack under his chest and pushed his arms between the struts to the elbow. Keo dropped beside him with Teva's pack wedged between them, and Teva lay on Keo's far side.

Then the rain came, and took the last of the light. All he could see was his own forearm, where the film still glowed in patches.

"Teva!" Keo's voice was right beside him. "Which way's the station?"

"Wait," she said.

He heard her breath go in and stop.

"I can't." It came out cracked. "It's gone. I can't find it."

After that he could not hear her at all. The rain hammered the deck and the sea and his head until it was the only sound there was. The reef was in there somewhere. So were Keo and Teva. Listening was what he was for, and it was gone.

The sea got up under them. The boat climbed and hung and dropped, each climb higher than the last. At the top of one the wind got under the whole deck and tried to lift it. At the bottom the next wave came over them solid. A blade knocked at his shins on its way over the edge, then the other, and something heavy dragged across his back and was gone. When the water ran off, there was nothing between him and Keo.

The next one came over him with his mouth open. The sea went in at his collar and down the suit, into his mouth and nose at once. He came up choking. When he tried to breathe in, his chest stopped halfway, at the place the constrictor had left in it. He breathed in halves.

He had always thought drowning would be one thing. It came in pieces, with air between them, and never enough of the air.

At the top of a climb a strut snapped in his right hand.

His arm came free. The deck tipped and he slid on his chest toward the edge, the fieldpack dragging under him. His left hand caught the next strut and slid off.

A hand closed in the back of his suit, between his shoulders, and stopped him.

Keo. Benal could feel the whole of him through that hand. He must have had his good foot jammed against a strut and an arm locked through the rail, the rest of him stretched across the deck to keep Benal on it. The next wave came over them, and the hand did not open. It did not open for the one after, or the one after that. Every time the boat fell, Keo's breath stopped short at the rib, and went on.

"Got you," Keo said against his ear. Benal felt it more than heard it. "Got you."

All his life Keo had been the one who could do this, and he the one it was done for. He lay still and let it be done.

In a trough, the boat rose.

The sea was going down around the floats, and the boat went up anyway, slowly, on something under it that was not water. It hung there. Along the floats beneath him something dragged, long and rough, from his feet toward his head, and kept dragging after he thought it must be done. It was longer than the boat. Then the boat settled back into the trough.

It was alive. He had no name for it and did not need one. It had come up to touch them. His whole body went cold and tight, and he could not get his breath past it.

Keo's fist tightened in the back of his suit.

He lay with his face on the deck and the rain on his back, and waited for it to come back.

Nothing came.

Then it came from below, at the edge of the reef, where the break was close enough now to feel in the deck. The boat heaved under him and came apart. The deck was gone from under his chest, Keo's hand from his back, and he was in the water.

He went under, and stayed under.

The suit was trying, pulling his shoulders toward the air. Something pulled the other way, harder, by the strap across his chest: the fieldpack's strap had snagged on a broken strut, and the piece of frame was going down and taking him with it.

He got both hands on the pack. Through it he felt the edge of the board in its greased hide and the hard curve of the tooth. He held on. His chest had wanted air before he went under. Now it wanted air with the whole of his body. The pack went on down, into the black where the thing was.

He let go. He dragged the strap up over his head, and it tore at his ear going. Then he was rising without it.

*Do something real.*

His father's voice, as plain under the water as it had been on the platform at home. Benal had carried that board eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->. He had kept the chalk on it through all the rain. And what he had done with it at the end was open his hands.

He came up into the rain coughing, blind. He held his arm out and woke the display as hard as it would go.

For a second there was light. An eye was beside him in the water, an arm's length off, as large as his head. Past it, at the edge of the light, Keo had his arms round a piece of the deck and his face turned into the glare.

Then the dark came back with the shape of the light burning in it. When that faded there was only rain, and nothing touched him. An eye as large as his head. He did not want to think what size of head it sat in, and there was no room to: his chest was full of the sea, and the next breath was the only thing in the world.

"Keo!" he shouted. "Teva! Keo!"

The rain tore it out of his mouth. Nothing came back. He shouted until the sea came in and stopped him.

The suit held him up. That was all it did. He lay back in the warm water with nothing in his hands. The wind had him now, and the swell, both going the same way, toward the break he could feel coming up through the sea.

A wave put him under. He came up into the next before he had breathed, and it put him under again, and the sea poured down his throat into his chest, and he went out.
