The reef reached Benal's ears long before it showed itself. Then a white line stood up out of the glassy water under the station, and the line was surf, breaking along the coral as far as he could see in both directions.

Behind it lay Genesis, low ground and a pale crown of rock standing up out of the sea. Up close, the station had walls. They were square-cornered, and after all the days of fern and mud and palm his eyes kept going back to the corners.

He had paddled since the rock with a blade broken off the rail, and his left arm had stopped answering an hour ago. He paddled on the right and let the left lie across his knee. The spray dried on his face as fast as it landed. It crusted at his lips and in his lashes and pulled the skin tight, and the corners of his mouth had split. Salt had found every cut on him and stayed in them. In front of him Keo pulled at his own blade with his breath snagging at the top of each stroke. Beyond Keo, Teva sat with the sweat running off her jaw, in a suit that cooled her only in patches now.

The surf came to him whole. It was one long roar that never stopped to breathe, with the slap of each wave inside it and the hiss of each one dragging back over the coral.

"Ben," Teva said. "Where's the way in?"

"Wait."

He laid the blade across his knees and listened.

"Where there's a channel through, the water's deeper," he said, "so the swell shouldn't stand up the same way there. It would break later, or not at all. I'd hear it as a gap in the noise."

"Do you?" Keo said.

"No. It's the same all along. Either there's no gap on this side, or it's under all the rest of it."

"Then we go along till you hear one."

She pointed, and they turned the boat and worked along outside the break. The current wanted them nearer. Every few strokes Keo dug in on his side and pulled them off again, and the walls slid by behind the surf.

That morning he had heard stone through the haze, and the other two had walked where he said. Here every wave was stone.

"Anything?" Keo said.

"No."

He said it again, later, to Teva. Then he said, "If we go through it here, we'll get there. In more pieces than we left in, possibly."

Teva pressed the back of her wrist hard against her forehead. She held it there a moment before she took it away.

"Keep listening," she said.

Air moved on his neck after a day of none. The water round the boat turned from glass to scales. The black that had sat on the horizon behind them all afternoon was over them, and the sun was gone.

The wind came off the land, at their backs, smelling of the plain, mud and palm. The rail's struts began to sing in it. The note climbed, and the boat slid toward the break.

"It's taking us in," Teva said.

Keo threw his blade down on the deck. "Down. Lie flat, both of you. Get your hands through the rail."

"The paddles—"

"Leave them. Hands through, Ben."

Benal lay down on his face with the fieldpack under his chest and pushed his arms between the struts to the elbow. Keo dropped beside him with Teva's pack wedged between them, and Teva lay on Keo's far side.

Then the rain came, and with it the last of the light. All he could see was his own forearm, where the film still glowed in patches.

"Teva!" Keo's voice was right beside him. "Which way's the station?"

"Wait," she said.

He heard her breath go in and stop.

"I can't." It came out cracked. "It's gone. I can't find it."

After that he could not hear her at all. The rain came down so hard it was the only sound there was, on the deck and on the sea and on his head. The reef was in there somewhere. So were Keo and Teva. Listening was what he was for, and it was gone.

The sea got up under them. The boat climbed and hung and dropped, and each climb was higher than the last. At the top of one the wind got under the whole deck and tried to lift it. At the bottom the next wave came over them solid. A blade knocked at his shins on its way over the edge, then the other, and something heavy dragged across his back and was gone. When the water had run off, there was nothing between him and Keo.

The next one came over him with his mouth open. The sea went in at his collar and down the suit, and into his mouth and nose at once. He came up out of it choking. When he tried to breathe in, his chest stopped halfway, at the place the constrictor had left in it, and would not take any more. He breathed in halves.

He had always thought drowning would be one thing. It came in pieces, with air between them, and never enough of the air.

At the top of a climb a strut snapped in his right hand.

His arm came free. The deck tipped and he went with it, sliding on his chest toward the edge with the fieldpack dragging under him. His left hand caught the next strut and slid off.

A hand closed in the back of his suit, between his shoulders, and stopped him.

It was Keo. Benal could feel the whole of him through that hand. He must have had his good foot jammed against a strut and an arm locked through the rail, and the rest of him stretched across the deck to keep Benal on it. The next wave came over them, and the hand did not open. It did not open for the one after, or the one after that. Every time the boat fell, Keo's breath stopped short against the rib, and went on.

"Got you," Keo said, close against his ear. Benal felt it more than heard it. "Got you."

All his life Keo had been the one who could do this, and he had been the one it was done for. He lay still and let it be done.

In a trough, the boat rose.

He could feel the sea going down around the floats, and the boat went up anyway, slowly, on something under it that was not water. It hung there. Along the floats beneath him something dragged, long and rough, from his feet toward his head, and kept dragging after he thought it must be done. Then the boat settled back into the trough.

Keo's fist tightened in the back of his suit.

He lay with his face on the deck and the rain on his back, and waited for it to come back.

Nothing came.

It came from below, at the edge of the reef, where the break was close enough now that he could feel it in the deck. The boat heaved under him and came apart. Then the deck was not under his chest, and Keo's hand was not in his back, and he was in the water.

He went under, and stayed under.

The suit was trying. He could feel it pulling at his shoulders toward the air, and something pulling the other way, harder, by the strap across his chest. The fieldpack's strap had caught on a broken strut. The piece of frame it hung from was going down, and it took him with it.

He got both hands on the pack. Through it he could feel the edge of the board in its greased hide and the hard curve of the tooth. He held on. His chest had wanted air before he went under. Now it wanted air with the whole of his body. The pack went on down.

He let go. He dragged the strap up over his head, and it tore at his ear going. Then it was gone, and he was rising without it.

*Do something real.*

It was his father's voice, as plain under the water as it had been on the platform at home. Benal had carried that board eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->. He had kept the chalk on it through all the rain. And what he had done with it at the end was open his hands.

He came up into the rain coughing and could see nothing. He held his arm out and woke the display as hard as it would go.

For a second there was light. An eye was beside him in the water, an arm's length off, and it was as large as his head. Past it, at the edge of the light, Keo had his arms round a piece of the deck and his face turned into the glare.

Then the dark came back with the shape of the light burning in it. When that faded, there was only rain, and nothing touched him. He had not understood what he saw.

"Keo!" he shouted. "Teva! Keo!"

The rain took it out of his mouth. Nothing came back. He shouted again until the sea came in and stopped him.

The suit held him up. That was all it did. It did not warn him, or tell him where anyone was. It kept his face near the air while he lay back in the warm water with nothing in his hands. The wind had him now, and the swell. Both were going the same way, toward the break he could feel coming up through the sea.

A wave came over him and put him under. He came up into the next one before he had breathed, and it put him under again, and the sea poured down his throat into his chest, and he went out.

The suit kept him up.
