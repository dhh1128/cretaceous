A murmur had lain under everything he heard since the tangle, far off and even, and Benal had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own breath. Now it was gone.

Not quieter. Gone. He walked inside the place where it had been and could not name what was missing, only that the air had a shape it should not have had. He could hear his feet in the leaves, with Teva's ahead of him and Keo's ahead of hers. He could hear his own blood. Beyond that there was nothing at all, and he had never in his life heard nothing.

He had said *wait*, and they had walked on. He did not say it again. He was last in line, where he always ended up. He kept his mouth shut and walked, and every brown in the litter was the same brown.

He tried to make it into a thought instead, the way he would have at home with the board on his knees. What stops all at once, over a whole forest? There was no wind or rain. Something the small things knew about and he did not. He turned it over and over and got nowhere, and the not getting anywhere frightened him more than the silence did, because thinking was the one thing he had always been able to do when everything else was going wrong. If he stopped them now he would have nothing to tell them but that, and Keo would not turn round.

They walked for more than half an hour with the missing sound beside them.

The litter moved, and was not litter.

Something struck him at the left elbow, hard enough to turn him half round, and before he understood that it had a mouth it was round him. The head stayed where it had struck, jaws locked on the seal at his elbow, pressed in against his ribs. A coil came over his shoulder. Another pinned his arm against his ribs and crossed the front of his chest, still working toward a full wrap. He was off his feet, then on his knees in the leaves, then on his side, with the weight of it on him like a fallen tree that was alive. And then the smell came, all at once. The snake's rank musk filled his mouth, the smell of something that had lain in the dark a long time, and under it he smelled his own fear, sharp and sour in his sweat.

He breathed out to shout, and it took the slack.

That was what it did. He felt it happen. His chest went down with the breath and the coil went down with it, so that when he tried to breathe in there was less room than there had been. He got a little air, not enough to shout with, not enough for anything but the next little breath, and on the next breath out it took the slack again.

He found that he was counting them. He could not stop himself. Each breath was smaller than the one before it, and some part of him that was always doing sums had already drawn the curve and seen where it met the line, and would not stop showing it to him.

The plating on his chest groaned. He heard it inside his own bones. It held, and the weight came through it anyway, as if the plating were not there.

He found the shock grid with his free hand and pressed, and felt the jolt go through the suit, thin and stinging, off his red-lined shoulder capacitor. The snake did not care. It was not the kind of thing that let go of a jolt.

His right hand was free. The razor-club was on his hip. He knew both things clearly, the way he knew sums, and his hand would not go there. It lay in the leaves by his face and did nothing while he watched it, and the coil took the slack.

This was how, then: not razortails or a croc, but lying in the leaves in the afternoon, being squeezed like a waterskin, with his hand a span from a weapon and not moving.

Noli went past his face like a thrown thing, low and screaming, gone.

Then Keo was there. He came back through the ferns at a run with the spear up, and stopped. Keo's eyes went from the point to the coils and to Benal inside them. There was nowhere to put it that was not also Benal. Keo threw the spear down and put his hand flat on the snake and shut his eyes. His face went tight and gray. The snake did not care about that either, and Benal, who knew what it was, saw what it cost and that it bought nothing.

So Keo took hold of the coil with both hands and pulled.

His face was so close that Benal could see the bites on his neck, the sweat, his teeth. His mouth was moving. He was saying Benal's name, or something, over and over, and Benal could not hear it over his own blood. The coil came up a finger's width off Benal's chest and Benal got half a breath. Then the snake shifted under Keo's hands and took it back. Keo's hands slid on the scales. When he took hold again there was blood on them, at the old bites and at new places where the scales had taken his skin.

Teva came in beside him and got her hands under the same coil. They pulled together. It came up. The snake heaved, the whole length of it at once. Teva went over backward into the leaves beside a cycad, as if she weighed nothing. She lay there a moment and got up holding one arm close.

She came back. Her face was white and set and she did not make a sound, and she put her hands where they had been. At the coil they got a hand's width and he breathed, and then they lost it. They got it again and lost it again. He did not know how long. There were no more breaths than there had to be. The light was going in at the edges of things, and the edges were going dark.

Then Teva let go of the coil. He saw her do it and did not understand. She was looking along the snake, the whole length of it, the way he had seen her look at the night sky, one point and then the next, as if it were something to be read.

Then something changed in the grip, and he did not know what.

He could see Teva. She was not at the coil any more. She was down at the far end of the snake, a body's length off, on her knees by a cycad. The snake's tail went round the trunk of it. She was dragging at the tail with her good arm. It came free. The coils around him loosened, very slightly, the way a rope does when the other end is let go. She did not stop. She had hold of the end and she was walking it backward, unwinding it, a turn and then another, and Keo's next pull did something the others had not. The coil came up off his chest and stayed up.

It let go all at once. The coils went slack and poured off him into the leaves, a long brown running weight. The ferns closed behind it. It was gone.

Air came into him, and it hurt more than the crush. He breathed again, and it hurt again. The trees went white and slid away.

He was on his back, and his forearm was talking to him.

Short pulses ran up the weave from his wrist, one after another, very even. He knew them before he knew where he was. He knew the warning; everyone did. The display on his forearm had woken and was counting down, and he knew what came after.

He could see the number and feel the pulses. He could not move. He could not make a sound or lift a finger while the number got smaller.

Keo's hands were already on his forearm. Torn and bloody, they moved too quickly for Benal to follow, the way an Explorer's did. The pulses stopped. The number went dark.

Keo did not say anything, only looked at Benal's face for a while and then sat back on his heels.

Benal lay and looked at the gray sky between the fronds. If Keo had gone after Noli. If Keo had been a little slower getting his hands off the coil. If it had been Teva kneeling over him, or nobody, because the other two were lying in the leaves as well. He had thought, all his life, about the ways his body would fail them. He had never once thought about it failing him and the suit finishing the job, neatly, while his friends watched.

It came back slowly, what had happened, the way the feeling came back into his hands. A band ran across his chest from one side to the other. Every breath caught on it halfway, so that he breathed in halves. The forearm display, when it woke again, told him the left elbow seal was compromised, integrity forty percent, and the shoulder charge had fallen further into red. Keo's hands lay palm up on his knees, and he was looking at them as if they belonged to someone else. Teva was holding her shoulder.

"Teva," Keo said.

"It's nothing," she said.

Nobody said anything after that. They sat close in the leaves where it had happened. After a while Keo put one hand flat on Benal's chest, the torn one, and left it there, feeling it go up and down. Teva sat with her back against Benal's shoulder, and he could feel her breathing through the suit, quick and then slower. Noli came back out of the ferns on her belly and pushed her head under his arm and stayed there, shaking.

He did not need them to say anything. For once he did not need anybody to say anything at all.

Keo picked up the spear before they went. They made three kilometers in the whole afternoon, and stopped early. He walked between them. When his breath caught on the band across his chest, whichever of them was ahead slowed down, and neither of them looked round to see why.
