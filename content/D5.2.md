Something had lain under everything he heard since the tangle, high and faint and scattered, and Benal had long since stopped hearing it. Now it was gone.

Not quieter. Gone. The air had a shape it should not have had. The insect wall went on through the suit. He could hear his feet in the leaves, and the others' ahead of him. He could hear his own blood. Above all of it, where the high faint thing had been, the nothing was loud.

He had said *wait*, and they had walked on. He did not say it again. He was last in line, where he always ended up, and every brown in the litter was the same brown.

He tried to make it into a thought, the kind he worked out at home with the board on his knees. Then it came to him: the birds, small voices up in the crowns, so many and so far off that he had never once counted them. They had stopped. Birds stopped for a hunter in the sky. He looked up through the fronds at nothing but cloud, lower and darker than at the lay-up. The birds knew something he didn't. He turned it over and over and got nowhere, and that frightened him more than the silence. If he stopped them now he would have nothing to tell them but that, and Keo would not turn around.

They walked for more than half an hour with the birds' silence over them.

At a frond by his face a wasp was riding a caterpillar, green and fat, its body curled under and the tip driven into the caterpillar's back. The caterpillar jerked and stayed on the frond. He knew what the wasp was doing. It was laying eggs, in it, not on it. When they hatched they would feed inside it while it went on eating and walking about as if nothing were wrong, for days, possibly, until they were ready to come out, and then—

For a moment he had stopped being afraid and started being interested. He looked away into the ferns.

The wasp lifted off and was gone. The caterpillar began eating again. He walked on after the others.

The litter moved, and was not litter.

Something struck him at the left elbow, hard enough to turn him half around, and before he understood that it had a mouth it was around him. The jaws stayed locked on the seal at his elbow, pressed in against his ribs. A coil came over his shoulder. Another pinned his arm to his side and crossed his chest. He was off his feet, then on his knees in the leaves, then on his side, under a weight like a fallen tree that was alive. The snake's musk filled his mouth, the smell of something that had lain a long time in the dark, and under it he smelled his own fear, sour in his sweat.

He breathed out to shout, and it took the slack.

That was what it did. His chest went down with the breath and the coil went down with it. He got a little air, not enough to shout, and on the next breath out it took the slack again.

He was counting them. He couldn't stop. Each breath was smaller than the last. Some part of him that was always estimating odds had already drawn the curve and seen where it met the line.

The plating on his chest groaned. He heard it inside his own bones. It held, and the weight came through it anyway.

He found the shock grid with his free hand and pressed. The jolt went through the suit, thin and stinging, off his red-lined shoulder capacitor. The snake did not care.

His right hand was free. The razor-club was on his hip. He knew both things, and for a long moment his hand lay in the leaves by his face and would not go there. He watched it do nothing while the coil took the slack.

Then it moved. He dragged the club out and struck at the head locked on his elbow, but he could not get a swing at it. His own arm was in the way, and his own ribs. The teeth along the edge caught scale and opened it. The snake clamped harder, and on his next breath out it took the slack. The club turned in his fingers and fell into the leaves, a span from his hand, out of reach.

This was how, then: not razortails or a croc, but lying in the leaves in the afternoon, being squeezed like a waterskin.

Noli went past his face like a thrown thing, low and screaming, gone.

Then Keo crashed back through the ferns with the spike up, and stopped. His eyes went from the point to the coils to Benal inside them. There was nowhere to put it that was not also Benal. He threw the spike down, put his hand flat on the snake and shut his eyes. His face went tight. The snake did not care about that either, and Benal saw what it cost and that it bought nothing.

So Keo took hold of the coil with both hands and pulled.

His face was so close Benal could see the bites on his neck, the sweat, his teeth. His mouth was moving. He was saying Benal's name, or something, over and over, and Benal could not hear it over his own blood. The coil came up a finger's width and Benal got half a breath. Then the snake shifted under Keo's hands and took it back. His hands slid on the scales. When he took hold again there was blood on his hands, at the old bites and where the scales had taken his skin.

Teva came in beside him and got her hands under the same coil. They pulled together. It came up. The snake heaved, the whole length of it at once. Teva was thrown backward into the leaves beside a cycad. She lay there a moment and got up holding one arm close.

She came back, her face white and set, and put her hands where they had been. They got a hand's width and he breathed, and then they lost it. They got it again and lost it again. He did not know how long. The edges of everything were going dark.

Then Teva let go of the coil. He saw her do it and did not understand. She was looking along the snake, the whole length of it, one point and then the next, as if it were something to be read.

Now she was at the far end of the snake, a body's length off, on her knees by a cycad. The tail went around its trunk. She dragged at it with her good arm, and it came free. The coils around him loosened, very slightly, the way a rope does when the other end is let go. She had hold of the end and was walking it backward, unwinding it, a turn and then another, and Keo's next pull did something the others had not. He felt the coil move on his chest. Then the dark that had been waiting at the edges came in the rest of the way, and he went with it.

He came back on his back in the leaves, and the weight was gone. The last of the snake was going into the ferns, a long brown running length of it, unhurried. Then the ferns closed behind it.

Air came into him, and it hurt more than the crush. He breathed again, and it hurt again. And his forearm was talking to him.

Short pulses ran up the weave from his wrist, and the display on his forearm was counting down. He knew what it was before he knew where he was. Everyone did. The suits carried the same sleeping thing as the wrappings on the Repository's taboo shelf. A suit that lost the pulse and warmth of the body inside decided that body was dead, and when the count ran out it woke what it carried, which ate the body first and then the suit, so that nothing was left out here for anything to find.

He could see the number and feel the pulses. He could not move or make a sound while the number got smaller.

Keo's torn hands were already on his forearm, too quick for Benal to follow. The pulses stopped. The number went dark.

"You're all right," Keo said. "Ben. You're all right." He said it four or five times, to Benal or to himself, and then he sat back on his heels.

Benal lay and looked at the sky between the fronds. If Keo had gone after Noli. If Keo had been a little slower getting his hands off the coil. If it had been Teva kneeling over him, or nobody, because the other two were lying in the leaves as well. He had thought, all his life, about the ways his body would fail them. He had never once thought about it failing him and the suit finishing the job, neatly, while his friends watched.

*You'll be ill*, his father had said, on the day of the Council. He had not been ill. He had been very nearly eaten, which his father had not thought to warn him about.

Feeling came back into his hands. A band ran across his chest, and every breath caught on it halfway, so that he breathed in halves. The display, when it woke again, said the left elbow seal was compromised, integrity forty percent, and the shoulder charge had fallen further into red. Keo sat looking at his hands, palms up on his knees. Teva was holding her shoulder.

Benal looked at her, and understood. She had read the snake until it told her where it was holding on. He would have done that, if he could have moved. He hadn't known anyone else did it.

"Teva," Keo said.

"It's nothing," she said.

"Lift it."

She lifted the arm halfway to her shoulder and stopped there, and put it down again. "It's nearly nothing."

Keo looked at her, and she looked back at him until he gave it up. "Your hands," she said.

He turned them over. The bleeding had mostly stopped. "They'll close by tonight." Nothing stayed open on Keo for long.

"How long did it have left?" Benal said. "The count."

"Long enough."

"How long?"

"Long enough, Ben."

Benal looked at the blank display. "Forty percent of an elbow," he said. "That's possibly still most of an elbow."

Keo nearly laughed.

"And at least it would have been tidy," Benal said. "Nothing left out here for anything to find. You wouldn't even have had to carry me."

Keo's face changed. "Shut up, Ben."

He shut up. They sat close in the leaves. After a while Keo put a torn hand flat on Benal's chest and left it there, feeling it rise and fall. Teva sat with her back against Benal's shoulder, and he could feel her breathing through the suit, quick and then slower. Noli crept back out of the ferns, pushed her head under his arm and stayed there, shaking.

For once he did not need anything more from anybody.

Keo picked up the spike before they went, and Benal's club out of the leaves. They made three kilometers in the whole afternoon, and stopped early. Benal walked between them. When his breath caught on the band, whichever of them was ahead slowed down, and neither of them looked around to see why.
