The smell reached her at the cleft, before her eyes had opened to the dark.

It was not the mineral damp of the kiva. That smell had meant safe since she was young enough to be carried down here. It was not the yeast of cultures doing well, either. This was sweet, and under the sweetness was protein coming apart in a way protein was not meant to come apart. The Keepers had a word for it. She had never once needed to say the word aloud.

She said it in her head anyway.

Inside, she counted. She had counted this chamber since she was small, standing in this same doorway, waiting for her grandmother to finish something and notice her. It had always taken a while.

Dozens of vessels sat back in the clefts of the rock. Each one was lopsided. Each was shaped to look like a clump of clay somebody had dropped and not troubled to pick up, because nothing in here was permitted to look made. She had asked about that when she was seven. She had been told it was a precaution, in the voice adults used when an answer was finished.

Every vessel held one line, and every line made one thing. A line doing well burned with its own color, so a Keeper crossing the floor could read the whole wall in a breath. That was what the color was for. Nobody in here had ever called it beautiful.

She read the wall in a breath. That was the problem.

The rust-red end should have been the brightest thing in the Enclave. Four still held anything at all, and two of those had gone dull enough that she leaned to be sure. Down the left, where the digestive lines ran, there was blue, and blue, and a long stretch of dark. She did not say the number aloud. She had given that up nine days ago, when Rano asked her, kindly, not to.

The chamber had been getting darker for a fortnight and nobody had said so. It came on slowly enough that a person could put it down to tired eyes. Rano had shifted the tray twice, each time a little nearer the rust-red wall, and had not mentioned doing it. Teva watched him do it the second time and said nothing either. There was no lamp to carry in here. The lines were the light. When the last of them went out the Keepers would work in the dark, or they would not work.

Elana had the child across her knees near the cleft, with one small arm out of the cloth and turned up, the way you hold a thing out for somebody to look at. Nobody had looked at it in a while. There was a sting on the wrist no bigger than a thumbnail. Out of the sting a line ran up the inside of the forearm, past the elbow, into the soft part above it, and the line had a color.

Teva could read an arm the same way she read a wall. She wished, standing there, that she had never been taught either one.

The child was two and a half. She had a name, which meant the waiting was over and the arguing was over and she was a person. It also meant that whatever happened in here tonight, somebody would count it.

"The rust-red," Rano said. "Position one-oh-seven. The final fold." He waited. "The rust-red, Omya. Position one-oh-seven."

Her grandmother sat at the tray with both hands over it. The silver hair was pinned up in the Master's loops, which somebody else had done for her that morning. The hands below it worked without hurry. They worked the way they always had when the work was going well.

She chose a key. It was yellow.

Rano did not sigh. Teva watched him not sigh, which was worse, and watched him set the yellow key back where she could find it again.

"The final fold protocol," he said. "You know this."

Omya put her fingers on the tray's edge and gave him the whole of the fourth sequence. She did not stumble anywhere in it. It came out flat and quick and in order, in the cadence of something learned before she had all her teeth. It ran on long enough that Elana stopped rocking to listen.

Rano's eyes stayed on the tray. He let it go past him the way you let rain go past. The fourth sequence was not what was killing them, and had not been for weeks.

"Two doses," Elana said. "It's two, for her weight. Two."

She had been in here an hour and she had learned the words. That was the part Teva could not get away from. A woman who had come in shouting had spent the hour listening hard enough to pick up the dose, because somewhere in the hour she had worked out that being useful might buy something.

Rano's mouth opened. Then he turned back to the tray, lifted the next key, and named the color and the position again. Teva understood that this was his answer. She understood that he knew it was.

Beyond the cleft the elders stood in the last of the light coming down through the roof hole. One shifted his weight forward as though to come through, and did not.

Cowards, she thought.

Limestone pulled the heat out of her heels. Rano had sweat along his jaw and his sleeves shoved up past the elbow, and nobody past the cleft had shifted in an hour. So it was hers. There was a way of standing that kept it from getting worse, and she had known it a long time, and she used it now without thinking about it.

Omya lifted the blue key and held it out across the tray.

"For the pan?" she said.

Teva's teeth came together. Blue was the digestive line. Blue was what they gave a yazhi so a child could eat what grew here. It had nothing in it that would touch the thing in that arm. It was in her grandmother's hand where the immunity work should have been, and her grandmother was asking whether it was for bread.

Rano took the blue key out of her fingers with no change at all in his face.

Omya's head came up. Her eyes travelled past him, and past the vessels, and found Teva in the doorway. Something surfaced behind them and did not arrive. It had been there when Teva was nine and learning the sky.

Then it was gone, and she turned back to the tray, and reached again for the yellow key.

The breathing at the cleft changed.

Everyone in the chamber heard it. Nobody turned around. Rano put another key in front of Omya and named the color and the position, and his voice had come up half a step, and he said it twice.

Teva knew the sound. It had a wet in it, low down, where the lungs had quit arguing. She had heard it two years ago, in a room on the far side of the kiva. It had come out of a woman who walked down at dusk to see to a trap and came back with a line rising up her forearm.

Her mother had carried the magnetic sense that Teva carried. She crossed a white-out fog once, from the far grove to the perimeter, on the pull in her own bones. Men who had been out there with her still told it to people who had heard it twice. The Keepers had been three then, and all three of them well. The protocols had worked. They made the vector, and they did the Seeding on a clear morning, with half the village standing outside to see it done.

It had not taken.

Teva sat against the wall in that room and listened and believed, right to the end of it, that somebody was going to come through the door and stop this. She had been fourteen. She could still remember deciding that the waiting was a kind of work, and that if she did it well enough it would be rewarded.

Nobody came through the door.

The rattle hitched, and thinned, and quit.

Rano's hands left the tray. Two keys travelled with them and struck the floor, and he did not pick them up. He put his hands on his knees and left them there. Her grandmother's hands stayed where they were, and after a moment she reached out and shifted a key half a finger's width, to true it up.

Teva went around the chamber.

Rano first, who had stopped. Rano knew eighty percent of the process. He had said that number aloud in here more than once, in the voice of a man reporting weather, as though it were something that had happened to him rather than something he was. Eighty percent was nothing. Eighty percent was a span missing out of the middle of a bridge, and you could stand at either end of it all your life and be no use to anyone.

Then the elders. She tried to make it fear, because fear was the generous reading and she wanted to be able to hold it. It would not hold. Fear moves. Fear does the wrong thing, and does it fast, and is sorry afterward. What stood out there in the light had decided, and had gone on deciding all evening, a minute at a time, and every one of them would be able to say tomorrow that nobody had decided anything.

Then she looked at her grandmother, truing up a key.

It was not grief. She had grief. She knew the shape of it, and it was the sound coming out of the doorway behind her, and it was not this. This was flat. This was cold, and it would still be here tomorrow.

Elana stopped asking.

She had asked for an hour, in the same words, in a voice worn down to almost nothing, and she had not once let it go. The chamber without it was worse.

Teva looked at the wall of vessels, because there was nowhere else in here to look.

Low on the left, one of them had been showing a color when she came in.

She crossed the floor. She did not go to her grandmother. She crouched and picked the two keys up off the limestone. They were cool, and they had held their shape, because the air in here was the one thing left that was still doing its job.

She put them back on the tray. The green one where the green one went. The yellow after it.

She knew where they went.

Nobody looked up.
