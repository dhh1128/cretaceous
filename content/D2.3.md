The smell of pan baking at the cook pits followed Keo up the ladder, and at the top one last gust of it caught him, dark crust and smoke. He was hungry before he knew it. So many mornings of his life had smelled like that.

He hauled himself through the opening in the floor. Light came through the vine walls in hard scraps, and the nest gave a little under his weight and settled, the ropes taking it. Below, the village was waking, louder than he wanted it to be.

"Third morning running," said his mother.

She knelt at the larder basket with her back to him. Keo stood with the ache from the morning's drill still pressing behind his eyes and waited. There was more coming.

"Three mornings running I've heard you come up that ladder like you were carrying it."

"She keeps track," Keo told his father.

Joram sat cross-legged by the gear pile with a tooth-knife across his knees and a strip of binding in his teeth. He took the binding out, considered whether the question deserved an answer, and said, "Always."

"He means," Keo said, "that she has never once in her life stopped keeping track, and that he loved her for it the day they met and has regretted it every day since."

"He means all that," Riel agreed, "with one word. You can see why I married him. So efficient."

Keo snorted.

"He said eleven words to me the day we met," she added. "Seven were about the weather."

"It was raining," said Joram.

"Did Teva go home in the end?" his mother said, to the basket.

"She was still out at the fire with Elana when we left." He had looked back from the trees to see.

"Then somebody should look in on Omya."

"Flowers," said Joram.

"Your father means you could take her some more," said Riel. "She liked the last ones. Eleven, you took up that ladder."

"Twelve," said Joram.

"One was a leaf."

"She liked the leaf," Keo said.

Joram put the binding back in his teeth. Keo dropped onto the mat across from him, and for the length of one breath it was any morning at all: his father's hands working the cord around the knife's haft in the tight wraps Keo had watched since he was small enough to sit in his lap.

Joram reached down without looking and scratched at his forearm, where a seam of pale scar ran from the wrist almost to the elbow. Keo smiled.

"What happened to your arm?" he asked. He knew. It was the oldest story in the family, and asking for it was a way of asking for the morning to go on.

Joram looked at the scar. "Fell."

"Off a ridge in the limestone country, onto a rock the size of this nest," said Riel, "and the bone came through the skin. It closed in four days. Four. I watched it close."

"And then?" Keo said.

"And then for two weeks <!-- @WF-joram.hollow: 2w --> he was the most useless man in the Enclave. He fell asleep standing up. He fell asleep eating. He was cold in the middle of the day, and he wanted to know why everyone was talking so fast." She turned at last with two pieces of yesterday's pan out of the basket, cold and dense, and she was smiling. "Healing like that takes it out of you from somewhere, and it took it out of him from everywhere. The year Yara was named. I couldn't get a word of sense out of him."

Joram shrugged, which was his whole argument.

Keo laughed. It came up out of him easily, the small click of a thing going right, and he had his mouth open for the next line when he remembered why he had climbed the ladder angry.

"Alira's dead," he said.

The rope creaked. His mother set the two pieces of pan down on the mat, side by side, as if they might break. She pressed the back of her wrist against one eye and held it there.

"I know," she said. "Elana's girl. I know."

Joram had stopped wrapping. He shook his head twice, and his voice had gone rough. "Two and a half." He looked at the knife in his hands, then went back to the binding.

That was all. Keo waited for one of them to say something about the Vitarium, or about what had been under the mats and then had not. He waited for his father.

His mother pushed his pan across the mat to him. "That's three times you've sighed since you sat down."

"Four," said Joram, without looking up.

It was the old game, the counting one, held out to him like a hand. All he had to do was say *five*.

"Don't," Keo said. He sat with his elbows on his knees and looked at the floor between his boots, and the pan stayed where she had pushed it.

"Are you going to say anything tonight?" he asked. "At the Council."

"Your father will say one word," said Riel. "Two, if it goes well."

"I'm asking him."

Joram finished a wrap and pulled it tight. "We'll be there."

Keo heard it land and lie there. His parents had been past the tangle more times than anyone he knew, and they were going to stand in the crowd and be there.

"That's not an answer," he said. "You know what's out there. Both of you. You could tell them."

"We do know." His mother sat back on her heels. Her voice was still the dry one she kept for his mornings, with no play left in it. "Five to ten a year <!-- @WF-losses.annual: 5-10 -->, Keo. That's how many we lose, with every rule we have. You want us to stand up in front of nine hundred people <!-- @WF-population: 900-1000 --> and tell them the outside is easier?"

Keo couldn't find a way to say what he meant that didn't sound like a child stamping, so he went at it sideways.

"Rano keeps telling everyone he's close," he said. "He's been close for three weeks."

"He's not lying to you," Riel said. "He thinks he is."

She said it without weight. Keo heard a mother making excuses for a grown man, and set his jaw.

Joram laid the knife across his knees. He looked at his son for a while. Then he said, "I could practice the second word on the way down. If it would help."

Riel laughed, a little. Keo did not. He watched his father pick the knife up again and felt handled, the way you handle a yazhi who has fallen and is deciding whether to cry.

"I can do it," he said. "Whatever it is. I've been out past the tangle on supervised trips. I know the ground. I can pin Noli inside ten meters of the Barrier, and nobody else in this Enclave can do that with anything. I can read the herds." He heard himself laying it all out like a child with the stones he had collected, and couldn't stop. "You know I can."

Nobody told him he couldn't. That was almost worse. His father looked at the knife in his hands, and his mother looked at him.

Riel got up. On her way past she picked up his pan, put it into his hand and closed his fingers on it. "You can be angry and eat," she said. "People do it all the time." Then she took the water skins from the hook by the opening and began to fill the gourds. She did not look at him. That was how she ended an argument she did not want to lose: she found something that needed doing, and left the argument standing in the middle of the floor.

Keo watched her pour. Then he turned to his father.

"We'll be there," he said. "That's three words. You're getting long-winded."

Nobody laughed, and nobody told him to stop.

She filled four gourds and set them in a row by the larder, and his father stood. It was the morning of the weekly balm, when the custodians went down to the Repository, and the balm did not wait for anybody's mood. Joram tested the new binding with his thumb, sheathed the knife at his belt and slung his fieldpack. Riel took her own pack from the pile.

"Give me a suit," Keo said.

His mother paused with one arm through a strap.

"You're not a custodian," she said, gently, in the same words as every time before. "You're not an adult yet, Keo. The suits go to people who have been trained and commissioned, there are twelve left <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 --> in the whole world, and nobody goes outside without a reason, or alone, or untrained. You know all that."

"I'd be fine in one."

Riel looked at his father and went down the ladder. Joram followed her to the opening and stopped with one foot on the top rung, half turned back toward the room, and for a moment his face did something Keo could not read.

"You'd die in it," his father said.

Then he went down.
