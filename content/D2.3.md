The smell of pan baking at the cook pits had followed Keo up the whole ladder, and as he reached the top one last gust of it came up from under the platform, dark crust and smoke, and he was hungry before he knew he was. Every morning of his life had smelled like that.

He hauled himself through the opening in the floor. Light came through the gaps in the vine walls in hard scraps, and the nest gave a little under his weight and settled, the ropes taking it the way they always did. Below, the village was waking, louder than he wanted it to be.

"Third morning running," said his mother.

She was kneeling at the larder basket with her back to him, and she did not turn around. Keo stood there with the ache from the morning's drill still pressing behind his eyes and waited, because he knew there was more coming.

"Three mornings running I've heard you come up that ladder like you were carrying it."

"She keeps track," Keo told his father.

Joram sat cross-legged by the gear pile with a tooth-knife across his knees and a strip of binding in his teeth. He took the binding out, considered the question of whether it deserved an answer, and said, "Always."

"He means," Keo said, "that she has always kept track, that she has never once in her life stopped keeping track, and that he loved her for it the day they met and has regretted it every day since."

"He means all that," Riel agreed, "with one word. You can see why I married him. So efficient."

Joram put the binding back in his teeth. Keo dropped onto the mat across from him, and for the length of one breath it was any morning at all: his father's hands working the cord around the knife's haft in the tight wraps that Keo had watched since he was small enough to sit in his lap.

Joram reached down without looking and scratched at his forearm, where a seam of pale scar ran from the wrist almost to the elbow. Keo smiled.

"What happened to your arm?" he asked. He knew. It was the oldest story in the family, and asking for it was a way of asking for the morning to go on.

Joram looked at the scar as if he had never seen it before. "Fell."

"Off a ridge in the limestone country, onto a rock the size of this nest," said Riel, "and the bone came through the skin. It closed in four days. Four. I watched it close."

"And then?" Keo said, because this was his part.

"And then for two weeks <!-- @WF-joram.hollow: 2w --> he was the most useless man in the Enclave. He fell asleep standing up. He fell asleep eating. He was cold in the middle of the day, and he wanted to know why everyone was talking so fast." She turned at last with two pieces of yesterday's pan out of the larder basket, cold and dense, and she was smiling. "Healing like that takes it out of you from somewhere, and it took it out of him from everywhere. The year Yara was named. I couldn't get a word of sense out of him."

Joram shrugged, which was his whole argument.

Keo laughed. It came up out of him easily, the small click of a thing going right, and he had his mouth open for the next line — he knew the next line, he had known it for years — when he remembered why he had climbed the ladder angry.

"Alira's dead," he said.

The rope creaked. His mother set the two pieces of pan down on the mat, side by side, carefully, as if they might break. She pressed the back of her wrist against one eye and held it there.

"I know," she said. "Elana's girl. I know."

Joram had stopped wrapping. He shook his head, slowly, twice, the way he did when he was refusing to say something, and when he spoke his voice had gone rough. "Two and a half." He looked at the knife in his hands as if it belonged to someone else, and then, because there was nothing else to do with them, he went back to the binding.

That was all. Nobody said anything about the Vitarium, or about what had been under the mats and then had not. Keo waited for one of them to say something more. He waited for his father.

His mother pushed his pan across the mat to him. "That's three times you've sighed since you sat down."

"Four," said Joram, without looking up.

It was the old game, the counting one, held out to him like a hand. Keo knew exactly what it was for: a way back into the morning, if he wanted one. All he had to do was say *five*. He did not say it. He sat with his elbows on his knees and looked at the floor between his boots, and the pan stayed where she had pushed it.

"Are you going to say anything tonight?" Keo asked. "At the Council."

"Your father will say one word," said Riel. "Two, if it goes well."

"I'm asking him."

Joram finished a wrap and pulled it tight. "We'll be there."

Keo heard it land and lie there. Everyone would be there; the whole Enclave would be packed into the kiva tonight, standing on each other's feet in the damp, and most of them would say nothing, and the Council would do what it had done the last time, which was decide to wait. His parents were Explorers. They had been past the tangle more times than anyone he knew. They knew what was out there better than anyone sitting on the dais, and they were going to stand in the crowd and be there.

"That's not an answer," he said. "You know what's out there. Both of you. You could tell them."

"We do know." His mother sat back on her heels. Her voice was still dry, still the voice she used for keeping track of his mornings, but there was nothing playful in it now. "Five to ten a year <!-- @WF-losses.annual: 5-10 -->, Keo. That's how many we lose, with every rule we have. You want us to stand up in front of nine hundred people <!-- @WF-population: 900-1000 --> and tell them the outside is easier?"

Keo did not want them to tell anyone anything easy. He wanted them to tell the Council what they knew, and he could not find the words for it that did not sound like a child stamping, so he went at it sideways.

"Rano keeps telling everyone he's close," he said. "He's been close for three weeks. Every day he comes out of the Vitarium and says he's nearly got it."

"He's not lying to you," Riel said. "He thinks he is."

She said it without weight. Keo heard a mother making excuses for a grown man who should have known better, and he set his jaw, and did not answer.

Joram set the knife down across his knees. He looked at his son for a while. Then he said, mildly, "I could practice the second word on the way down. If it would help."

Riel laughed, a little. Keo did not. He watched his father pick the knife up again, the joke spent and gone, and he felt handled, the way you handle a yazhi who has fallen and is deciding whether to cry.

"I can do it," he said. "Whatever it is. I've been out past the tangle on supervised trips. I know the ground out there. I can pin Noli inside ten meters of the Barrier, and nobody else in this Enclave can do that with anything. I can read the herds." He heard himself, listing things like a child laying out the stones he had collected, and he could not stop. "You know I can."

Nobody told him he couldn't. That was almost worse. His father looked at the knife in his hands and his mother looked at him, and neither of them said anything at all.

Riel got up. She took the water skins from the hook by the opening, unstoppered the first, and began to fill the gourds from it. She did not look at him while she did it. That was how she ended an argument she did not want to lose: she found something that needed doing and did it, and the argument was left standing in the middle of the floor with nowhere to go.

Keo watched her pour. Then he turned to his father.

"We'll be there," he said. "That's three words. You're getting long-winded."

Nobody laughed, and nobody told him to stop.

She filled four gourds and set them in a row by the larder, stoppered the skin and hung it back on its hook, and his father stood. That meant it was time; the custodians went down to the Repository on the morning of the weekly balm, and the balm did not wait for anybody's mood. Joram tested the new binding with his thumb, slid the knife into its sheath at his belt, and slung his fieldpack. Riel took her own pack from the pile.

"Give me a suit," Keo said.

His mother paused with one arm through a strap.

"You're not a custodian," she said. She said it gently, and she said it the way she had said it every time before. "You're not an adult yet, Keo. The suits go to people who have been trained and commissioned, there are twelve left <!-- @WF-suits.remaining: 12 --> in the whole world, and nobody goes outside without a reason, or alone, or untrained. You know all that."

"I'd be fine in one."

Riel looked at his father, and then she went down the ladder. Joram followed her to the opening and stopped there, one foot on the top rung, half turned back toward the room, and for a moment his face did something Keo could not read.

"You'd die in it," his father said.

Then he went down.
