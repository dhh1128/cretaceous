The floor took the heat out of her through the suit. Under her palm the stone was gritty with salt dried into it, and she could feel each grain. Keo was shaking against her arm. Benal sat on his other side with his knees up and his head back against the end wall.

It got said before anyone slept.

"Is this it?" Keo said. "Eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km -->. Noli. The boat. And this is…"

He left it there, and the roof gave back the start of it.

"Two knives," Benal said. "Two suits, and both of them dying. No food. No fire, because the kindler went with the fieldpack. No boat. The rain on the bank will be gone by tomorrow, I think. And nothing in here that anybody ever wrote on." He breathed in, and it caught on his chest. "I looked. I looked at every bit of wall I could reach."

Teva had watched him do it, going along the gallery with his face close to the wall and his fingers on the rusted stubs.

"I brought you here," she said.

Nobody answered.

Later the wind dropped, and she knew it by the reek. All evening it had come and gone on the gusts from the broken end. Now it stopped coming and going and hung over them, sharp at the back of her throat. She breathed through her mouth and it was on her tongue instead.

Keo's breathing had gone long. Benal's had the catch in it that meant he was nearly under.

Her skin prickled. Then there was light, from overhead, white and even, and no flame made it. In it the vault was whole. Below the gallery's edge the floor was dry, and where the lumps of rust had stood in the water, the things they had been stood instead, pale and smooth and taller than a person, in rows. People were down among them, a great many, in suits like hers.

Some of them had loads on their backs. Those stood toward the doorway, where the doors were whole too, and the rest stood facing them. Everyone was talking. Hands went up. A woman with a load took a step back and came forward again, and a man among the others had both hands out, palms up. Teva could hear none of it; their mouths moved and there was no sound, not even the water.

The air was dry and smelled of lightning. There were no birds in it, no salt, no sea. That was how she knew it was not now.

She took them for the founders, from the suits and from the place. The loads told her which were going. Those were hers, then, the ones who had walked. Nobody had ever told her there was an argument. The story she knew began on the turtle beach, with the boats and the rock.

She had always thought the ones with loads had walked out of Genesis with nothing in them. Their faces were not like that. They were frightened, all of them, so frightened that some could hardly hold their heads up, and some were grieving already, for the ones facing them, who were not coming. And some had set their jaws and would not be moved, though the doors stood open in front of them. None of them was wicked. One face among the ones with loads held her, and she held on to it. She would know it again.

She did not know whether any of it was there.

Then the light went. She was on the gallery in the reek, with Keo's shoulder against hers and her heart going hard enough to feel in her neck. She sat with it until it slowed, and did not wake them.

Benal jerked upright beside Keo. "No—"

"Ben?" she said.

He was breathing fast. "My equation. I was looking at it, and it was mine, all of it, and somebody else had written it out. It wasn't my hand. It smelled like after lightning, and my skin was crawling." He stopped. "And there was a term in it I've never written. Not once."

"You were dreaming," Keo said, thick, from between them.

"It felt like being lied to." Benal let his head go back against the wall. "It was a nightmare. That's all it was. I don't want to talk about it."

She could feel him shaking now too, through Keo.

Keo lay still a long time.

"I did it for nothing," he said.

He said it to the roof, and she waited.

"My parents. I took the suits and went in the night, and they'll have woken up to that. And it was for nothing." His teeth knocked once. "And Yara."

They knew how Yara had died. Everybody did: after heavy rain, in a flooded grove away from the river, gathering snails, to a croc that had come through the grating small and grown up inside.

"I was with her," Keo said. "In the grove. Right beside her, and I pushed at it with everything I had, and it never knew I was there."

Teva had not known that.

"That's why I left the third suit. There was one on the pole my size." He stopped, and started again. "I wanted to come through it with nothing on. What she didn't."

Out at the seaward end the house drew its breath, slow, and let it go.

"They've still got their own suits," Benal said. "Both of them. And the one you left, so that's three on the pole. And each other. And their work. Set against that, it's—proportionally, it isn't—"

"Ben," Teva said. "Stop."

He stopped, and Keo did not move.

For a while there was only the birds shifting, and the slap of the water below, slower than it had been.

"I wasn't going to say this," Benal said.

He went on anyway, in the voice he used for things he had worked out.

"My father pities me. Both of them do. He puts his hand on my shoulder and it covers the whole of it, and he waits for me to finish talking, and then he's kind." His breath caught and he waited for it. "And the work's no use to anybody. I came all this way for the answer, and I haven't even got what I had. It's at the bottom of the sea. I let go of it."

Teva thought of the board in its greased hide.

"Mine don't pity me," Keo said. "They don't trust me. They look at me and see someone to keep out of the water."

"My father told me to do something real."

"Mine told me to stay inside the perimeter. Same thing. Other way round."

Benal laughed once. The vault took it and sent it back much bigger than he had made it, and at the far end the birds grumbled and shoved at each other until it had gone.

"Yes," Benal said, more quietly.

There was a frayed edge where she had cut Keo's legging off at the knee, back in the gulch. Her fingers had found it. They had three threads out of it and were plaiting them, the way they did Omya's loops every morning, and she let them.

"You're lucky," she said. "Both of you. You've got parents to fight with." Her voice was level, and she could hear it being level and could not make it anything else. "I've got Omya, and I left her. She's disappearing, and I walked out on her in the night."

She had not said that, not even inside her own head, not in words. She had walked eighty kilometers <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> beside it.

"My mother died of a bite," she said. "It went red up her arm, the same as Alira's. The Keepers brought a Seeding and it didn't take. I sat beside her and listened to her breathing until it stopped."

Under the birds and the water she could hear it, the breathing, slow and then quick and then slow. It was louder now, because she had said it.

"I'm cold," she said. "I'm frightened."

It did not help. She had thought, if she ever said it, that something would come off her, and nothing did. Her throat hurt, and she was emptier than before.

Out at the broken end a bird complained, and was answered, and was still.

Then Keo's hand found hers. His fingers came down over the back of it and closed. She had thought of him as the warm one since his fever. His hand was as cold as her own.

She held on.

It was a long time later that she said, "Ben."

"Mm."

She leaned across Keo to say it, so that her shoulder was against his chest and her face toward Benal's shape in the faint patches of his suit. "Do you remember the boy who used to shout behind you? To make you cover your ears?"

"Yes," Benal said, after a while. "He stopped. I always thought he got tired of it."

"He didn't get tired of it. Keo made him stop."

"Keo?"

"Told him what would happen if he did it again. I was there."

She felt Benal turn his head. "Why didn't you ever say?"

"There was nothing to say," Keo said.

Then Benal's shoulder came over against Keo's. He leaned into it and left it there.

Teva sat back against the wall. Keo's hand was still in hers.

"Teva," Keo said. His voice was light, almost the old voice. "Do you remember, when we were small, you used to chase me round the nest trees? You said you'd kiss me if you caught me."

She tried, and could see the nest trees and the ground under them, with small children running everywhere on it. She could not find herself among them.

"Did I?"

"You did."

"I don't remember."

He laughed. "I was faster then."

It was a joke, and she nearly smiled. After a while his hand let go of hers, and he put it in his lap.

Toward morning the air turned cooler, and damp on her face. Keo was sitting up.

"What's left to try?" he said.

He said it to both of them, and she heard the difference: it was a real question.

"Two hundred people <!-- @WF-founders.count: 200 --> lived here for three years <!-- @WF-genesis.occupancy: 3y -->," Benal said. "Two hundred people <!-- @WF-founders.count: 200 --> don't live in one building. And this one was only the water works. So there's more of it. Possibly a great deal more."

Teva saw it before she knew she was remembering it: the top of the rock on the turtle beach, the sea glassy below it, and across the water the island with its crown and something on the crown that was too straight. "From the rock," she said. "Yesterday. There were square edges on the crown, pale ones. On the top."

"Then why couldn't we see them today?" Keo said.

"Because we were down on the bank," Benal said. "From down there, the brow hides the top."

"The archive's up there," Teva said. "The story puts it on Genesis, and nobody would keep metal tablets down by the sea."

"Then we go and look," Keo said. "In the morning, when the water's off the low ground."

Benal nodded against the wall; she felt it through Keo.

They sat on against the end wall, the three of them, with Benal's shoulder still against Keo's. At the broken end the wind had dropped, and the birds had settled. Under them the house drew its slow breath and let it go. They had no food and no fire, and no way into anything that still stood. In the morning they would go and look.
