There was powder in the creases of Teva's fingers, and rubbing them together did not get it out. It had come off Cecilia's messages, which she had found in the night in a room along the top of the crown, among Cecilia's own things. Every sheet she turned had come apart a little more in her hands.

The sun was just up out of the sea. The water in the hollows stood a hand lower than it had yesterday morning.

Benal was on his knees at the biggest one.

"Three each," he said. "Mouthfuls, not swallows. Three now and three at dusk, and tomorrow morning what's left. We'll be thirsty by the time the sun's high."

He drank his three and sat back on his heels. Keo went down after him. Teva watched his throat. It moved twice, and then he lifted his face out of the water and wiped his mouth on his wrist.

"Go on, Teva," he said. "Drink yours."

"You had two."

"I'm fine."

She waited. He went down again and drank the third, and then she drank hers. She counted three, and stopped.

"I found Cecilia's messages in the night," she said.

"What did they say?" Keo said.

"Pieces of things." She rubbed the powder between her fingers. "They came apart when I turned them."

Benal looked up at her from the water, and then down into it again. She left it there.

From the hollows the land lay across the water to the south, lit sideways by the low sun. In every gap between the coast's trees, as far as she could see either way, there were patches of shine that held the sky. It looked like the groves at home after rain, going on to the edge of the world.

"Was it like that yesterday?" she said.

Benal shaded his eyes. "Less, I think. And less the day before."

She had seen it both mornings from here and taken it for storm water that would drain.

"Let's go look at what's left of the boat," she said. "It's the only thing on the island we know floats."

Keo was already getting up. "I'll come."

They went down the slope they had come up the first morning. High on the shell, where the surge had thrown it, lay one float, split end to end. Deck boards lay farther along in the weed, and a length of the frame, bent nearly double. They kept to the high shell, well back from the water's edge.

Teva put her hands on the float. It was warm already, and thinner than she had thought for a thing that had held three of them up. Inside, it was packed with sodden sand.

Benal crouched at the split end and looked into it. "If we had another float," he said, "and the frame were straight, and something to steer it with, then possibly—"

He stopped and looked past the float at the reef.

There would be other boats on the ledge under the overhang at the rock, where the founders had stowed them, on the far side of the water.

"What about your raft, Ben?" Keo said. "Logs, tied together. You wanted one at the river."

Benal looked along the shore at the driftwood in the weed. "There's enough here to float one of us. Maybe. Not three." He rubbed his weak arm. "And then there's the reef. And what's out past it."

"No," Teva said.

Out on the reef the swell lifted a whole tree, roots and all, dropped it on the coral and lifted it again. Each time the trunk came down, its knock reached her a moment late. Mud still caked its roots. Birds stood along it and went up shouting whenever it moved. Beside it a mat of fern turned, and beyond that another.

The water between the reef and the land was brown, the color of the river at home in flood. Off past the end of the island the brown stopped against blue, along a line as plain as one scratched in mud with a stick. That hadn't come up out of the sea. It had come down off the land.

"After heavy rain the groves are the river's," she said.

She had said it to every yazhi she had ever drilled, until they could say it back to her.

"That's what the shine is," she said. "It's the river. It's out of its bed, all across the plain. Like the groves at home, only kilometers of it."

"The narrows," Keo said.

She could not see them from here, and did not need to.

"There were marks on those stones," she said. "High up, way over the river we crossed. A river that's out across a whole plain is over those. There isn't a narrows any more."

"And whatever was in the river," Benal said, "is in all of it."

"All of it."

"How long does it stay like that?" Keo said.

"At home the low groves stand a few days. Even after the flood that took the two Masters, it was days. That river has somewhere to go." She looked across at the shine. "This one has a whole plain and nowhere to go. Longer. Weeks, maybe."

It was a guess.

"The hollows hold a day," Benal said.

The sun had found the back of her neck. There was no shade on the shore.

"All right," she said. "The boat."

"Gone," Keo said.

"The sea."

"No," Benal said.

"Walking." She looked at him. "Ben?"

"Coming out, it took eleven days," <!-- @WF-mudwalk.days: 11 --> he said. "Now the ground's under water for as long as you say. And we've got water for a day." He looked at his hands. <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> "It's eighty kilometers of walking on a route that isn't there any more. Even if we could get across to where it starts."

She had every number now.

The protocols were on the plates up on the crown, every line of them. At home the cultures went grayer in their vessels each day the three of them were gone. The Seedings had stopped. Omya was going.

"We have it," she said.

Then she sat down on the shell. It was sharp under her, and she did not get up.

Benal sat down beside her, his shoulder against hers.

Keo stayed standing, looking past them across the water at the land. Through the count he had said one word, and that was not like him.

"Kilometers of shallow water," Teva said, to nobody.

Keo's face changed. She saw it change, and could not read it.

Then he turned to her.

"Teva. There's one way left." He waited until she looked up. "It's been charging two days."
