There was gray grit in the creases of Teva's fingers, and rubbing them together did not get it out. It had come off the soft sheets in the night. She had found Cecilia's messages among Cecilia's own things, in a room along the top of the crown, and read what she could of them by the panel light. Every sheet she turned came apart a little more in her hands. In the end she had left them where they lay.

The sun was just up out of the sea. The water in the hollows stood a hand lower than it had yesterday morning.

Benal was on his knees at the biggest one.

"Three each," he said. "Mouthfuls, not swallows. Three now and three at dusk, and tomorrow what's left, which I think gets us to about the middle of the day. Roughly."

He drank his three and sat back on his heels. Keo went down after him. Teva watched his throat. It moved twice, and then he lifted his face out of the water and wiped his mouth on his wrist.

"Go on, Teva," he said. "Drink yours."

She let it go and drank. She counted three, and stopped with her lips still wet.

"I found Cecilia's messages in the night," she said. "In a room along the top, with her things."

Benal looked up at her from the water, and then down into it again. Keo nodded. She left it there.

From the hollows the land lay across the water to the south, the whole length of it in plain view, and the low sun lit it from the side. Between the dark of the coast's trees there were flat pale patches that held the sky. They were in every gap she could see, as far as she could see either way. Behind the first trees she could see nothing. It looked like the groves at home after rain, going on to the edge of the world.

"Was it like that yesterday?" she said.

Benal shaded his eyes. "Less, I think. And less the day before."

She had seen it both mornings, kneeling here, and taken it for what the storm had left lying, which would drain.

"We'll go down and look at what's left of the boat," she said.

"There won't be much," Benal said.

"It's the only thing on the island we know floats."

Keo was already getting his good foot under him. "I'll come."

They went down the slope they had come up the first morning, and the hum fell away behind them before they were halfway to the low ground. Up the shell, where the surge had thrown it, lay the float, split end to end and full of sand. Deck boards lay farther along in the weed, and a length of the frame, bent nearly double. They kept to the high shell, well back from where the water lapped at the edge.

Teva put her hands on the float. It was warm already, and smooth, and thinner than she had thought for a thing that had held three of them up. The hollow inside it was packed with sand, and the sand was wet with the sea.

"Which bit did you hang on to?" she said.

Keo looked along the weed. "Deck. That, maybe. I don't know."

Benal had crouched at the split end and was looking into it. "If we had the other float," he said, "and the frame were straight, and something to steer it with, then possibly—"

He stopped and looked past the float at the reef.

There were other boats, on the ledge under the overhang at the rock, where the founders had stowed them and the three of them had dug one out. They were on the far side of the water.

"What about your raft, Ben?" Keo said. "Logs, tied together. You wanted one at the river."

Benal looked along the bank at the dry driftwood in the weed. "There's enough here to float one of us. Maybe. Not three." He rubbed his weak arm. "And then there's the reef. And what's out past it."

The sea had taken the boat in the storm with all three of them on it.

"No," Teva said.

Out on the reef the swell lifted a whole tree, roots and all, and let it down on the coral, and lifted it again. Each time it came down the trunk knocked, and the sound came to her a moment late across the water. Mud was still caked in its roots. Birds stood along it and went up shouting whenever it moved, and dropped back onto it. Beside it a raft of fern turned slowly, and beyond that another.

"That's a whole tree," Keo said.

"How does a whole tree get out here?" Teva said.

"Down the river, I'd think," Benal said. "And then out."

The water between the reef and the land was brown, every bit of it, the color of the river at the narrows. She followed it along with her eyes. Off past the end of the island, where nothing stood between it and the open sea, the brown stopped, and the sea beyond was blue, along a line as plain as one scratched in mud with a stick. Nothing like that had come up out of the sea. It had come down off the land.

"After heavy rain the groves are the river's," she said.

She had said it to every yazhi she had ever drilled, at the edge of the pool, until they could say it back to her.

"That's what the shine is," she said. "It's the river. It's out of its bed, all across the plain. Like the groves at home, only kilometers of it."

"The narrows," Keo said.

She could not see them from here, and did not need to. She had sat on the rise above them before they crossed and looked down at the stones, and the old water marks had stood high up on them, far above the river that day.

"There were marks on those stones," she said. "High up. Way over where we crossed. A river that's out across a whole plain is over those, and over its banks. There isn't a narrows any more."

"And whatever was in the river," Benal said, "is in all of it."

"All of it."

She thought of a low grove behind the nests the morning after a storm, brown and still to the ankle, with the trunks standing up out of it and nothing to show where anything lay. Then she made it go on for kilometers.

"How long does it stay like that?" Keo said.

"At home the low groves stand a few days. Even after the flood that took the two Masters, it was days." She looked across at the shine. "That's a river with somewhere to go. This one has a whole plain and nowhere to go. Longer. Weeks, maybe."

It was a guess. Nobody had a better one.

"The hollows hold a day," Benal said.

The sun had found the back of her neck. There was no shade anywhere on the bank.

"All right," she said. "The boat."

"Gone," Keo said.

"The sea."

"No," Benal said.

"Walking." She looked at him. "Ben?"

"Coming out, it took eleven days," <!-- @WF-mudwalk.days: 11 --> he said. "That was with the gel, two suits that still worked, the spikes, food, four skins. Now there's no gel and no spikes, and the suits are dead, and there's Keo's ankle, which I'd guess is half the pace on good ground. Possibly less. And the ground under water for as long as you say. And water for a day." He looked at his hands. <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> "It's eighty kilometers of walking on a route that isn't there any more. Even if we could get across to where it starts."

She had every number now.

The protocols were on the plates in the archive up on the crown, every line of them, whole. At home the cultures went grayer in their vessels each day the three of them were gone. The Seedings had stopped. Omya was going.

"We have it," she said.

Then she sat down on the shell. It was sharp under her, and she did not get up.

Benal sat down beside her, close, with his shoulder against hers.

Keo stayed standing, with his weight off the bad ankle. He was looking past them, across the brown water at the land. Through the whole count he had said one word, and that was not like him.

"Kilometers of shallow water," Teva said, to nobody.

Keo's face changed. She saw it change, and could not read it. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the shine on the land.

He had stood through all of it, she thought, and let her count.

Then he turned to her.

"Teva. There's one way left." He waited until she looked up. "It's been charging two days."

He said it to her and not to the sea. At the fire he had told them, and she had been holding the hot leg of a crayfish in her fingers. She had said *Not now*, and gone back to the leg.
