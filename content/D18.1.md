There was gray grit in the creases of Teva's fingers, and rubbing them together did not get it out. It had come off the soft sheets in the night. She had found Cecilia's messages among Cecilia's own things, in a room along the top of the crown, and read what she could of them by the panel light. Every sheet she turned came apart a little more in her hands. In the end she had left them where they lay.

The sun was just up out of the sea. The water in the hollows stood a hand lower than it had yesterday morning.

Benal was on his knees at the biggest one.

"Three each," he said. "Mouthfuls, not swallows. Three now and three at dusk, and tomorrow what's left, which I think gets us to about the middle of the day."

He drank his three and sat back on his heels. Keo went down after him. Teva watched his throat. It moved twice, and then he lifted his face out of the water and wiped his mouth on his wrist.

"Go on, Teva," he said. "Drink yours."

She let it go and drank. She counted three, and stopped.

"I found Cecilia's messages in the night," she said.

Benal looked up at her from the water, and then down into it again. Keo nodded. She left it there.

From the hollows the land lay across the water to the south, lit from the side by the low sun. Between the dark of the coast's trees there were pale patches that held the sky. They were in every gap, as far as she could see either way. Behind the first trees nothing showed. It looked like the groves at home after rain, going on to the edge of the world.

"Was it like that yesterday?" she said.

Benal shaded his eyes. "Less, I think. And less the day before."

She had seen it both mornings from here and taken it for storm water that would drain.

"We'll go down and look at what's left of the boat," she said. "It's the only thing on the island we know floats."

Keo was already getting his good foot under him. "I'll come."

They went down the slope they had come up the first morning, and the hum fell away behind them. Up the shell, where the surge had thrown it, lay the float, split end to end. Deck boards lay farther along in the weed, and a length of the frame, bent nearly double. They kept to the high shell, well back from where the water lapped at the edge.

Teva put her hands on the float. It was warm already and smooth, thinner than she had thought for a thing that had held three of them up. The hollow inside it was packed with sodden sand.

Benal had crouched at the split end and was looking into it. "If we had the other float," he said, "and the frame were straight, and something to steer it with, then possibly—"

He stopped and looked past the float at the reef.

There were other boats on the ledge under the overhang at the rock, where the founders had stowed them and the three of them had dug one out, on the far side of the water.

"What about your raft, Ben?" Keo said. "Logs, tied together. You wanted one at the river."

Benal looked along the bank at the dry driftwood in the weed. "There's enough here to float one of us. Maybe. Not three." He rubbed his weak arm. "And then there's the reef. And what's out past it."

The sea had taken the boat in the storm with all three of them on it.

"No," Teva said.

Out on the reef the swell lifted a whole tree, roots and all, let it down on the coral and lifted it again. Each time the trunk came down, its knock reached her a moment late across the water. Mud was still caked in its roots. Birds stood along it and went up shouting whenever it moved. Beside it a raft of fern turned slowly, and beyond that another.

The water between the reef and the land was brown, the color of the river at the narrows. Off past the end of the island, where nothing stood between it and the open sea, the brown stopped against blue, along a line as plain as one scratched in mud with a stick. Nothing like that had come up out of the sea. It had come down off the land.

"After heavy rain the groves are the river's," she said.

She had said it to every yazhi she had ever drilled, at the edge of the pool, until they could say it back to her.

"That's what the shine is," she said. "It's the river. It's out of its bed, all across the plain. Like the groves at home, only kilometers of it."

"The narrows," Keo said.

She could not see them from here, and did not need to.

"There were marks on those stones," she said. "High up. Way over the river we crossed. A river that's out across a whole plain is over those, and over its banks. There isn't a narrows any more."

"And whatever was in the river," Benal said, "is in all of it."

"All of it."

"How long does it stay like that?" Keo said.

"At home the low groves stand a few days. Even after the flood that took the two Masters, it was days. That river has somewhere to go." She looked across at the shine. "This one has a whole plain and nowhere to go. Longer. Weeks, maybe."

It was a guess. Nobody had a better one.

"The hollows hold a day," Benal said.

The sun had found the back of her neck. There was no shade anywhere on the bank.

"All right," she said. "The boat."

"Gone," Keo said.

"The sea."

"No," Benal said.

"Walking." She looked at him. "Ben?"

"Coming out, it took eleven days," <!-- @WF-mudwalk.days: 11 --> he said. "That was with the gel, two suits that still worked, the spikes, food, four skins. Now there's no gel, no spikes, no food, no skins, and the suits are dead. There's Keo's ankle, which slows all of us, possibly by a lot. And the ground under water for as long as you say. And water for a day." He looked at his hands. <!-- @WF-route.overland: 80km --> "It's eighty kilometers of walking on a route that isn't there any more. Even if we could get across to where it starts."

She had every number now.

The protocols were on the plates in the archive up on the crown, every line of them. At home the cultures went grayer in their vessels each day the three of them were gone. The Seedings had stopped. Omya was going.

"We have it," she said.

Then she sat down on the shell. It was sharp under her, and she did not get up.

Benal sat down beside her, close, with his shoulder against hers.

Keo stayed standing, with his weight off the bad ankle. He was looking past them, across the water at the land. Through the count he had said one word, and that was not like him.

"Kilometers of shallow water," Teva said, to nobody.

Keo's face changed. She saw it change, and could not read it.

Then he turned to her.

"Teva. There's one way left." He waited until she looked up. "It's been charging two days."

He said it to her and not to the sea. At the fire he had told them, and she had been holding the hot leg of a crayfish in her fingers. She had said *Not now*, and gone back to the leg.
