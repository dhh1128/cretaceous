"Not yet."

Teva stepped back out onto the open floor next to Benal. Her heart was going hard.

She had the speech ready. She had rehearsed it these past few days, whenever her mind was free. Three things, in order, level, with no tears in it for anyone to point at. It was an accusation from its first word, and she had checked every word of it against the founders' own metal.

She opened her mouth.

Then shut it.

For a week the anger had held her up. It had got her off the floor at Genesis and up the cut to the water, and all the way home. She reached for it now and found less than there had been.

Here and there in the crowd somebody was still crying over the number, into a hand or a shoulder. The people nearest the open floor were looking at her suit. Elana, at the front of them, was looking at her face.

Teva opened her mouth again.

"I had a speech ready for you. I made it at Genesis. I was so angry at you that I could hardly breathe. All my life this Council has told us we had centuries. It told us the founders wrapped the old tech, that the founders forbade going outside, that the founders left us every rule we keep. Half of that isn't on any plate at Genesis. And nobody up there ever went to look."

Hesh sat forward, elbows on his knees and hands hanging between them, his eyes on the floor at her feet. Sila sat very straight, her jaw set. Around them the rest of the Council sat in the yellow-green light.

They wore the faces she had seen in her flash at the water building.

Now she realized she had been dreading this moment of seeing since Genesis, without letting herself name it. Here it was — a moment when her anger crumbled. She stood still and waited for it to break her. Instead something under her breastbone let go, like a knot pulled loose, and her breath went out with it.

The room waited. She let it.

"But I've seen the founders' faces, in one of those flashes that the stories talk about. I saw that some stayed, when the others walked to Enclave. They looked the way you look now. They weren't lying to anyone. They were frightened, and they did the best they could with what they knew. And so have you."

Her words went out, and nothing came back.

"That's all any of us has done. It's all Keo and Ben and I did, when we took the suits." She turned to face the side wall and gestured. "It's all Keo's parents did, keeping them."

She turned back to the dais.

"So. Let them go. They kept those suits faithfully, every week, and the suits brought three of us home. If anyone answers for the suits, it's us. We took them."

Sila came forward to the edge of the dais, her color up.

"This Council has kept every child in this village alive for two hundred years, on the founders' word. You stole from the Repository in the dark. You want to answer for it? Then you will." She turned to the watchers. "Take the three of them. Hold them with the custodians."

Two watchers came forward from the foot of the dais. Keo moved in against Teva's shoulder, and Benal on her other side.

Elana stepped out of the crowd, unhurried, and stood on the open floor between the watchers and the three of them.

"No," she said flatly. "You will not be taking them anywhere." She paused. "My daughter died waiting for what that girl carried home."

The watchers stopped.

Behind her the floor came up, all of it at once, over one another.

"Leave them be."

"Look at them."

"They brought the cure home, Sila."

On the dais Hesh had kept his eyes on the room. He said one word without raising his voice.

"Sila."

Along the dais the rest of the Council had turned to him, away from her. Nobody on it said a word for her.

Then he stood, beside her, and spoke to all of them. "I think the rest of us agree that nobody is held tonight. Let the custodians go."

At the side wall the watchers stepped back from Joram and Riel.

Sila looked out over the floor for a long moment. Then she sat, very straight.

"This Council sits again tomorrow. On the suits. On what these three did. And on the thing that is lying in our river."

The chamber held its breath for a moment, then the meeting came apart. People stood and talked to each other instead of to the dais. Somewhere a voice was crying and talking through it.

"Seventeen years. <!-- @WF-impact.window.max: 17y --> Mine isn't even named yet."

The ones who passed Teva went wide around her and looked at the suit.

Keo crossed the floor to the side wall, where his parents stood with nobody at their shoulders now. Teva had come a few steps after him without meaning to, near enough to hear.

Joram and Riel were looking at their son. Over Keo's shoulder she could see both their faces, and Joram's was working.

"It's everything you said it was. Out there." Keo's voice gave in the middle of it, and he pushed on. "You said I'd die in it, and I went without a suit, and I…"

"Came back," Joram choked out.

Keo stepped into his father's arms. Riel came in close against the two of them with her mouth by Keo's ear. She spoke low, and only Teva was near enough to hear.

"Two words. So efficient."

Tears ran down Riel's cheeks. Joram had shut his eyes. Keo's back was shaking, and Teva could not tell whether he was laughing.

A few paces off, Marek still stood at Benal's side, gazing up at his son. Teva knew what his pity looked like, from Benal in the dark at Genesis: the hand on the shoulder, covering the whole of it, the waiting, and then the kindness. This was a much more beautiful expression, and it was matched by Lunet, whose fingertips were pressed against her lips.

Benal sighed at Marek.

"You said a thing to me once, in passing. *Those aren't anything, Ben. You're just modeling a theory. What might be. Spend some time with something that you actually live.* That was the part I couldn't get. It turns out that what you actually live is the part that makes the whole equation come out. I had it from you the entire time."

The man who was quick with his words stood with his mouth a little open and let his son finish, and had nothing to say after.

Teva stood on the open floor below the dais, between the two families, and felt her heart groan.

First her father had died. Then her mother, two years ago, in their own nest, with the morning coming pale through the roof. Omya was still sitting on a stool against the Vitarium wall, with somebody else's loops in her hair, and this afternoon she had looked at Teva and asked about figs.

She was glad. So glad. The happiness shot through her hot and fierce, for Keo with his face in his father's shoulder, and for Benal, whose father had let him finish. And there was pain, too, just as hot and fierce. Nobody in this room was standing beside her. The feelings came from the same place, and they couldn't be separated.

The lightmold along the wall swam and doubled. Something ran warm down beside her nose and into the corner of her mouth, and it was salt. Her chin would not hold still. She kept her arms at her sides and did not put a hand up, and her face went on getting wet.
