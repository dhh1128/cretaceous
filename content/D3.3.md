The fog went as quietly as it had come and took the last of the night with it. Teva watched it lift.

What it left behind was not dark. She had always thought it would be, the way the Enclave talked about it: dense jungle, past the tangle, where light did not reach. But the light reached. It came down through the high crowns in long slanting slices, and between the crowns she could see the sky, pale and already hot-looking. It felt... not spacious, but big, and full of dangerous gaps. And at the height of her chest the world closed. Cycad and fern and some shrub with leaves like hands stood packed together, taller than she was, and she could see perhaps two steps into them. She could see a long way up and nowhere at all ahead.

She stood still in it with her eyes shut.

North was there, steady as a hand on her back. That had never been the difficulty. The pull had come down to her from her mother, not from any Seeding, and she could not shut her eyes for it without thinking of her. She let it sit and went back through the run instead, as she would go back through a protocol learned under her grandmother's eye: not in words, but in her body, which had been there.

North almost ahead at the start, a little to the left. Then the turn at the screaming, sharp, when she ran toward it. Then the part which had been longest, north over her left shoulder the whole way. A bend she had not chosen, following Keo. Then the rise, the ground going up under her hands and north swinging across in front of her as they went over. Then they had gone down the other side and on, more slowly.

She opened her eyes. "Ben."

He was sitting on his heels in the ferns, still gray in the face.

"I can tell you how we went," she said. "Your arm says how far. Can you work out where that leaves us?"

He got the geometry problem at once. She gave him the legs one at a time, and he stooped and drew with his finger and moved his lips. When she said *the long part* he asked how long, as a share of the whole. She didn't know, and said so. Then he asked whether it was longer than the bit before the rise, and she found that she knew that. He did it all out loud, in long folding sentences that went around a thing twice before they set it down.

"So we're — possibly — east of the line. I can't say how far, because nobody knows how long that leg really was." He drew a line in the dirt with one finger and then another beside it, close. "Aim a little west of the heading for now, and I'd want to say possibly again."

"You've said it," said Teva.

She turned until north sat where it should for the narrows, then a little further.

Keo had stood at the edge of the ferns the whole time with his spike. He looked from one of them to the other as if he had come in partway through. "Well? Which way does the idiot go?"

Benal's face went dark red over the collar of the suit. "I didn't — I sh-shouldn't have —"

"You should have. You left one out, though." Keo grinned at him. "Reckless, unsuited and handsome. Which way, Teva?"

She pointed, and he went. They followed, Noli close at Keo's side and well clear of Teva's spike.

By the time the sun was well up the heat had come down to meet it. It lay on the ferns and on the backs of their necks, and the insects came up out of everything. A branch she brushed gave off the smell of crushed needles, sharp with resin. Inside the suit Teva was cool, and it kept surprising her. Her whole life heat had found her first and stayed longest. Now it stood a finger's width off her skin and could not get in. Only her face knew it was there.

Ahead of her, Keo's neck was lumped red with bites. So was his clawed forearm, and his knees through the split leggings. He slapped at them now and then and walked on.

Benal stopped and tilted his head. "Frogs."

She could not hear them at first. Eventually she could, though, a soft clotted croaking ahead and to the right. A moment after that the ground under her boots went soft.

Slow water. Every one of them knew what that meant.

The backwater lay in a hollow, brown at its edges and clear further out, with horsetail standing up out of it like bundles of green rods. The frogs went quiet. Teva stopped well short of the edge without deciding to. She knew the drill as well as her own name; she had drilled it into a line of children yesterday. Her body did not care what she knew.

Their skins were half empty already, and the day was not half done.

Keo went forward a few steps and stood with his eyes half closed, and she knew he was reaching out with his sense. Then he shook his head once.

"Too many living things. It's all noise."

He took another step and stopped dead. He lifted his face, nostrils flaring. Teva smelled mud and rot and nothing else. Then he looked, carefully, not at the water but at the horsetail growing thick along the bank to their left.

"Back up," he said quietly. "Slowly. Don't run."

She looked where he was looking and saw stems. Only stems.

"There's a croc lying up in there. On the bank, in the horsetail."

"I don't see it."

"Neither did I, till I caught its musk." He did not take his eyes off the place. "Everything in there goes up and down. There's one thing in it that goes across."

She looked again. She saw stems.

They backed away as the drill said, keeping their faces to it, keeping together, and circled wide of the hollow to where a runnel came down over a lip of pale rock into it. It was a hand deep and two hands wide, so clear she could see every pebble on its bottom. Nothing could hide in water like that. The yazhi pool was water like that.

Keo knelt and filled the skins one after another. Teva stood over him with the spike held toward the backwater, Benal beside her. Frogs sat along the runnel's edge, and the boys caught some for later, Keo quick, Benal with a kind of horrified care. Where the runnel widened, little silver-sided fish hung in the current. When her shadow fell on them they were gone.

Teva watched the hollow until the ferns shut it from view.

They lay up when the late-morning heat drove them under a big conifer with its roots spread like knuckles over the ground. Keo showed her how to feed her suit. Just under the ribs it was thicker than anywhere else and gave under her thumb like moss. He had her bend over it, so the drips off the conifer would not get in. She tipped her waterskin over it a mouthful at a time, and the patch drank each one before she poured the next, the way dry ground takes rain. It went dark, then slowly came back to the color of the bark behind her. She fed it before she drank, though her mouth was dry. Neither boy said anything about it.

"It'll want something sweet," Keo said. "In a day or two."

None of them had anything sweet.

Benal's arm said how far they had walked since the tangle. Teva set the run's turns against it, as well as anybody could, and laid what was left against the whole of the way she had carried in her head since the kiva, and did the division. She did not say the answer out loud.
