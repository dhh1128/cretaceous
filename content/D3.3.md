The fog went as quietly as it had come and took the last of the night with it. Teva watched it lift.

What it left behind was not dark. She had always thought it would be, the way the Enclave talked about it: dense jungle, past the tangle, where light did not reach. But the light reached. It came down through the high crowns in long slanting slices, and between the crowns she could see the sky, pale and already hot-looking. It felt... not spacious, but big, and full of dangerous gaps. And at the height of her chest the world closed. Cycad and fern and some shrub with leaves like hands stood packed together, taller than she was, and she could see perhaps two steps into them. She could see a long way up and nowhere at all ahead.

She stood still in it with her eyes shut.

North was there, as it always was, steady as a hand on her back. That had never been the difficulty. She let it sit where it was and went back instead through the run, the way she would go back through a protocol she had been made to learn under her grandmother's eye: not in words, but in her body, which had been there.

They had started with north almost ahead of her, a little to the left. Then the turn at the screaming, sharp, when she ran toward it. Then the part which had been longest, with north over her left shoulder the whole way and the spike held back behind her. A bend she had not chosen, following Keo. Then the rise. The ground went up under her hands, and north swung across in front of her as they went over it. Then they had gone down the other side and on, more slowly.

She opened her eyes. She knew how they had gone. "Ben."

He looked up. He had been sitting on his heels in the ferns, still gray in the face.

"I can tell you how we went," she said. "Your arm says how far. Can you work out where that leaves us?"

He understood at once, which she had not expected. She gave him the legs one at a time, in the order they had come back to her, and he stooped and drew with his finger and moved his lips. When she said *the long part* he asked how long, as a share of the whole. She did not know, and said so. Then he asked whether it was longer than the bit before the rise or shorter, and she found that she did know that. He did it all out loud, in long folding sentences that went around a thing twice before they set it down. It took a while.

"So we're — possibly — east of the line, though I can't tell you by how much, because nobody knows how long that leg really was." He drew a line in the dirt with one finger and then another one beside it, close. "Aim a little west of the heading for now, and I'd want to say possibly again."

"You've said it," said Teva.

She turned until north sat where it should for the narrows, then a little further. She pointed.

Keo had been standing at the edge of the ferns the whole time with his spear, waiting to be told which way, and he looked from one of them to the other as if he had come in partway through. Then he went where she pointed. They followed, Noli close at Keo's side and well clear of Teva's spike.

By the time the sun was well up the heat had come down to meet it. Somewhere a branch she brushed gave off the smell of crushed pine, sharp with resin. It lay on the ferns and on the backs of their necks, and the insects came up out of everything. Teva walked inside it and was cool. She kept noticing it, and kept being surprised. Her whole life heat had been the thing that found her first and stayed longest, and now it stood a finger's width off her skin and could not get in. Only her face knew it was there.

Ahead of her, Keo's neck was lumped red with bites. So was his clawed forearm, and his knees through the split leggings. He said nothing about any of it and she did not either.

Benal stopped, and tilted his head. "Frogs."

She could not hear them. Ten more steps and she could, a soft clotted croaking ahead and to the right. A moment after that the ground under her boots went soft.

So it was slow water. Every one of them knew what slow water meant.

The backwater lay in a hollow, flat and brown at its edges and clear further out, with horsetail standing up out of it in dense stands like bundles of green rods. The frogs went quiet as they came. Teva stopped well short of the edge without deciding to. Her body stopped her. She knew the drill as well as she knew her own name, she had drilled it into a line of children yesterday, and her body did not care what she knew.

Their skins were low. They had drunk them half down by midmorning, in this heat, and the day was not half done.

Keo went forward a few steps and stood with his eyes half closed, and she knew he was reaching out with the sense he had that she did not. Then he opened his eyes and shook his head once.

"Too many living things," he said. "It won't separate them. It's all noise."

He took another step and stopped dead. He lifted his face a little, and his nostrils flared. Teva smelled mud and rot and nothing else. He did not move for a long moment. Then he looked, carefully, not at the water but at the bank, at the horsetail growing thick along it to their left.

"Back up," he said, quietly. "Slowly. Don't run."

She looked where he was looking and saw stems. Only stems.

"There's a croc lying up in there," he said. "On the bank. In the horsetail."

"I don't see it."

"Neither did I, till I caught its musk." He did not take his eyes off the place. "Everything in there goes up and down. There's one thing in it that goes across."

She looked again. She saw stems.

They backed away as the drill said, keeping their faces to it, keeping together, and circled well clear of the hollow until they found where a little runnel came down over a lip of pale rock and into the hollow. It was a hand deep and two hands wide, and so clear that she could see every pebble on its bottom. Nothing could hide in water like that. The yazhi pool was water like that.

Keo filled the skins there, kneeling, one after another. Teva stood over him with the spike held toward the backwater. Benal stood beside her. Teva watched the hollow while Keo filled the last skin, and kept watching after he stood, until the ferns shut it from view.

There were frogs along the runnel's edge. Keo and Benal caught some, Keo quick and Benal with a kind of horrified care, and put them in a pouch for later. In the clear water where the runnel widened she saw fish, little and silver-sided, hanging in the current. When her shadow fell on them they were gone.

They lay up when the late-morning heat drove them into shade, in the shade of a big conifer with its roots spread like knuckles over the ground. Keo showed her how to feed her suit. There was a place on the belly, just under the ribs, where the suit was thicker than anywhere else and gave under her thumb like moss. He had her bend over it, so the drips off the conifer would not get in. She tipped her waterskin over it a mouthful at a time, and the patch drank each one before she poured the next, the way dry ground takes rain. It went dark where it had drunk, and then slowly came back to the color of the bark behind her. She fed it before she drank, though her own mouth was dry. Neither boy said anything about it.

"It'll want something sweet," Keo said. "In a day or two."

None of them had anything sweet.

Benal's arm said how far they had walked since the tangle, the run and all. Teva set the run's turns against it, as well as anybody could, and laid what was left against the whole of the way she had carried in her head since the kiva. Then she did the division. She did not say the answer out loud.
