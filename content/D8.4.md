"Keo."

It was hardly a whisper. Benal was lying on his side with his eyes open, and his hand had come out of the dark and closed on Keo's ankle.

"Listen."

Keo listened. The fire was down to coals, and beyond the fence the woodland was full of its night noise. Under the insects and the drip and the river below them out of sight, now that he was listening for it, something heavy came down in the litter. Then nothing, and then again, slow, the weight of it settling, like a big animal in no hurry at all.

Then he heard it breathe, long and deep and wet, through something large.

He reached for it before he could stop himself, through the ache the push had left behind his eyes. It was there, beyond the stakes, among the trees: one presence, big, alone. The kind that ate other things. That was all his sense would give him. It never gave him more.

"Something big," he whispered. "One. It's a hunter."

Teva was already sitting up.

Keo turned his head to find Noli. Tonight, for the first time, she had bedded down close against the bare fence by the gap, because there was no gel on the stakes to keep her off. She lay flat now in her hollow with her chin on the ground and her whole body pressed into it, and what came down the bond from her was fear so pure it had no shape at all.

The weight in the litter was on the far side of the thicket now, going away from them. He got to the gap on his knees. Teva was there before he had asked, pulling the thorn back with her sleeves over her hands. He pushed *come, come, good, come*, and Noli came, low and fast. She went in under his arm and pressed herself against his legs and did not move. Teva pulled the thorn back across the gap.

"Build up the fire," Teva whispered.

"Put it out."

"Things are afraid of fire."

"It shows them where we are."

"It knows where we are, Keo. It can smell us."

"Possibly," Benal whispered, "you're both right, and that's the problem."

"Whose watch is it?" Teva said.

"Mine," Keo said.

"It's everybody's," Benal said, and that was the end of that.

Teva had her spike up across her knees. She turned it in the starlight so the tip showed, the croc's tooth bound to the hardwood, bare and pale.

"There's nothing on it," she said flatly, as if telling them the time.

They left the fire as it was.

It walked the fence. They heard it go along the downhill side, slow, stopping, going on, and Keo followed it with his sense until he was too tired to hold it, and then followed it with his ears instead. It went around the corner by the big ginkgo, where the fence ended and the thicket began. It stopped there a long time.

Benal counted in a whisper. After a while Keo understood what: its steps, and the spaces between them.

"It's walked this side twice," Benal breathed, after a while. "It hasn't hurried once." He shut his eyes. "I wish I hadn't counted."

It breathed outside the stakes, close. Keo could smell it now, rank, meat gone old in a mouth. Benal already had his sleeve over his face.

It did not try the fence. It stood there and breathed, and then it went on around the back of the thicket. They heard the trunks creak where it brushed them.

Benal laughed, very quietly, high, not like himself. "Well," he whispered. "At least it'll want the delicious one first."

It came out too thin, his voice going up at the end, and Benal stopped.

"You'll have to go out and stand in front of us," Keo whispered. "Like a fence."

Benal let his breath go, shaking, and after a moment his shoulder came to rest against Keo's.

It went away and came back, and they did not know if it was the same one. They talked, in whispers, about anything. About the crossing, and the skins. About whether Noli had known before Benal did, and whether she had been calling all morning because she had smelled it on the other bank. About nothing at all. Every time one of them stopped, the woodland came in around the silence, and somebody started again.

Keo's head was splitting. Noli lay pressed against his legs and shivered. He kept one hand on her and did not try to push anything.

Some time before dawn it came along the fence one more time, slow, and stopped by the gap to breathe.

"It's counting us," Benal whispered.

"It can't count," Teva whispered. "That's your job."

Keo snorted before he could stop it and put his face down on his knees. Benal was shaking against his shoulder, and he could not tell if it was laughing.

None of them slept.

Gray came up between the trees. Near dawn they heard it go: the slow weight in the litter going away downhill, farther each time, until there was nothing. The woodland went quiet all at once, as it did at the turn of the night, and then the birds started. They waited until it was full light and listened, and Keo reached as far as his head would let him and found nothing large anywhere near. Then he opened the gap and went out with his spike, and the other two came behind him.

The ground outside the stakes was soft, and the tracks were in it. Three toes, wide-splayed, each print as long as his forearm, sunk deep. They went along the fence on the downhill side, around the corner by the ginkgo, along the outside of the thicket, and down again. All the way around.

Keo crouched and put his hand in one. It did not come close to filling it.

They packed, and went.
