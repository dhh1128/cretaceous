The fire was down to coals, and Keo sat over it watching the dark. Benal's hand closed on his ankle.

"Keo," Benal breathed. "Listen."

Beyond the fence the woodland chirred. Under the insects and the river below, something heavy came down in the litter. Then nothing. Then again, slow, the weight settling.

Then it breathed, long and deep and wet, through something large.

Keo reached for it before he could stop himself, through the ache the push had left behind his eyes. It was there beyond the stakes: one presence, big, alone, the kind that ate other things. His sense would give him no more.

"Something big," he whispered. "One. A hunter."

Teva was already sitting up.

Keo found Noli. She lay flat in her hollow by the gap, chin on the ground, her whole body pressed into it, and what came down the bond was fear so pure it had no shape.

The weight in the litter had gone round to the far side of the thicket. He crawled to the gap. Teva was there before he asked, dragging the thorn back with her sleeves over her hands. He pushed *come, come, good, come*, and Noli came, low and fast, in under his arm and hard against his legs. Teva hauled the thorn back across.

"Build up the fire," Teva whispered.

"Put it out."

"Things are afraid of fire."

"It shows them where we are."

"It knows where we are, Keo. It can smell us."

"Possibly," Benal whispered, "you're both right, and that's the problem."

"Whose watch is it?" Teva said.

"Mine."

"It's everybody's," Benal said.

Teva had her spike across her knees. She turned it in the starlight so the tip showed, the croc's tooth bound to the hardwood, bare.

"There's nothing on it," she said.

They left the fire as it was.

It walked the fence. They heard it go along the downhill side, slow, stopping, going on. Keo followed it with his sense until he was too tired to hold it, then with his ears. It went around the corner by the big ginkgo, where the fence met the thicket, and stood there a long time.

Benal was whispering numbers. After a while Keo understood: its steps, and the spaces between them.

"It's walked this side twice," Benal breathed. "It hasn't hurried once." He shut his eyes. "I wish I hadn't counted."

It breathed outside the stakes, close. Keo could smell it now, meat gone old in a mouth; Benal already had his sleeve over his face. It didn't try the fence. It went on around the back of the thicket, and the trunks creaked where it brushed them.

Benal laughed, not like himself. "Well," he whispered. "At least it'll want the delicious one first."

It came out thin, going up at the end, and he stopped.

"You'll have to go out and stand in front of us," Keo whispered. "Like a fence."

Benal let his breath go, shaking, and his shoulder came to rest against Keo's.

It went away and came back, and they didn't know if it was the same one. They talked in whispers, about anything: the crossing, the skins, whether Noli had known before Benal did, whether that was why she had called and called from this bank. Whenever one of them stopped, the woodland came in around the silence, and somebody started again.

Keo's head was splitting. Noli shivered against his legs. He kept a hand on her and pushed nothing.

Some time before dawn it came along the fence once more and stopped by the gap to breathe.

"It's counting us," Benal whispered.

"It can't count," Teva whispered. "That's your job."

Keo snorted and put his face down on his knees. Benal was shaking against his shoulder, and Keo couldn't tell if it was laughing.

The dark thinned between the trees. They heard it go: the slow weight in the litter, downhill, farther each time, until there was nothing. The woodland hushed, as it did at the turn of the night, and then the birds started. They waited for full light. Keo reached as far as his head would let him and found nothing large. Then he opened the gap and went out with his spike, the other two behind him.

The tracks were in the soft ground outside the stakes. Three toes, splayed, each print as long as his forearm, sunk deep. Along the fence on the downhill side, around the ginkgo, along the outside of the thicket, and down again. All the way around.

Keo crouched and put his hand in one. It did not come close to filling it.
