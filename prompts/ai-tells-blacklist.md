# AI tells — the blacklist

*Built 2026-09-10 from Daniel's line-by-line critique of two generated drafts of scene 2.3. Every entry below is a defect he actually found in generated prose, in his words where possible. This file grows: each time he critiques a draft, new tells are added and the old ones are checked against the new draft.*

**How to use it.** Run it as a checklist over a draft *before* Daniel sees it. Every hit is either fixed or justified. This is a pre-delivery gate, not a style aspiration.

**Read the quote, not just the rule.** The blockquotes are Daniel's own words about a real draft and they are the evidence. The **Rule** lines under them are a paraphrase — somebody's reading of what each complaint implies in general — and a paraphrase can be wider than the thing it paraphrases. Where a rule sounds more absolute than the quote above it, the quote wins. Reviewed on this basis 2026-09-10.

---

## 1. Sentence fragments in exposition

> "Both things repeated so often…truth" — not a complete sentence. AI tick. I write in complete sentences. Sometimes they are short. Once in a great while, I might actually use an incomplete sentence. AIs do this partial sentence stuff WAY more than I do, and it's a dead giveaway. It feels inelegant to me, and clipped. I only do it for effect.

**Rule.** Complete sentences in narration, in his words *"sometimes short."* A fragment once in a great while, for effect, doing work no complete sentence could do. *"Protectively, perhaps."* should have been a continuation of the sentence before it.

**Dialogue is exempt.** People don't speak in complete sentences.

**Test.** If prepending *"It was… / There was… / This is…"* recovers the meaning, it was a chopped sentence. Write it out.

## 2. Stacked adjectives

> "small clay knock": stacked adjectives. clay adds nothing.
> "thin clear" is stacked adjectives; never use 2 if 1 suffices, and only use 1 if it makes something vivid or clearer in the mind's eye.

**Rule.** Never two where one suffices. Never one unless it makes the thing vivid or corrects a mistaken picture.

**Test.** *"If the reader can imagine it without an adjective, and is likely to get it right enough without your help, cut it."*

## 3. Adverbs and modifiers that add nothing

> "Water tapped hollowly" — what does "hollowly" add?

**Rule.** Delete any modifier whose removal leaves the sentence meaning the same thing.

## 4. Repeated words in close quarters

> "pale square" — third use of pale in 500 words.

**Rule.** A content word landing three times inside ~500 words is a flag to check, not an automatic cut — some repetition is load-bearing and some is invisible. What he caught was a word doing no work on its third appearance. See also `repetition-hawk.md`.

## 5. Detail introduced and never used

> What is the point of "Riel lifted the second gourd but did not drink"? It seems like you're trying to fill up the scene with detail, but you're not doing anything with that detail. It has no point.
> What is the point of saying "he had not slept properly before going out, but that was nobody's concern"? Seems like filler.
> "Anyone could see that" does nothing and should be cut.
> What is the relevance of "Neither apologized". Seems like a non-sequitur.

**Rule.** Every concrete detail earns its place by doing one of three jobs: making the environment vivid, making a character better understood, or surfacing subtext. A detail doing none of these is filler, and filler reads as an AI padding to a word count.

## 6. Specifics that slow the reader without paying

> "eastern screen" — why does it matter that it's the eastern one? Don't add details that slow the reader down.
> The recitation of location and sizes/dimensions has lost me completely. What is a class four substrate mimic, and WHY ON EARTH is it relevant to Keo or to me as a reader? … This is full of mysterious non-sequiturs and is worthless drivel, very frustrating.

**Rule.** A specific must be legible and relevant at the moment it appears. Technical-sounding precision with no function is the worst offender, because it reads as competence and delivers nothing.

## 7. Terms used with no hint of meaning

> What is a "morning jar"?
> What is a "perimeter cord"? You've used the term twice and I have no idea what it is, even though I read the entire milieu brief and helped plan the whole novel.
> "overnight cloth" and "catch basin" need some explanation. Using the terms without explanation may be what your instructions say, but this one needs slightly better hints. Maybe instead of "Water ran" you could say "Rainwater caught overnight ran in a thin line".

**Rule.** Leaving in-world terms unglossed is right — that instruction stands. What he is asking for is *hints*, not explanation: his own fix was *"Water ran"* → *"Rainwater caught overnight ran in a thin line"*, which glosses nothing and teaches everything. The test he applied: if the author, who planned the novel, cannot decode it, the reader cannot. All three terms he flagged were **coined compound nouns**, so those are the ones to look at hardest — a clause showing the thing working usually beats a name for it.

## 8. Unearned callbacks

> "The old joke was gone": you haven't developed the joke of his taciturn nature enough to make the joke work. The previous version of this, where it was a game sustained over several turns of the conversation, made the humor actually work, which made the banishment of it work.
> "Not a joke now" — again, you haven't shown enough joke to earn this line.

**Rule.** A callback needs enough of the original on the page to be recognized, and the two he rejected were each carrying more weight than their setup could bear. In map terms this is a `[requires]` pointing at something too thin. How much setup is enough is a judgment; that there was not enough here is not.

## 9. Redundant emotional labelling

> I think "with the insult settling into him" is overkill. We can tell that he feels insulted because we said "That made it worse" just above. Plus it's redundant with "wanted the suit more fiercely", which comes after and is better.

**Rule.** State an emotional fact once, in its strongest form. Naming it again afterwards is the narration distrusting the reader.

## 10. Implausible somatic description

> "heat begin beneath his ribs" — this is an odd location for heat. If he's angry, I would imagine his face would flush or his palms would sweat.

**Rule.** Put a physical sensation where a body actually puts it.

## 11. Confusing blocking

> "Riel leaned toward the trunk…Joram leaned away": this is confusing, especially when it says that Keo is going to slant to meet them. I can conceive of a way that this could be true, but it positions people oddly, and it requires mental gymnastics from the reader. Slows the reader down.
> In contrast, "they only leaned together" makes sense and works, as a visual image that fits what's being described.

**Rule.** The reader must be able to build the picture on one pass. If positioning takes reconstruction, rewrite it.

## 12. Invented precision no character would have

> Not realistic that Keo would count his paces and know that it took him 2 hours and 9 minutes. Where did you get this obsession with precision measurements?
> Riel's measurements: this doesn't make sense to me. She has a magnetic sense. How does that translate to precise measurements or perfect sequential memory? Those are two different things. She sounds more like a mentat in Dune.
> I don't know why you're so obsessed with measurements, but even in your obsession, you're inconsistent. You have exact units (11 degrees) and inexact ones (body-lengths, paces).

**Rule.** A character's precision must follow from an established faculty, and the faculty must actually imply it. Do not generalize one character's stated trait to the family or the culture. Keep units consistent within a POV.

## 13. Characters with nothing to want

> Why do you have the parents not looking up, not engaging with their son, being so dang wooden? They are total duds, showing no interest or emotion and not engaging at all. I hate them, in both versions. I can see Keo being frustrated, but these are good people who care about their son, and they would be making an effort to connect with him unless they are truly dysfunctional.
> Both Riel and Joram are cardboard cutouts here, having no goal but to sit and say things as foils to Keo.

**Rule.** A character who exists only to be spoken at is furniture, and he will hate them. Note his own carve-out — *"they would be making an effort to connect with him **unless they are truly dysfunctional**"* — so the rule is not that everyone always wants something, it is that people who would care are shown caring, and a character who genuinely has nothing to want needs that to be the point. Giving every present character a `wants` line in the scene map is a cheap way to catch the failure; it is a diagnostic, not a quota. This is usually a brief defect rather than a prose defect — check the map first.

## 14. Unmotivated significant action

> The device of having her fill a gourd for the lost daughter sort of works, but not completely, because the narrative raises an unanswered question that we'll never pay back (why did she do this today? Is it just because of Alira? If so, we need to motivate that connection better).

**Rule.** A gesture the reader is meant to notice needs an in-world reason to happen *now*. A standing habit shown once looks like a trigger and creates a question the book must answer.

## 15. Unsubstantiated invention

> A big part of the failure here is inconsistent or implausible invention. The AI invented sleeping arrangement, family members, and all kinds of other stuff that was not substantiated — and then built a lot of stuff on top of it that I needed to unwind.

Actual instances: nests at the wrong height; a suit stored in a basket in the nest instead of the Repository; Noli sleeping at the top of a rope ladder; a set of twins; Tavi aged younger than Keo when he is older; dawn rendered as cold in a hothouse climate.

**Rule.** Nothing enters a draft that is not either in canon or authorized through the invention ledger. This is the tell that costs the most to fix, because inventions accrete — each one gets built upon, so unwinding one means unwinding a small structure.

## 16. Climate and setting drift

> "clinging cold" / "dawn cold": dawn wouldn't be cold; this is a hothouse environment. Might be misty.

**Rule.** Check the draft's weather, temperature and light against `milieu-brief.md` and `journey-calendar.md` every time. Default AI atmospherics — cold dawns, autumn light, temperate damp — do not belong in this world.

## What worked, kept as positive exemplars

Not everything failed, and the successes are as instructive as the tells.

> "The room changed" — this works. Good contrasting pace. A paragraph by itself. Let the nakedness of it function as an invitation for a reader to guess why.

> "That was why…": this paragraph and the one after it work, and so do "Give me a suit" and "Joram's gaze lifted at last" just before. This part is more vivid and purposeful.

> A number of things about this version work. The variety of sentences, some short, some long, give a good rhythm. Some stuff is left unsaid, implied by action.

**Rhythm was not the problem in those four drafts** — measured against `viking.md`, their sentence-length distribution came out close to target every time, and the real failures were all in §§5–15.

**But do not read that as a standing exemption.** The first draft of scene 1.1 came out at **42.4% sentences of five words or fewer against viking's 25.7%**, and mean 13.0 against 11.2 — the same staccato that made `content/superseded/` unusable, from a drafter who had read this line and taken it at face value. Measure the distribution every time. It is one command and it caught a rewrite.
