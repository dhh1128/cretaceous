# Journey calendar — days, distance, weather, light, temperature

Planning layers B1 (timeline spine) and B7 (weather score), book-wide. Weather is a **score**, written for the whole novel like a soundtrack: every day chosen for dramatic function, for contrast with its neighbors, and for meteorological coherence. It is not decided at the keyboard.

Granularity is the **day**, not the scene, so this survives the Act 2 and Act 3 rescene intact.

---

## 1. Two bugs in the existing schedule

**The calendar is off by one day from 4.3 onward.** `scene-list-v4.md` tags 4.2 as *Day 2, late night* and 4.3 as *Day 2, pre-dawn* — but pre-dawn after late night is the following morning. 5.1 then reads *Day 2, morning*, and 6.1 reads *Day 3*, so everything downstream inherits the slip. **Corrected: 4.3 and 5.1 are Day 3.**

**The Dark Night of the Soul happens in the morning.** 9.4 washes them up at *Day 12, dawn*; 10.1 is *Day 12*; 11.1 is *Day 12, morning*. So the emotional nadir of the novel — the confessions, the chosen family forged, Teva's bridge speech — gets perhaps two hours, in daylight, between a dawn arrival and a mid-morning discovery.

**The fix is already sitting in the material.** 9.4 puts them ashore "in the eerie, unnatural calm of the cyclone's eye." An eye passes in an hour or two, and then **the back half of the storm arrives, and the back half is the worse half.** So: they wash up at dawn on Day 12 into the eye, spend the day trapped and failing to find a way in as the second wall closes over them, and **the Dark Night is the night of Day 12, inside the storm's tail.** Beat 11 moves to the morning of Day 13. This is meteorologically correct, costs nothing, gives the novel's lowest point an actual night, and hands Act 3 the extra day it needs.

## 3. Weather is derived, not chosen

**Weather is constrained by scene mechanics first and tuned for mood last.** Assign it the other way round and you get logical discontinuities — a set-piece that cannot physically work in the weather it was given.

The dependency order:

1. **Mechanics.** What does the scene physically require? Sightlines, scent, sound, footing, buoyancy, fire, the reach of mental projection.
2. **Season coherence.** The arc has to be a possible year.
3. **Geography.** Coastal and inland do not share a sky.
4. **Differential vulnerability.** Which of the three does this hurt, and which does it flatter?
5. **Emotional valence — last, and frequently inverted.**

### The mechanics constraints, worked

| set-piece | requires | forbids | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First river crossing, Day 3** | **still, hot, low water — a glassy river** | rain, storm, flood | The decoy depends on a driven hadrosaur being *the loudest thing in the water*. Rain stipples the surface and masks vibration; a swollen current sweeps the decoy downstream and disperses the crocs it was meant to gather; and Keo has to hold a panicked two-tonne animal with his mind while chaos happens around him. A mirror-flat river with everything in it listening is also **more frightening** than a storm crossing, because the trick is legible and so is its failure. |
| **Constrictor ambush, Day 5** | still air, dense understory | wind, hard rain | An ambush predator the color of dead leaves needs leaf litter that isn't moving, and Benal's hearing has to be defeated by *silence*, not noise. |
| **Sunfever trek, Day 7** | cloudless, no wind | any cloud | Teva's suit has to lose. |
| **Noli taken, Day 8 dawn** | clear low sun | overcast | The flybeak uses the sun at its back. No sun, no ambush. |
| **The raft, Day 11** | building sea, then cyclone | calm | — |
| **Wig run, Day 14** | clear and hot | fog, rain | They must be visible. That is the price of the heresy. |

**The existing scene list gets this wrong at 6.1** — "the first major rains have begun, the river visibly swollen, fast, and opaque with mud" — and it breaks the trick that same list specifies. Struck.

### There is no season pivot, and there does not need to be one

I over-egged this and the research contradicts it. `geo-flora-fauna.md:44` says rainfall was **"moderate and relatively evenly distributed throughout the year, suggesting a pattern of wet and dry seasons rather than a constant monsoon."** A dramatic seasonal turn observable inside fourteen days is not supported.

**It is also unnecessary.** A multi-day rain spell needs no seasonal argument at all — a wet week is ordinary anywhere tropical. So: ordinary rain on Days 4–5 and 8–10, and a cyclone on Day 11. Nothing has to turn.

### Rivers here do not swell. They spread.

This is the correction that matters, and it comes straight out of the geology.

The terrain is a **low-relief coastal plain with no highlands** (`geo-flora-fauna.md:20`) drained by **slow-moving rivers, swamps and marshes** (`:26`). There is no gradient. **A flat-plain river cannot flash-flood and cannot become a torrent** — it has nowhere to fall from and nothing to fall down.

What it does instead is go **wide**. A three-hundred-meter channel becomes three kilometers of shallow standing water across the floodplain, and the crossing point stops existing because the river stops having banks.

**And that is worse than a torrent, not better.** Slow, warm, spreading shallows over a flat plain is the finest crocodilian habitat that could be designed. You are not crossing a river any more. You are wading a croc nursery for half a day with no far bank in sight.

*(Note that this also reinforces the decoy constraint above. A driven hadrosaur only works as a decoy in slow water — a current sweeps the thrashing downstream and the gathering never happens. The geology and the trick agree.)*

### So the door closes in one night, and the cyclone closes it

**They never cross a river twice, and the reason is not the crocs.** It is that after Day 11 there is no river to cross.

The cyclone hits on Day 11 while they are offshore, and a cyclone on a low-relief coastal plain means storm surge plus days of standing water. **The entire route home floods behind them, in a single night, while that same storm is nearly killing them.** They come out of Genesis on Day 13 into a world where the eighty kilometers they walked is now shallow water.

Three things this buys, and they are all better than what I had:

1. **They were not stupid to walk out.** The way home was fine when they left and fine for most of the journey. The world changed. That is a different and much more bearable kind of catastrophe than a miscalculation.
2. **The wig becomes rescue rather than convenience**, and finding it lands as relief rather than as luck.
3. **The realization arrives at the right moment** — not on Day 11, but on Day 13, after Genesis, when they turn to go home and Teva works out what the storm did. She should not say it. Benal should say it out loud, later, because he is the one who does arithmetic aloud.

### One crossing, not nine

A set-piece crossing spends enormous credibility and there is only enough for one. Most water on this route is **not** crossed dramatically — it is waded at shallows, walked around, or bridged by a fallen trunk, the way anyone crossing real terrain finds the easy line. **Exactly one crossing is a genuine channel that cannot be avoided**, and that is Day 3.

They do not get lucky nine times. They get lucky once, at the one place luck was required, and the plan for it was good.

### The score

### Rules

**No two adjacent days share a register.** The table above never repeats a sky twice running, and any rescene must preserve that.

**Weather is differentially dangerous, and that is the instrument.** Heat is Teva's enemy and Keo's flattery — and heat is her *only* climatic enemy, because her thermal failure runs one way. Rain is Benal's enemy, because it is *noise*, and Keo's, because he is unsuited and then injured. Fog favors them. Sun favors the flybeak. **Every weather assignment should hurt at least one of the three and help at least one.** A day that hurts everyone equally is a day the weather is doing nothing.

**Cold is a symptom in this book, never a climate.** This world has no cold in it to threaten anyone. Maastrichtian tropical shelf water runs 28–32 °C, mean annual air temperature is 20–24 °C (`kb/worldbuilding/geo-flora-fauna.md`), and rain lands near the wet-bulb — call it 22 °C, which is a warm shower. Nobody is killed by weather that is merely wet, no matter how long it lasts, and **cold-water shock is not available at any depth they can reach**: the response needs water below about 15 °C and there is none.

So when a character feels cold — and they should, often — it is coming from **inside** them. Starvation impairs thermoregulation, and by Day 9 they are several kilos down (`body-and-resources.md` §1). Blood loss does it. Exhaustion does it. Teva's broken regulation does it worst. That is why `body-and-resources.md` can say *cold at night*, *thin, cold, quiet* and be exactly right, while "cold rain" is wrong: the first is a body failing and the second is a claim about the sky. **Write the symptom, never the climate.** The one genuine external chill in the book is evaporative — wet skin, wind, eighteen hours of cyclone tail on Day 12 — and even that is misery rather than a mechanism of death.

**Never remark on weather that is doing nothing.** The reader can fill in ordinary mud, ordinary damp, ordinary heat. See `writing-style-v2.md` — the Cretaceous only earns description where it is strange.

## 4. The night sky, and the thing they cannot find

**The asteroid is up there, and nobody in this novel has looked.** It is the largest unexploited object in the book.

**They cannot see it, and that is the point.** A ten-to-fifteen kilometer body two decades out is far away and faint — naked-eye invisible until it is nearly on top of them. So they look up for the thing that will end the world and find ordinary stars. **This is how the species stakes ladder becomes visible in the narrative**, alongside the epigraph channel: an empty sky, searched, night after night.

And Benal knows what it would take. You would need instruments, and the instruments are at Genesis. **The sky is one more reason to go, and nobody has used it.**

### The Watch, and what Omya is also losing

See `prompts/milieu-brief.md` §5. **Sky-watching is a Keeper duty**, and the Keepers hold a memorized sky — no chart, because a chart is a durable artifact — drilled from one Keeper to the next and checked against the real sky every clear night for 206 years. They are not looking *for* the impactor, which no unaided eye can see. They maintain a reference exact enough that **anything which was not there before becomes obvious.**

**And nobody has noticed that the Watch is dying too**, because a watch that finds nothing is indistinguishable from a watch that is not happening. The whole colony is measuring the loss of the synthesis protocol. The other half of Omya is going unmeasured.

**So the impactor could already be findable, and there is nobody left who would know.** That is the species stakes ladder, made concrete, in Act 1, without a word of exposition.

**How it lands, scene by scene:**

- **2.4, and this is what the scene is for.** Omya, lucid, recites the sky *perfectly* — sixty years of drilling, remote memory, intact — and then cannot remember a protein fold. **The knowledge nobody values survives; the knowledge keeping them alive is going.** Marek would call star lore dead symbols, which is the exact phrase he uses on Benal's mathematics, and both turn out to matter. Somewhere in it Omya asks whether the sky was watched last night, and Teva has no good answer, because nobody ever told her the duty was hers now.
- **Day 3, first night out.** Teva tries the recitation alone and gets partway. Then finds a gap she cannot fill, and understands that the gap is permanent.
- **Days 6 and 7.** Benal works out what a memorized positional sky actually *is* — a 206-year observational dataset held in one dying woman's head — and is the only person alive who would recognize it as a resource.
- **Genesis.** The colony had two ways to find the impactor: instruments here, and the Watch at home. It lost both, and it only noticed one.
- **Book 2.** Teva holds the only fragment of the sky anyone has left, and it is incomplete.

**This also recasts Teva.** She was being trained for the Watch — that is why Omya taught her the stars at `content/superseded/01.1.md:49`. Her sensory upgrades failing means she has a Keeper's memory and not a Keeper's senses: **she was supposed to inherit the sky and got part of the way.** It gives her a role beyond navigator and it gives her grief an object.

### What is actually overhead

- **No modern constellation exists.** Sixty-six million years of proper motion has dismantled every one of them. The sky is unrecognizable to us and perfectly familiar to them.
- **So the constellations are the founders' — named 206 years ago and passed down intact.** And here is why that matters: **the star lore survived uncorrupted because it is checked against the sky every single night, while the mission instructions drifted because nothing ever tested them.** That is the novel's thesis, sitting in the sky, available to any character who notices it. Give it to Benal.
- **The moon is the one thing that is exactly the same.** It was roughly 2,500 km closer, which is under a percent — imperceptible. Everything in this world is strange except the moon.
- Day length was closer to 23.5 hours. *(Open: whether the colony keeps a 24-hour clock that no longer matches the sun, or long ago recalibrated. They use military time — `milieu-brief.md` §Measurements — so somebody decided this once. Worth deciding on purpose.)*

### Invert the valence, or the score is monotone misery

A weather layer that maps bad weather to bad feeling is a mood ring. The assignments that earn their place are the ones that cut against expectation:

- **Rain as deliverance, Day 8.** They have spent Day 7 nearly dying of heat. When the front breaks that afternoon the first ten minutes of rain are the best thing that has happened to them, and Teva puts her face up into it. The misery starts later, and it starts because it *doesn't stop*.
- **The clear day as the killer, Day 7.** Sun is the antagonist and there is no shelter in a fern savanna. Good weather, worst day.
- **Rain as mercy, Days 8–9.** It is unbearable, and it is the only thing that clears the biting insects. Weather that hurts and helps at once, in the same hour, is worth more than either.
- **The still hot day as the dangerous one, Day 3.** Beautiful, glassy, and the worst possible conditions to be near water.
- **Fog as an ally, Day 3 pre-dawn.** The only reason they get out.
- **The eye of the storm as the cruellest weather in the book, Day 12.** It looks exactly like deliverance.

The pattern to avoid: weather that agrees with the scene. **Do not let it move to suit an emotional beat.** The temptation to refuse specifically is Omya's lucidity ending as the storm-dark comes on in 2.4 — it ends because she tires. If Teva notices herself wanting the dark to mean something and refuses it, that is character. If the narration makes it mean something, that is pathetic fallacy.
