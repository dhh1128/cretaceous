# Benal's equation

**CANON.** Converted from a chat transcript; the reasoning is retained, the conversational framing is gone. One open issue is flagged at the end and belongs to the science-plausibility pass.

## The equation

$$T_d = T_o \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}} \cdot f_{drift}(?)$$

It appears in the text as a written artifact — on Benal's board, in gypsum chalk <!-- @WF-benal.writing.surface: board -->, later etched at Genesis. **The narration never explains it.** The reader is meant to grasp its *shape*: a known part, and a part with a question mark on it.

**It is one line of a system.** The founders' full transit equation is a system of lines: **three lines for place**, which locate the destination in space, and **one line for time**, the one above. The place lines came out right: they put the founders on the right spot on the right world, which is why the error has to be about *when* (below). Benal's board has only ever carried the time line, because it is the only one that does not agree. **The page never shows the place lines.** Benal talks about them as part of a system ("the place lines came out", "it's only the time line that won't agree"), and at Genesis the narration gives them a glance, never a transcription. Their details are recorded here anyway, because a sequel may need them; see *The place lines*.

**The founders' version adds one more term, under the whole system: the return divisor, $\langle R \mid T_d \rangle$** (Q-D5E8, Q-TFTM). At Genesis the time line reads

$$T_d = \frac{T_o \cdot \sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}} \cdot f_{drift}(?)}{\langle R \mid T_d \rangle}$$

and the same divisor stands under the place lines, so that where it is zero there is no *where* as well as no *when*. In plain words: where you land is the origin time, corrected for the transit, corrected for not knowing when we are, **divided by whether you have already been where you are going.** $R$ is the traveler's record: everything earlier than the point they depart from, plus every moment they have lived through. The bracket is 1 when the destination is in that record and 0 when it is not; divided by 1 the equation gives a day, divided by 0 there is no answer and nowhere to land. $f_{drift}$ is a different term and stays unsolved, on the plate as on his board. Benal calls the new term **the return divisor** and $f_{drift}$ **the drift factor**, and nothing else (Q-Z27S): a factor multiplies and a divisor divides, the two names share no word, and *the anchor* already names the drift problem in D2.2, so neither name may borrow it. The bracket notation is not derived from quantum mechanics; it is the book's one postulate about time, stated as a rule the founders found, and the page never explains it.

## The place lines

Recorded although the page never shows them, because a sequel may need them. Three lines, one per dimension of space, solving for where the target spot on Earth is at the destination time. They have to, because nothing holds still across a jump: in 66 million years the solar system travels something like a quarter of an orbit around the galactic center. They are the part of the founders' work that worked. `[-]` Their inputs are the target spot on Earth's surface, the transit's setting (the same one that sets the time line), and Earth's motion through the depth of the jump: its spin, its orbit, and the sun's path around the galaxy. That motion is the long, hard part of the system, and the founders got it right.

## What a jump costs

**The equation says where you land for a given setting of the transit. It does not contain the energy, any more than a trajectory contains its fuel.** The cost is a separate rule, and the page never states it.

**The transit runs through a hidden, curled-up dimension in which every moment of time lies close to every other.** <!-- @WF-transit.route: hidden-dimension --> Its size is never stated, and it is never called Planck-sized: in real physics the smallest scales are the most expensive to reach, and a physics-literate reader would catch the claim that small means cheap. **So depth costs almost nothing.** A thousand years <!-- @WF-secondjump.distance: 1000y --> and sixty-six million <!-- @WF-transit.depth: 66Mya --> cost nearly the same, because in that dimension they are neighbors. **Nearly all the energy is a cost per person of opening the way in**, `[-]` on the order of a hundred kilowatt-hours each, so about twenty-five megawatt-hours for all two hundred and seventy-five <!-- @WF-founders.count: 275 -->. That is shockingly little for sixty-six million years, about what a small town uses in a day, and still some six times the wig's whole charge per traveler, so a jump is never cheap and a short hop is not free. **Short hops cost what long ones do**, which is why nobody can casually go back a week.

**Energy cannot be sent through time either, so every leg is paid for where it starts.** The first jump was powered from home. The way home had to be carried: banking it was the americium generators' other job, about a kilowatt across the planned stay (`plan/milieu-brief.md` §3), roughly twenty-five megawatt-hours in three years <!-- @WF-genesis.occupancy: 3y -->. `[?]` The bank's store, whatever it was, has long since leaked away; what remains is the generators, at about half their old output. What the sequels must reckon with is in `plan/sequels.md`, *What a jump costs*.

## The terms

**$T_o$, origin time.** The anchor. The fixed event they measure from — the moment of arrival, or the impact itself as a fixed point in the record.

**$T_d$, destination time.** What Benal is trying to compute. Where, or rather *when*, they actually are.

**$\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, the Lorentz factor.** Real physics, and the reason the prop works: any reader who took a year of undergraduate physics recognizes it on sight. It says the colony's time travel is not magic — it descends from twentieth-century relativity, and the original scientists understood that any transit at relativistic parameter carries a dilation term. This is the load-bearing credibility of the whole device.

**$f_{drift}$, the missing function.** Benal's entire arc compressed into one variable.

## The problem

The equation ought to work and does not. The $T_d$ it predicts does not match what they can observe. So a term is missing, and Benal is trying to find it.

**His hypothesis, stated in scene D2.2:** *"They anchored to a point in space, not a point in spacetime. The anchor is — possibly — unmoored."* @D2.2

The founders were brilliant and reasoned as though space held still. It does not. **In 66 million years the solar system travels something like a quarter of an orbit around the galactic center** — one galactic year runs roughly 225 to 250 million years — and the galaxy itself is moving within the Local Group. Across that span, "the same place" is not a statement that means anything. $f_{drift}$ is Benal's attempt at the correction, and the residue he cannot resolve he calls **asymptotic noise**.

*(Note for the science pass: cosmological expansion is the wrong mechanism here and should not be used — gravitationally bound systems don't participate in it, so it does not stretch Earth's orbit, the solar system, or the galaxy. Ordinary galactic motion is both correct and far larger. Benal's insight is unchanged; only the mechanism under it is.)*

## What the drift is actually an error *in*

This is the load-bearing consequence, and it drives the novel's master clock.

Whatever the founders anchored to plainly worked **spatially** — they arrived in the Yucatan, on the right planet, at the right point on its surface. So the residual error cannot be about place.

It is about **when**. $f_{drift}$ is the uncertainty in their own epoch. **The colony does not know precisely when it is.**

Everything follows from that. Nobody can date the impact closer than *about two decades, plus or minus one*, not because the astronomy is hard but because their own position on the timeline is uncertain, and Benal is the only person alive trying to compute it. That is why the Council has a genuine argument rather than a foolish one — you cannot plan a civilization around ±50% — and it is why Benal's "useless" abstraction turns out to be the most important work anyone is doing. The equation is not set dressing. It is the mechanism behind the deadline, the schism, and his vindication, all at once.

**Why the founders' error is good for the story.** It is small, understandable and fatal: they were neither stupid nor villainous, and they missed one term. That is the shape of every other mistake in this book, including the parents', and the rhyme is deliberate.

## What it pays off

The equation is the mechanism behind the revelation at Genesis. The founders' plate carries the *return divisor* (above): travel is not symmetrical, forward and backward are not the same operation, and forward goes only to where the traveler has already been. **Only the original colonists could ever have gone back. Everyone born here cannot.** Benal reaches this by connecting the equation to the temporal flash he dismissed as a nightmare, prompted by an offhand frustrated line from Keo or Teva — *"It's like they just left. Why couldn't they just go back?"*

And the second jump — a thousand years <!-- @WF-secondjump.distance: 1000y --> back, to the same spot — [~] was the heretics' own, and they marked it with the symbol Keo sees in his first temporal flash; where the mark came from is left open (`kb/worldbuilding/the-second-jump.md` §11). *(Not 67 Mya. That is book 3's emigration, a different jump a million years deep; see `plan/sequels.md`.)*

## Why you can only return where you have been — the mechanism

**Travel is asymmetric, and the asymmetry is entanglement.**

**Backward is unrestricted.** You can go, in person, to any time you like. Nothing has to be true of you first.

**Backward is anchored to where you leave from.** Everything earlier than the point you depart from has already happened, and you came out of it, so it is already in your record. The founders lived in a timeline derived from the point they jumped back to; they inherited their entanglement, and jumping back is never a problem. It is *earlier than the departure point*, not *everything the traveler descends from*: a founder at 66 Mya cannot go forward to 1950, which is neither behind them nor lived.

**Forward is not.** Occupying a time entangles you with it, and **you can only travel forward to an interval you are entangled with — one you have physically lived through.** <!-- @WF-return.rule: own-worldline --> That is the whole of FG. Some two hundred and seventy-five people <!-- @WF-founders.count: 275 --> had to make the trip in person because a cure cannot be entangled with anything; only a traveler can.

**And it means the colony's children are not stranded by rule but by biography.** Everyone born here has occupied exactly one interval in the whole of time. There is nowhere forward for them to go, because there is nowhere forward they have been.

**Worked, for the one person it matters most for.** Cecilia's entangled intervals are the twenty-first century, 66 Mya from year 0 to year 22, and the deep past from year −975 to −953. Those three, and nothing else, are where she can travel forward to. Coming home she retraces the deep-past interval to 66 Mya, and then the twenty-first century from there.

**So the anchor is the departure *event*, not the arrival instant.** She boards the leg home from year 22 rather than from the moment she originally landed, because the interval is what she is entangled with and the whole interval is live. **The trilogy's ending rests on this, and it had never been written down.**

The displacement is measured from the **arrival baseline** — the moment the expedition landed — rather than from the traveler's own departure year. That is the reading under which the figures close: the stayers go back a thousand years <!-- @WF-secondjump.distance: 1000y --> to year −1000, Cecilia intends a thousand and travels 975 to year −975, and she is collected at −953.

**None of this is explained on the page in book 1.** Benal reaches *only original colonists could have gone back*, and the mechanism under it is book 2's and book 3's. See `plan/sequels.md`.

## What a change in the past can do — pinned history

**What anyone has lived is fixed. What nobody has lived is one history, and it can still change. An act that would contradict something someone has lived does not change it: it forks the actor off into a world of their own.** <!-- @WF-paradox.model: pinned-history --> *Lived* means observed by anyone, not only by travelers. The name is a coinage for this file; nobody in book 1 has it.

**Neither faction is right, and both are half right.** The Traditionalists are right that the past can be changed: there is one history, not a branch per jump. The Branchers are right that nobody can be harmed by it: a change that would contradict what someone lived forks the changer away instead. So **paradox is impossible**, and that, not "the Branchers are right," is what Benal proves at the end of book 2 (`plan/sequels.md`).

**What it settles.**

- **The capsule.** Buried in rock at year −1000, seen by nobody between then and year 206, it contradicts nothing anyone lived, so it stays on the one shared history and the trio dig it up. Under strict branching nobody would be there to dig it: a branch opened at −1000 has no cause to contain the founders' arrival at year 0, which came from outside.
- **Book 3's emigration.** A civilization built openly for a million years at 67 Mya contradicts what the founders lived at year 0, an empty island, so the emigrants fork into a world of their own. They start over free, and they can never come back to their own Enclave's history.
- **Cecilia saving the twenty-first century.** She carries the cure forward along her own worldline into the 2050 she lived, arriving at or after the moment she left. That contradicts nothing anyone lived, so it is the same world, and the people she left are the people she saves.
- **The return rule is untouched.** Forward still needs lived time (above), so everyone born at 66 Mya is still stranded by biography, and Cecilia goes home from −953 by way of year 22, never through year 206.
- **The founders' fear was unnecessary.** The twenty-first century is pinned by everyone who lived it, so nothing done at 66 Mya could have reached it. The whole apparatus of wrapping and hiding guarded against a danger that could not happen, which completes book 1's *drift, not deceit*.

**None of this is in book 1.** Book 1 shows the Branchers' belief and Benal's faith in it, and the capsule dug up and carried; the realization belongs to book 2 (`plan/sequels.md`, *Benal's road to pinned history*).

## Rules for the prose

1. **Never explain it.** No character delivers a lecture. Benal talks in fragments, to himself, so that the reader assembles the shape from his frustration.
2. **He is stuck for most of the book**, and being stuck is characterizing — it is why Marek can call the work useless and why Benal half believes him.
3. **The fieldpack loss at D14.2 destroys his written work.** After that he carries the equation only in his head, which is what makes the Genesis tablets matter to him personally and not just tactically. @D14.2
4. Use the notation sparingly and as an *image* — something seen on a board, something etched in metal — not as text to be parsed.

## Open issue for the science pass

The Lorentz factor is a relation between proper time and coordinate time for a moving observer. Using it directly to yield a *destination epoch* conflates two different quantities, and a physics-literate reader may catch that.

The recoverable reading, and the one to adopt: **the factor describes the transit itself.** The jump is not instantaneous; it carries a velocity-like parameter, and dilation during transit is then a genuine term rather than a borrowed one. That reading holds together and costs nothing.

Flagged rather than silently fixed, because the notation may want adjusting once the temporal-mechanics layer is planned, and because the claim that this equation rewards a savvy reader is only worth making if it survives one.
