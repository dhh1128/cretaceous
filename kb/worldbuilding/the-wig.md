# The wig

**CANON.** The old-tech vehicle at Genesis that carries them home on Day 19. The action climax, and the largest act of heresy in the novel. Supersedes all references to a "skiff."

---

## 1. The word

**WIG** — wing-in-ground-effect. A 21st-century acronym worn down into an ordinary noun across 206 years, exactly the way *saropo* wore down from *sauropod*. On the closed list in `lingo.md`.

**Nobody in the Enclave has seen one.** They have the word because their ancestors had it, and it survives as a term with no referent — a word for a thing that is not there. When Benal says it at Genesis he is naming something he has only ever read about.

## 2. What it is

Real technology, and lookupable: a **ground-effect vehicle**, IMO **Type 2** — *"certified to temporarily increase its altitude to a limited height outside the influence of ground effect."* That regulatory category exists and it is built around exactly this problem. The nearest modern analog for scale and appearance is the **AirFish 8**.

**Form.** A wide, flat-bottomed hull like a shallow-draft powerboat. Low on either side, **stubby wings — very short, very wide, almost fins**, sitting close to the surface. At the back a **tall tail with the horizontal stabilizer mounted high**, which is not styling: a straight-wing GEV needs its horizontal surface up out of the disturbed air near the water. **The tall tail is the visual signature.**

**Four seats, in an enclosed cabin inside the hull**, low, behind a windscreen a meter or two above the surface. At cruise they are not looking down at the world. They are screaming across it at eye level, and obstacles arrive edge-on.

**Solar.** The dorsal surfaces are photovoltaic skin. This is why anything works at all after 206 years — a fuel-burning machine would be a sculpture. Range on a full charge: **80 km**, which is precisely the distance home and not one kilometer more.

**Two propulsion systems, and the difference between them is the whole climax.**

- **Cruise:** a main propulsor at the back. Efficient in ground effect, roughly a meter up, where the compressed air beneath does most of the lifting for free.
- **Lift rotors:** drone-derived units for vertical thrust. They allow a controlled lift off the bank instead of a long, rough planing run — which a 206-year-old hull would not survive. **They also drain the charge ruinously fast.**

**Ceiling: about fifty feet.** Above most of the canopy. **Not above the conifer emergents**, which stand at thirty meters — twice its limit. So the flight home is not "fly over the forest." It is threading **virtual canyons between the conifers**, hopping the low canopy, and going *around* anything tall.

## 3. The founder history, and why the wigs did not save them

The problem this has to solve: two hundred people walked eighty kilometers, lost a hundred and six, and left aircraft behind. The answer is not that the aircraft were useless. **The aircraft worked, and were used, and could not solve the problem that killed them.**

**Four wigs came through.** Two survive at Genesis today.

**Phase 1 — Arrival and stabilization.** Transit went badly. Equipment damaged, people dead, and a long stretch of going nowhere at all: securing the bank, treating casualties, working out what still functioned. Genesis is defensible against land predators precisely because it is offshore, and it is hopeless for fresh water. That is the trade they lived on while they recovered.

**Phase 2 — Survey, and the wigs did this.** They had drones and they had four wigs, and they used both to look at the coast and inland. **This is how the Enclave site was found.** Aerial survey is exactly what a four-seat 80 km craft is for.

**Phase 3 — The sky turned out to belong to something else.** Drones were taken out of the air. **Two wigs were lost, at least one of them to a flybeak** — a ten-meter wingspan aerial predator against a slow, loud, low-flying machine is not a contest. The founders learned that flying here is survivable rather than safe, and they learned it expensively.

*This is worth having in the records, because it makes the Day 19 chase a repetition of a founder-era disaster rather than a novel threat. Benal or the AI can know that flying kills people here, because it already has.*

**Phase 4 — The decision, and then the walk.** Having found the site, they had to *move to it* — and this is the whole answer. **A four-seat craft cannot relocate a colony.** Two hundred people, the cultures, the equipment, the suits, everything they owned. You could in principle shuttle it in four-seat hops over months, with two degrading airframes, an 80 km range, a solar charge cycle, and predators learning the route — while everyone left behind waits at a site with no fresh water. They walked instead, and it cost them a hundred and six.

**So: the wigs solved the finding problem and could not solve the moving problem.** Survey is not transport.

**Phase 5 — And then the wigs stopped being a resource and became a sin.** They were at Genesis; the colony was now eighty kilometers inland; retrieving them meant repeating the journey that had killed half of them. The remaining airframes had already been stripped for the move. And within a generation the hard-tech taboo hardened, so going back for them shifted from *difficult* to *unthinkable*, and **nobody noticed the transition.** Same drift as everything else in this book.

### The inversion

The trio do not need to move a colony. They need to move **three people and a satchel.**

**The wig is exactly the right size for the thing the founders could not use it for.** And they know precisely where they are going, because they walked it — knowledge only someone who made the journey on foot could possibly have.

**So the walk was not wasted. The walk is what makes the flight possible.** Nobody has to say so.

## 4. The two hulls

They find **two** wigs, and neither flies.

- **The dry one** — hull sound, structure intact, sitting where it was left. Its solar skin is dead and its lift rotors were taken.
- **The wet one** — settled in shallow water off the bank, hull ruined by two centuries of sea. But it went down level, so its **dorsal solar array and its rotor units are above the waterline** and salvageable.

**The job is a swap, not a fabrication**, which is what makes three untrained teenagers believable: take that off that one, put it on this one. No machining, no electronics, no expertise — just instructions, leverage, and hours. Heavy, awkward, in and out of shallow water on a coral bank with marine predators in it. It is a real scene with real jeopardy and it gives all three of them different work.

## 5. The AI, and it should be Keo

Not a general intelligence and not an oracle. **It is the wig's maintenance and flight-training system** — a pilot's assistant, which is plausible 21st-century equipment, knows exactly what the plot needs it to know, and knows nothing else.

**Hard limits, because an AI that knows everything defuses the entire mystery layer:**

- It knows **the machines**. It does not know the mission, the timeline, the schism, or that the upgrades are the cure. **Those stay Benal's discoveries.**
- It cannot repair anything itself, cannot leave, and cannot fly the wig for them.
- After 206 years it is degraded, and it should be **slightly wrong** about some things.

**Keo activates it, and the reason is not convenience.**

Benal is the obvious choice, which is why he is the wrong one — he is in the temporal lab having his own vindication at 12.1, and giving him this as well concentrates everything on one character. **Keo has nothing to do.** That has been his condition since Day 11: useless, carried, waiting. So he wanders off, and finds something to talk to.

And thematically it is his: **Keo is the one who talks to minds.** Proximity sense and projection are his whole gift; he spent the novel bonded to an animal that way and lost her at the midpoint, and he has been alone in his own head ever since. The boy who talks to minds meets a mind that is not alive, and it answers.

It also solves the pilot problem cleanly. **He is the pilot because the training system trains him** — that is literally its function — and because it would much prefer a qualified pilot and does not have one, which is a good character note for a machine.

## 6. Who does what in the air

**Keo flies.** **Teva navigates**, in the seat beside him, reading the line through the conifers — and this is where she gets back what she lost offshore at 9.2, in a form that matters more than it ever did on foot. **Benal is behind them with the salvage**, doing arithmetic on charge, distance and daylight, and saying numbers nobody wants.

Three jobs, three people, one machine. Nobody is a passenger.

## 7. The flight, and what makes it frightening

**It is solar, so it flies by day** — but the deadline is the battery rather than the clock. The range is a full charge and not a meter more, so what makes the flight possible at all is the days it spent parked in the sun at Genesis. They lift at noon with everything it will hold and put eighty kilometers behind them in about two hours. They cannot fly at night, and a wig that runs out short is down in a flooded plain.

**They leave undercharged**, because charging takes hours they do not have and they cannot make themselves wait. Every decision afterward is spent out of margin they chose not to have.

**Ground effect is cheap; altitude is expensive; hovering is ruinous.** Cruise a meter off the flood and the air does the work. Climb and it costs. Use the lift rotors and it costs enormously — so the rotors are **hoarded from the moment they launch**.

**The flood is the runway.** The cyclone put eighty kilometers of shallow standing water across a low-relief plain — see `plan/journey-calendar.md` §2 — and that is precisely the surface this machine wants. **The storm that closed the walking route opened the flying one.** But it is a sheet, not a lake: woodland stands out of it, conifers stand far above it, and saropo herds move through it.

**Obstacles, in order of how they must be handled:**

| obstacle | height | answer |
|---|---|---|
| flooded fern savanna | — | cruise, a meter up, cheap |
| low canopy | ~10 m | hop it, and pay in speed |
| **saropo herd** | 8–10 m and moving | clearable, barely — but a herd is a wall that shifts, and a startled one rears |
| **conifer emergents** | **30 m** | **cannot be cleared. Go around.** This is the canyon system Teva has to read at speed |

**The chase is a resource crisis, not a stunt.** The flybeak forces them to climb; climbing bleeds speed; bleeding speed means sinking; below is water with things in it. Every evasion makes the next one harder, and the charge, the altitude and the airspeed are one number being spent three ways. **Keo's redemption is arithmetic under pressure rather than courage** — he is not braver than he was at the midpoint, he is *thinking*, with a machine, about a problem mental projection cannot touch.

### The last obstacle is the best one

**The border-tangle is fifteen meters high.** The wig's ceiling is about fifty feet — **fifteen meters.** The final barrier of the entire novel is *exactly* as tall as their maximum, and it is a wall their own people built to keep the world out.

They arrive low on charge, in hard afternoon light, and they have been hoarding the lift rotors the whole way for precisely this. **That is what the rotors are for, and it is a Chekhov's gun established at launch and spent at the wall.**

**Guard against anticlimax.** The walk out took eleven days, and the sea two more, and the return takes hours. That is only acceptable if the return is a *different* ordeal rather than a reward — faster, louder, wholly out of their control, and frightening precisely because they cannot stop.

## 7b. The landing, and where the wig lives afterward

**It clears the tangle by almost nothing** — fronds on the hull, everyone inside hearing it — and comes down **on the river**, settling onto its own boat hull and running out to a stop against the near shore. Substantially intact. It will fly again.

**That last part is deliberate and it is the point.** A wrecked wig is a story that ends. An intact, working, **solar-recharging** wig is an object the colony now has to have an argument about, forever — and it is the centerpiece of book 2.

### Why he aims for the river specifically, and why it is the last turn of his arc

On Day 19 there is floodwater everywhere, and almost all of it is easy. **The river channel is the hard target — narrow, fast, and full of the animals his culture is most afraid of.** It is also the only water that will still be there when the flood goes down. Everything else strands the machine on mud within a week.

**The AI tells him this, and he has to decide whether to believe it.** A machine his people call an abomination, telling him to put down in the water his people call death. Choosing to trust it is the heresy completed in a single act, and it is Keo doing arithmetic about three months from now while something is chasing him — which is exactly the competence his arc has been building toward, and the opposite of the boy who took no suit because he wanted to prove a point.

### Where it comes down

**A bend of the river runs through the Enclave** — see `plan/milieu-brief.md` §4. That is why there is a tangle to clear on the way in, and it is why there is water on the far side of it.

The reach inside the perimeter is barred at both ends: log pylons driven between boulders, a droppable pole grating, weirs, netting. **The wig comes over the tangle by almost nothing and settles onto the inside reach**, between the two barriers, running out to a stop against the near bank.

**The reach is one to two kilometers of water**, which is barely enough to launch from and never comfortable. **Every future flight starts as a nail-biter**, and that is correct — an aircraft with an easy margin is an aircraft that stops being frightening.

### The wig sits on the Enclave's own internal frontier

This is the richest consequence and it should never be spelled out.

**It has to live on water**, because that is where it lands and where it launches from. And the river inside the perimeter is not the safe part of the safe place — **it is the least safe part**, the reason for the gratings, the drills, the buddy rule, and Yara. So the most valuable object these people have ever owned sits in the one dangerous place inside their sanctuary. A wound in the middle of the settlement rather than a thing beyond the wall.

- Anyone who uses it goes to the water.
- Anyone who maintains it stands in croc range to do it.
- **It cannot be hidden**, because it charges on sunlight and must sit in the open.
- **It cannot be moved inland**, because inland it is scrap.
- **And flying it means raising the grating** — deliberately opening the croc barrier, with a crew, every single time. Using the wig is not a decision about heresy. It is a decision to let the river in.

So it is visible to every person in the Enclave, permanently, floating on the water they have been forbidden to approach since they could walk. **Every yazhi in the village wants to look at it.** That is a tragedy waiting in book 2 and it needs no help from anyone.

*Water is death*, and the only future they have floats on it. That is the argument book 2 is about, and the object makes it without anyone speaking.

## 8. The heresy

At 4.2 Keo leaves a machete on a shelf because taking a single blade — bought with two lives — is unthinkable.

**On Day 19 he flies home in the largest piece of forbidden technology his people have ever seen**, and sets it down in front of the Council. That is the arc measured in objects, and nobody needs to comment on it.

It also makes the colony unable to pretend. You can argue with what three teenagers *say*. You cannot argue with a machine sitting in the open — which is the object book 2 fights over.
